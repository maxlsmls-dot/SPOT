# Audiobooks — the add-on precedent and the margin mechanism

Why this matters for the short: audiobooks are the one add-on Spotify has actually shipped and monetized, with three years of data, and they are the non-music vertical management credits with roughly two thirds of the gross-margin bridge alongside podcasts. They therefore tell you two things the pitch depends on: what paid attach looks like on this platform when engagement is genuinely high (thesis point 2), and what "non-music gross margin" is actually made of (thesis point 4 and the 2030 target).

## 3. How the economics actually work

**Cost.** Audiobook licensing is consumption-based and sits in Premium cost of revenue. Reported structures: the large publishers are paid a per-title fee once a listener passes roughly 20% of a book (10% in some deals), "as though it were a sale"; smaller publishers and self-published authors are paid from a pro-rata consumption pool whose per-hour rate Spotify sets and does not disclose; à-la-carte purchases pay 50% of list. Spotify described its payments to publishers as "tens of millions" in February 2024 and "hundreds of millions of dollars a year" by October 2024. Every quarterly 6-K since Q2-24 lists "audiobook licensing costs" as a line that Premium revenue growth has to outrun.

**Revenue.** There is almost none that is attributable. Audiobooks+ at ~$100M ARR is ~0.5% of group revenue. The Access tier's uptake has never been disclosed. Audiobooks' "revenue" inside the Premium segment is an internal allocation of the music subscription price.

**The allocation is the point.** Under the US mechanical-royalty rules, revenue from a bundle is apportioned to the music component by the ratio of the standalone prices of the components. Spotify's standalone prices are the Basic plan at $10.99 (music only) and the Access tier at $9.99 (audiobooks only). On those numbers roughly half of the $11.99 Premium price is allocated to audiobooks, which is why the National Music Publishers' Association and the MLC put the publishers' loss at about $150M a year, and why the judge who dismissed the MLC's first complaint still called the structure a "cynical trick." The Access tier exists to set that ratio; the Basic plan exists so the music component has a standalone price. The MLC's amended complaint attacks exactly this: whether the audiobooks-only tier is a genuine standalone product. The court declined to send that question up on appeal in September 2026, so it stays live through the hold period.

**What "audiobooks drove margin" meant.** Q2-24: gross margin "partially offset by audiobooks costs." Q3-24: "Premium gains driven by music and audiobooks." Nothing about audiobook revenue changed between those two quarters. What changed was the bundle reclassification flowing through a full quarter and the June 2024 US hike that was justified by audiobooks. The margin contribution attributed to audiobooks is a royalty-allocation effect plus a price increase, not audiobook monetization. At Investor Day the company said non-music verticals operate above 30% gross margin; that figure only exists because the Premium price is allocated to audiobooks internally.

## 4. What this says about add-ons (thesis point 2)

The audiobook record is the only on-platform evidence for paid attach, and it is the best case for an add-on: a product more than half of eligible subscribers have tried and a quarter use monthly.

| Attach measure | Value |
|---|---|
| Audiobooks+ payers / global subscribers | >1M / 300M ≈ 0.35% |
| / subscribers in markets where audiobooks are live (~55–60% of base) | ≈ 0.6% |
| / monthly audiobook listeners (~25% of subs) | ≈ 1.4% |
| Time from feature launch to $100M ARR | ~33 months (Oct-23 → Jul-26) |
| Time from paid add-on launch in the US to $100M ARR | ~11 months (Aug-25 → Jul-26) |

Two more datapoints from the same playbook. First, when Spotify offered US subscribers a $1 discount to drop audiobooks, 14–17% of individual subscribers took it (Morgan Stanley survey, 2024), so about one in six valued the feature at less than a dollar a month. Second, every expansion since 2024 has been at 12 hours rather than 15, and the US top-up and add-on exist because heavy users hit the cap: the paid product monetizes the top of the usage curve only.

Applied to Music Pro and the AI covers add-on: the platform's demonstrated attach at $11.99 on a product with 25% monthly penetration is under 1% of subscribers after a year. A $5.99 tier would need five to ten times that attach to reach the 3% some bull cases assume, in a product category (hi-res, remixing, ticket access) with lower engagement than audiobooks, and under Streaming 2.0 terms where the labels, unlike the audiobook pool, set the wholesale rate. The "bundle first, charge later" sequence also sets the clock: three years from feature launch to half a percent of revenue.

## 5. What this says about gross margin (thesis point 4 and the 2030 target)

Audiobooks are a cost line growing at 35–60% a year against monetization of ~0.5% of revenue. The company has three governors on the cost: the hour cap, the per-title trigger thresholds in the big-publisher deals, and the pool rate it sets for everyone else. It has one offset, Audiobooks+. And it has one large accounting benefit, the bundle allocation, which is in litigation.

Illustrative cost model (estimates; the company has disclosed only "hundreds of millions a year"):

| | 2025E | 2026E | 2027E |
|---|---|---|---|
| Audiobook licensing cost, €M | 350 | 500 (+40%) | 650 (+30%) |
| As % of group revenue | 2.0% | 2.6% | 3.0% |
| Gross-margin headwind from growth, bp | | ~55 | ~45 |
| Audiobooks+ revenue, €M | 30 | 75 | 130 |
| Offset, bp | | ~15 | ~20 |
| Net audiobook headwind, bp | | ~40 | ~25 |
| Bundle allocation benefit already in the base, €M/yr | ~140 | ~140 | ~140, at risk |

Against a consensus that needs about 130bp of gross-margin expansion in 2027, audiobooks on these numbers are a 25–40bp headwind, and the ~90bp that the bundle allocation contributed to the base since mid-2024 is (a) already lapped, (b) partly given back through the 2025 direct deals with the majors' publishers, and (c) exposed to the MLC case for the independents' share. Sensitivity: if consumption keeps compounding at 50% and the cap is not tightened, the 2027 headwind is closer to 60bp; if Spotify cuts the allowance to 12 hours in the US as it did in every market launched since 2024, the cost growth slows to the subscriber growth rate.

The pricing umbrella is also gone. With Audible at $8.99 and Amazon giving away a book a month inside its music plan, Spotify cannot raise Audiobooks+ or Access pricing, and the Basic plan proves a sixth of US subscribers will trade the feature for a dollar. So the vertical cannot grow into its cost through price; it can only cap consumption, which caps the engagement management cites as the reason it exists.

## 6. The question to put to the transcripts

- Former Spotify audiobooks lead: the per-title fee level and the trigger threshold in the big-five deals; the pool rate per hour; monthly hours per listener versus the cap; what share of listeners hit the cap; Audiobooks+ attach and churn; whether the US allowance will move to 12 hours.
- Publisher digital executives (PRH, HarperCollins, Hachette, Bloomsbury): what Spotify pays per title relative to an Audible credit; whether the deals have minimums; whether the 2026 renewals changed the trigger.
- Music-publishing or MLC-side experts: the allocation ratio in practice, how much of the ~$150M the direct deals with UMPG, Warner Chappell and Sony clawed back, and the realistic range of outcomes in the amended complaint.

## 7. One-line version for the pitch

Audiobooks are the proof that Spotify's add-on playbook produces engagement first and revenue last: three years in, more than a quarter of subscribers use them monthly, fewer than one in a hundred pays for more, the cost line grows 35–60% a year, and the "margin" attributed to the vertical is a royalty allocation that a federal court is still examining.
