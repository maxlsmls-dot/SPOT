<div class="part-divider"><div class="pn">Part VI</div><h1>PMA parts and DER repairs</h1><p>This Part explains the two legal routes by which a company other than the engine's maker may put a new part into a CFM56 (the engine family Part I describes), or repair one in a way the maker's manual does not describe. It covers the regulation, the hard case of hot-section parts, the European acceptance rules, the market that has grown up around approved alternative parts, how airlines, lessors and the manufacturer have responded, and what is filed and what is only reported about FTAI's own joint venture. A reader who finishes it will be able to read a parts-approval announcement, a lease's parts clause and a claimed per-visit saving with every term understood and every number placed against a total.</p></div>

### Translation-table rows answered

This Part answers the rows of the Part III translation table that concern the price and supply of new material from the original equipment manufacturer (OEM) for an engine that is kept flying longer than planned. It answers (Part III §24, row: OEM parts escalation), because an approved alternative part is the only legal way to buy a new part for the CFM56 at a price the manufacturer does not set. It answers (Part III §24, row: hot-section material supply), because the high-pressure turbine blade, the largest non-life-limited cost line in a restoration (Part I §7), received its first approved alternative for the -5B and -7B on 30 October 2025. It answers (Part III §24, row: engines kept past planned retirement), because an engine on its third or fourth shop visit needs new hot-section parts that an engine retired on schedule would never have bought. And it touches (Part III §24, row: lease extensions and return conditions), because whether a leased engine may carry an alternative part at all is decided in the lease. What it does not stress is the life-limited-part line: no approved alternative exists for any CFM56 life-limited part, so that line stays single-source at list price (Part I §4). The orchestrator fills the row identifiers.

## 37. Why PMA exists, and the four approval bases

Every new part that goes into a CFM56 must be made under an approval that a regulator granted to a named company. For most of the engine's life only one company held such an approval for most of its parts: the company that designed it. This section explains why that is so, what it does to prices, and the four ways a second company can obtain an approval of its own.

### 37.1 The gate: who may make a new part

An aircraft engine is certified as a design, and the company that owns the design is the only one that can make parts to it without a further approval. Part I §9 gave the short form of the two certificates involved; this Part owns them and gives the full form.

<div class="defn"><b>Type certificate (TC) and type-certificate holder (TCH)</b><p>A type certificate is the Federal Aviation Administration's (FAA) approval, issued under Title 14 of the Code of Federal Regulations (CFR), of the design of an aircraft, an engine or a propeller, issued after the design has been shown to meet the applicable airworthiness standards. Its holder, the type-certificate holder, is the design's owner and the party responsible for keeping the design airworthy in service. For the CFM56 the holder is CFM International, the 50/50 joint venture of GE Aerospace and Safran Aircraft Engines (Part I §2). The holder's own new spare parts are produced under its production certificate, so for the holder the type certificate is also the gate to the spare-parts business. <span class="cite">[eCFR, 14 CFR Part 21; FAA PMA design-approval page]</span></p></div>

<div class="defn"><b>Production certificate (PC) and production approval</b><p>A production certificate is the FAA's approval of a manufacturer's quality system, under which that manufacturer may produce parts to an approved design and release them as airworthy. The regulations treat the type-certificate holder's production certificate, a Parts Manufacturer Approval and a Technical Standard Order authorization (an approval for standardised articles such as instruments and seat belts) as the three forms of production approval. A replacement or modification part for a certificated product may be installed only if it was produced under one of them, or falls into a narrow exception: standard parts made to a published industry or military specification (the AN/MS hardware of the rules, meaning fasteners and fittings made to published US military AN/MS specifications), parts an owner or operator produces for its own aircraft, and parts a repair station makes in the course of a repair. <span class="cite">[eCFR, 14 CFR Part 21 Subpart K; FAA PMA design-approval page]</span></p></div>

The practical result is a single source. The manufacturer's new spares come off its own production certificate. Anyone else who wants to sell a new part for the engine must first obtain a Parts Manufacturer Approval, which §37.2 defines. Until that approval exists, the manufacturer is the only legal seller of that part number new. <span class="cite">[eCFR, 14 CFR Part 21 Subpart K]</span>

Used material is the other legal source of a part, and it sits outside this debate. Used serviceable material (USM), the parts recovered from retired or dismantled engines and re-released, was made by the manufacturer under its own production certificate, so no further design approval is needed to install it (Part V §31). But it competes for the same slot in a shop visit's parts bill as a new alternative part, and Part V §34 and this Part's §44 should be read together for that reason. <span class="cite">[D4 §2.1; D3 §2.1]</span>

Why does the single source matter enough to have produced a regulation, an industry and an antitrust complaint? Because of what it does to prices. The manufacturer publishes a catalogue list price for every spare part and revises it upward on a schedule; Part IV §28 carries the full treatment and Part I §4.3 the life-limited-part series. The figures the research reached are these. New engine spare-parts prices had been rising around 10% a year in 2022 and 2023, with a double-digit increase on 1 November 2022 and a high-single-digit increase in August 2023, and Safran's chief executive described the rule as pricing "above inflation by 3 to 4 points" for "at least for some years". <span class="cite">[Aviation Week, late 2023]</span> A full CFM56-7B life-limited-part set compounded at about 7% a year from 2008 to 2025 on the trade-press series Part I assembled (derived in Part I §4.3). <span class="cite">[Part I, worked example 1.3]</span> One independent shop reported that -7B parts rose 20–30% in 2023 after the August catalogue revision. <span class="cite">[Magnetic Group via Aviation Week, 2024]</span> Those readings cover different parts and years and the primer carries all of them. <span class="cite">[reconciliation R-018]</span>

The escalation lands on a bill that is mostly material. Material is 60–70% of a shop visit, labour 20–30%, repairs and other shop work 10–20%, according to one trade-press breakdown. <span class="cite">[Air Cargo Week, 2025/26]</span> And the CFM56 aftermarket is mostly transactional: Safran describes about 85% of its CFM56 aftermarket revenue as spare parts and time-and-materials work rather than long-term contracts. <span class="cite">[Safran via Aviation Week, c. 2023/24]</span> So on this engine the manufacturer's main capture is the parts line, whoever runs the shop, and a shop that is not the manufacturer has only three ways to reduce that line: used parts instead of new (Part V), repair instead of replacement (§40), and an approved alternative part instead of the manufacturer's (§37.2 onward). <span class="cite">[D2 §2.2]</span>

### 37.2 What a Parts Manufacturer Approval is

Part I §5 gave a short definition because the high-pressure turbine blade needed one there. This is the full form.

<div class="defn"><b>Parts Manufacturer Approval (PMA)</b><p>A Parts Manufacturer Approval is the FAA's combined design-and-production approval, issued under 14 CFR Part 21 Subpart K, that lets a company other than the type-certificate holder legally make and sell a replacement or modification part for a type-certificated aircraft, engine or propeller. It is a design approval, in that the FAA agrees the part's design meets the airworthiness standards that apply to the product it goes on, and a production approval, in that the FAA agrees the holder's quality system can make every unit to that design. It is issued as a letter with a supplement listing each approved part number and the models it is eligible for; the parts are marked "FAA-PMA" with the maker's name and part number, and each shipment is released on FAA Form 8130-3. There are four approval bases (§37.3). PMA has historically concentrated in consumables and expendables; a cooled, coated turbine blade approved in October 2025 is treated in the trade as the hot-section milestone (§38). No PMA exists for any CFM56 life-limited part. <span class="cite">[eCFR, 14 CFR Part 21 Subpart K and 14 CFR 45.15; FAA Order 8110.42D; FAA PMA design-approval page; D4 U7]</span></p></div>

<div class="defn"><b>14 CFR Part 21 Subpart K</b><p>Part 21 Subpart K is the section of the FAA's certification regulations under which Parts Manufacturer Approvals are issued. Section 21.303 sets the design showing: the applicant must provide "test reports and computations necessary to show that the design of the article meets airworthiness requirements, unless the applicant shows that the design is identical to an article covered under a type certificate", and where the design came by a licensing agreement, evidence of that agreement. Section 21.307 requires a quality system meeting the standard of section 21.137 (the same standard a production-certificate holder meets), and section 21.316 lists the holder's continuing obligations once approved. <span class="cite">[eCFR, 14 CFR Part 21 Subpart K]</span></p></div>

Two FAA offices divide the work. The design side is handled by an Aircraft Certification Office (ACO), or by engineers the FAA has designated to act for it (§40 describes the designee system). The production side is handled by a Manufacturing Inspection District Office (MIDO), which inspects the applicant's quality system and the conformity of the test articles. Both must be satisfied before the supplement is issued. <span class="cite">[FAA Order 8110.42D; FAA PMA design-approval page]</span> The release tag that accompanies each shipment is FAA Form 8130-3, which Part I §9 defined: for a new part it certifies that the part was produced under an FAA production approval and conforms to its approved design. A part not produced under a production approval cannot carry one. <span class="cite">[Aviation Suppliers Association, 2016, via Part I §9]</span>

### 37.3 The four approval bases

Section 21.303 draws one line: either the applicant proves the design by its own tests and calculations, or it shows the design is identical to the manufacturer's. FAA guidance, in Advisory Circulars (AC) 21.303-3 and 21.303-4 and Order 8110.42D, turns that line into four approval bases, which differ in what the applicant has to possess and what it has to prove. <span class="cite">[FAA AC 21.303-3; FAA AC 21.303-4; FAA Order 8110.42D]</span>

<div class="defn"><b>Test and computation (T&C)</b><p>Test and computation is the PMA basis an applicant uses when it has none of the manufacturer's design data. It reverse-engineers the part: it measures examples of the manufacturer's part, determines the material and any coatings, and writes its own drawings and process specifications. It then proves by its own analysis and testing that the part meets the airworthiness standards that apply to the product (for an engine part, 14 CFR Part 33) and does not degrade the engine's own compliance. It is the only basis on which a company can compete with the manufacturer on a part the manufacturer has not licensed, and Chromalloy's CFM56 high-pressure turbine blade is a test-and-computation part. <span class="cite">[FAA AC 21.303-3; FAA AC 33-8; D4 §2.2]</span></p></div>

<div class="defn"><b>Identicality (without a licensing agreement; by licensing agreement)</b><p>Identicality is the PMA basis in which the applicant shows that its design is identical to the type-certificate holder's in every respect that matters. Without a licence, the applicant usually holds the manufacturer's drawings legitimately, for example as a former supplier; FAA Notice 8110.55 of 1996 set the policy. With a licence, the type- or supplemental-type-certificate holder has licensed its design data and the licence itself is the evidence; this is how a manufacturer's subcontractor, or a company the manufacturer has chosen to work with, obtains a PMA. For a critical or life-limited part approved by identicality the FAA additionally requires evidence that the manufacturing, inspection and test processes are identical, not only the geometry (§38). <span class="cite">[FAA Notice N 8110.55, 1996; FAA Order 8110.42D; FAA AC 21.303-4]</span></p></div>

<div class="defn"><b>Supplemental Type Certificate (STC)</b><p>A Supplemental Type Certificate is the FAA's approval of a change to a type-certificated design. As a PMA basis, the part is the subject of its own design approval, the STC, and the PMA becomes the production approval for that STC's parts. The STC route matters for international acceptance: under the bilateral agreement between the FAA and the European Union Aviation Safety Agency (EASA), a critical part approved by test and computation is accepted in Europe only through an STC that EASA has validated (§39). The same instrument is the approved modification design a conversion house owns for a passenger-to-freighter conversion, which Part II §15 glossed. <span class="cite">[FAA AC 21.303-4; FAA–EASA Technical Implementation Procedures (TIP) Rev 7; D4 §2.2, §2.5]</span></p></div>

Read across the rows and the pattern is that the harder the data are to obtain, the more the applicant must prove. A licensee proves almost nothing beyond the licence. A former supplier proves identicality. An independent with no data proves airworthiness from scratch. That is why the independent PMA industry grew up in parts whose airworthiness is easy to prove, and why §38 is a separate section. <span class="cite">[D4 §2.2–2.3]</span>

<div class="box"><div class="h">Why the fourth basis matters abroad</div><p>Inside the United States a PMA part approved by any of the four bases is installable on any eligible engine. The bases matter abroad. The European acceptance rules (§39) accept an FAA PMA directly only in defined cases, and for a part the manufacturer designated as critical the only direct route is a Supplemental Type Certificate that the European agency has itself validated. So an applicant's choice of basis is partly a choice of addressable fleet: a test-and-computation approval alone may serve US-registered engines, while serving EU-registered engines may require the fourth route as well. Whether that applies to the CFM56 turbine blade and vane is one of this Part's open questions. <span class="cite">[FAA–EASA TIP Rev 7; D4 §2.5, U5]</span></p></div>

### 37.4 The production side

An approved design is half of a PMA. The other half is an approved factory. The applicant's quality system must meet 14 CFR 21.137, the same standard applied to the type-certificate holder's own production certificate. Before issuing the supplement the FAA checks the conformity of the test articles to the drawings, reviews the test and analysis reports, and inspects the quality system. After issue, section 21.316 obliges the holder to keep the system in place, to report failures and defects, and to allow FAA inspection. <span class="cite">[eCFR, 14 CFR 21.307 and 21.316; FAA Order 8110.42D]</span> Every unit then leaves on an 8130-3 that references the PMA. The approval is therefore to a company and a part number, not to a design in the abstract: a second company wanting to make the same part must obtain its own PMA, and a PMA holder that changes its process must show the change does not alter conformity. <span class="cite">[FAA Order 8110.42D; D4 §2.2]</span>

## 38. Critical parts and the hot-section problem

Alternative parts were for decades a business in seals, bushings, fasteners, filters, brackets and non-rotating structure. The parts that cost the most in a CFM56 shop visit are not those. This section explains the two classifications that govern what a maker must prove, why a turbine blade sits at the hard end of both, and what the research found about how long the hard case takes.

### 38.1 Two definitions that are easy to confuse

Two different categories are in play, and they come from two different documents.

The first is the life-limited part, which Part I §4 defined: a rotating or pressure-bearing part whose failure the engine case cannot contain, certified for a fixed number of flight cycles and scrapped at that limit regardless of condition. On the -7B the limits are 20,000 cycles in the high-pressure compressor and turbine, 25,000 in the low-pressure turbine and 30,000 in the fan and booster. <span class="cite">[Part I §4.1; AJW (an engine trader) and StandardAero records mini-packs]</span> The list of such parts, and each one's limit, sits in a specific section of the engine's certification data.

<div class="defn"><b>Airworthiness Limitations Section (ALS) and Instructions for Continued Airworthiness (ICA)</b><p>The Instructions for Continued Airworthiness are the manufacturer's approved maintenance instructions for a type-certificated product, attached to the type certificate: the Engine Shop Manual and the component maintenance manuals (Part I §9) are part of them. The Airworthiness Limitations Section is the part of those instructions that lists the mandatory life limits and inspection intervals, which no operator may exceed; every life-limited part's cycle limit appears there. The two matter in this Part because a part's appearance in the Airworthiness Limitations Section is the usual trigger for the "critical" designation below, and because a repair that departs from the Instructions for Continued Airworthiness needs separate approval (§40). These definitions are drawn from first principles and from the regulatory structure the dossier cites; the research did not quote the regulation's own text. <span class="cite">[D4 §2.3, §2.6; registry, Part VI row]</span></p></div>

The second category comes from the agreement between the FAA and its European counterpart, and it is the one that decides how an approval travels.

<div class="defn"><b>Critical component (critical part)</b><p>Under the FAA–EASA bilateral's Technical Implementation Procedures, a critical component is "a part identified as critical by the design approval holder during the product certification process or otherwise by the Authority for the State of Design. Typically, such components include parts for which a replacement time, inspection interval, or related procedure is specified in the Airworthiness Limitations section or certification maintenance requirements." Every life-limited part is therefore a critical component. Whether a given turbine blade or vane is one depends on how the type-certificate holder designated it during certification, and that designation is not public in anything the research retrieved. The classification matters because it selects which European acceptance clause applies (§39) and because, for an identicality approval, it adds the requirement that processes and inspections be identical, not only geometry. <span class="cite">[FAA–EASA TIP Rev 7, as returned by search; FAA Order 8110.42D; D4 §2.3, U5]</span></p></div>

The third row is where the money in a restoration sits. The high-pressure turbine blades and vanes are not life-limited; they are replaced when inspection finds them beyond the manual's repair limits, and that happens at the hot end of the engine faster than anywhere else. Part I §5 explained that most of the lost temperature margin is recovered in that module, and one consultancy puts the blades and vanes at 15–25% of a shop visit's parts cost. <span class="cite">[Safe Fly Aviation, 2026, consultancy blog; Part I §5]</span> So the on-condition hot-section row, not the life-limited row, is where an approved alternative changes a bill.

### 38.2 Why a turbine blade is the hard case

Two regulatory facts make the hot section hard for an applicant with no manufacturer data.

The first is the showing itself. For a test-and-computation approval of a turbine engine part the FAA's product-specific guidance is Advisory Circular (AC) 33-8. Under it the applicant must substantiate the part's material properties, its cooling and thermal behaviour, its durability, and its effect on the engine's safety analysis by its own testing, because it has no access to the manufacturer's data on any of them. <span class="cite">[FAA AC 33-8; D4 §2.3]</span> A high-pressure turbine blade is a cooled casting, grown as a single crystal and coated, sitting at the hottest point in the engine. This primer names those three properties and stops, since the depth that matters here is what they do to the approval: each is a property the applicant must measure, reproduce and prove, with no reference data from the designer.

The second is the identicality rule for critical and life-limited parts. If the basis is identicality, the FAA's procedures require the applicant to show that the manufacturing processes (casting, forging, heat treatment, coating) and the inspection and test procedures produce a part identical to the original, not merely that the drawing matches. <span class="cite">[FAA Order 8110.42D, as cited in D4 §2.3]</span> For a part whose life depends on its microstructure, that is a showing few companies outside the manufacturer's own supply chain can make.

<div class="box"><div class="h">Why a blade is harder than a bracket</div><p>A bracket's airworthiness is a matter of geometry, material and static strength, all of which an applicant can measure from an example and prove by a calculation and a pull test. A cooled turbine blade's airworthiness is a matter of how a particular alloy, grown as a single crystal and coated, behaves over thousands of hours at the hottest point in the engine, under centrifugal load, with cooling air flowing through passages whose shape the applicant must infer. The regulation does not forbid an independent from proving that; AC 33-8 tells it how. But the proof is an engine-level test and durability programme the applicant funds itself, and the trade treats the first such approval for the CFM56-5B/7B, announced on 30 October 2025, as a milestone for exactly that reason: the applicant called its blade "the only aftermarket alternative to the OEM manufactured new part" for a platform with "over 20,000 engines" in service. <span class="cite">[FAA AC 33-8; Chromalloy release, 30 Oct 2025; D4 §2.3]</span></p></div>

Those two facts explain the historical shape of the industry. Approved alternatives concentrated in consumables and expendables, meaning the seals, bushings, fasteners, filters, brackets and non-rotating structural pieces whose airworthiness can be proven with modest test programmes. <span class="cite">[D4 §2.3]</span> They did not reach life-limited rotating parts at all: the research found no PMA for any CFM56 disk, spool or shaft, and that line of the parts bill remains single-source. <span class="cite">[D4 U7; Part I §4.4]</span> And they reached the cooled hot-section airfoils only recently, and only after a programme whose length §38.3 sets out as far as the sources allow.

### 38.3 How long a PMA takes

Neither the FAA nor the companies disclose the interval from application to approval in anything the research retrieved, so this is an open question (D4 U4). Two datapoints bracket it. At the simple end, the annual report of HEICO (the largest independent PMA maker, §41.1) says the company has been adding new approvals at a rate of "approximately 300 to 500" a year by one dossier's reading of the filing, or "approximately 400 to 550" by another's, across a portfolio dominated by less complex parts; simple approvals are evidently a matter of months, not years. <span class="cite">[HEICO 10-K FY2025, two readings; reconciliation R-024]</span> At the hard end, third-party commentary dates the FTAI–Chromalloy partnership to 2018 and the FAA approval of the CFM56 turbine blade came on 30 October 2025, about seven years later; the application date is not public, and the 2018 date is itself an investor-blog figure that §43 sets beside what is filed. <span class="cite">[Komodo Capital and Kairos Research, investor blogs, undated; Chromalloy release, 30 Oct 2025; D4 §2.4]</span> The FAA's own registry of approvals would carry the dates, and the research did not read it. <span class="cite">[D4 U4]</span>

## 39. EASA and the bilateral

An FAA approval is a US approval. For engines registered in the European Union, acceptance of US approvals is governed by a treaty and the procedures written under it. This section sets out those procedures as the research found them and names the question they leave open for the CFM56 hot-section parts.

<div class="defn"><b>Technical Implementation Procedures (TIP)</b><p>The Technical Implementation Procedures are the document by which the FAA and the European Union Aviation Safety Agency (EASA) implement the US–EU Bilateral Aviation Safety Agreement for airworthiness and environmental certification; the current edition is Revision 7 with Amendment 1. Among much else, they set the rules under which EASA accepts FAA Parts Manufacturer Approvals for installation on EASA-certificated or EASA-validated products. As returned by search, EASA accepts an FAA PMA directly, without further showing, when the part is not a critical component and was approved by test and computation or by identicality without a licence; or when it conforms to design data licensed from a type- or supplemental-type-certificate holder whose certificate EASA has validated; or when it is a critical component approved through an FAA supplemental type certificate that EASA has validated. <span class="cite">[FAA–EASA TIP Rev 7 with Amendment 1; TIP Rev 6 (PDF); D4 §2.5]</span></p></div>

A caution on the wording. The dossier recorded that a search summary rendered the first clause as "identicality without a licensing agreement" and advised that the exact text of Revision 7 be checked before it is quoted. The research did not read the document itself, so this Part reports the three clauses as the dossier recorded them and does not quote them. <span class="cite">[D4 §2.5]</span>

The practical consequence is that a non-critical test-and-computation part travels to Europe on its FAA release tag, while a critical test-and-computation part needs the supplemental-type-certificate route plus EASA's validation of that certificate. <span class="cite">[D4 §2.5]</span> Part I §9 defined EASA Form 1 as the European release certificate, the equivalent of the 8130-3, issued by a holder of a European production organisation approval; a US-made PMA part moving into the EU system under the clauses above does so on its 8130-3, not on a Form 1, since its maker holds an FAA approval rather than a European one. <span class="cite">[FAA EASA FAQ; Part I §9]</span>

The question the research could not close is the one that decides the addressable fleet. Whether CFM International designated the CFM56-5B/7B high-pressure turbine blade and stage-1 vane as critical components during certification is not public in the sources retrieved; whether Chromalloy has obtained EASA acceptance for them by any clause was likewise not found. <span class="cite">[D4 U5]</span> If the parts are not critical, clause 1 applies and the FAA approval of October 2025 serves EU-registered engines directly. If they are critical, clause 3 applies and a validated supplemental type certificate is required, which the research did not find. This Part presents both branches and does not choose between them. Acceptance by other authorities, including the UK Civil Aviation Authority (CAA), Transport Canada Civil Aviation (TCCA), Brazil's ANAC (its national civil aviation agency), the Civil Aviation Administration of China (CAAC) and India's Directorate General of Civil Aviation (DGCA), runs under separate bilaterals and national rules, and none was retrieved. <span class="cite">[D4 U6]</span>

## 40. DER repairs

A new part, whoever makes it, is one way to restore an engine. Repairing the part already in it is the other, and the manufacturer's manual does not describe every repair that is possible. This section explains who may approve a repair the manual does not cover, on what document, how such a repair differs from one taken from the manual, who develops and uses them, and how they save money. Part I §9 gave the short form of the designee and the repair; this Part owns both.

### 40.1 The designee system

The FAA does not have enough engineers to examine every set of engineering data itself. It appoints private engineers to do so on its behalf within defined limits.

<div class="defn"><b>Designated Engineering Representative (DER)</b><p>A Designated Engineering Representative is an individual engineer appointed by the FAA under 14 CFR Part 183 to examine engineering data and make findings of compliance on the FAA's behalf within a delegated technical area, such as structures, powerplant, engines or systems. A company DER is employed by a manufacturer or repair station and may act only on that company's data; a consultant DER may act for any client. To approve the data for a major repair the designee needs a specific authorisation, "Special – Major Repairs", from the managing Aircraft Certification Office, granted one-time or as part of the designee's standing delegation. The governing document is FAA Order 8110.37, the DER Handbook, currently revision F. <span class="cite">[FAA Order 8110.37F; D4 §2.6]</span></p></div>

### 40.2 Manual repairs, major repairs and the form that approves them

The manufacturer publishes its approved repairs in the Engine Shop Manual and the component maintenance manuals, which Part I §9 defined and which form part of the Instructions for Continued Airworthiness attached to the type certificate (§38.1). A shop that follows the manual needs no further approval: the manual is already FAA-approved data. <span class="cite">[D4 §2.6]</span> The question arises when a shop wants to do something the manual does not describe.

<div class="defn"><b>Major repair</b><p>A major repair is a repair the manufacturer's manual does not cover, or a repair of a part the manual says must be scrapped, that does not qualify as minor. Major repairs "must be accomplished in accordance with technical data approved by the Administrator", meaning the FAA or someone acting under its delegation. The approval covers the data: the repair scheme, the substantiating analysis, the process specifications and any test. The repair itself is then performed by a certificated repair station under its own certificate. <span class="cite">[FAA Order 8110.37F; FAA Order 8300.16; D4 §2.6]</span></p></div>

<div class="defn"><b>FAA Form 8110-3 (Statement of Compliance with Airworthiness Standards)</b><p>Form 8110-3 is the document on which a Designated Engineering Representative records an approval, or a recommendation for approval, of engineering data, including the data for a repair or alteration. The designee keeps the original and sends copies to the FAA's managing office and to the owner, operator or repair station that requested the approval. The form approves data, not a part: the repair station that performs the repair releases the repaired part on FAA Form 8130-3 (Part I §9), and that release references the 8110-3 under which the repair data were approved. The FAA notes that designee-approved data "may not be adequate to cover every aspect of the repair", which is why the repair station's own certificate and release remain necessary. <span class="cite">[FAA Order 8110.37F; FAA, Documenting Compliance Findings (Form 8110-3 guidance); D4 §2.6]</span></p></div>

<div class="defn"><b>DER repair</b><p>A DER repair is a major repair whose data a Designated Engineering Representative approved on FAA Form 8110-3, rather than being taken from the manufacturer's shop manual. The approved data may be one-time, covering a specific serial-numbered part, or multiple-use, repeatable on any eligible part; a multiple-use approval is what turns a repair into a product a shop can sell. The part remains the manufacturer's part; nothing is remanufactured. A DER repair saves money by restoring a part the manual would scrap, so that no new part of any origin is bought, or by restoring it more cheaply than the manual's repair. <span class="cite">[FAA Order 8110.37F; D4 §2.6–2.7; D2 §2.2]</span></p></div>

### 40.3 Who develops and uses them, and how they save

Three kinds of organisation develop DER repairs. Independent turbine-part repair houses are the first: Chromalloy's historic core business is turbine-part repair and coating, and the trade press describes it as positioned in the "PMA and designated engineering repair sector". <span class="cite">[FlightGlobal, Chromalloy profile, undated; D4 §2.6]</span> Airlines with in-house engineering are the second: Southwest Airlines was reported as evaluating PMA and DER options for its CFM56-7B fleet as it phases out its 737-700s. <span class="cite">[Aviation Week, c. 2023]</span> Independent MRO shops are the third. <span class="cite">[D4 §2.6]</span>

The saving is on the repair line, and the size of that line is the first thing to establish. Repairs and other shop activities are 10–20% of a shop visit in the trade-press breakdown Part I carried; material is 60–70%. <span class="cite">[Air Cargo Week, 2025/26]</span> A repair that keeps a part in service avoids a purchase on the material line, so its saving is the new part's price less the repair's cost, and the research did not reach a repair price for any CFM56 hot-section part. What it did reach is the cadence that decides whether a repair is available at all. According to an expert interview, at a first shop visit, around 20,000 cycles, airlines typically repair the high-pressure turbine blades rather than replace them; at a second, around 30,000 cycles, the hot-section parts typically cannot be repaired again and a new part is needed. <span class="cite">[In Practise, undated, interview digest]</span> So a repair, manual or DER, is the lever at the first visit, and a new part, manufacturer's or PMA, is the lever at the second. The manufacturer has been working the repair lever too: CFM says it has industrialised about 400 CFM56 repairs since early 2023, meaning it has developed them into repeatable production processes, including automated laser welding of high-pressure turbine blades that cuts repair turnaround by about a third. <span class="cite">[CFM International, "CFM boosts HPT blade output and repair", 2025/26, date not captured]</span>

Penetration by category of part is better described in words than in numbers, because no source gave a split. ## 42. Acceptance: airlines, lessors, and the OEM's responses

A PMA part is legal to install the day it is approved. Whether it is installed depends on three other parties: the airline that owns or operates the engine, the lessor whose lease may forbid it, and the manufacturer whose warranty, service and residual-value programmes may discourage it. This section presents each party's position as the sources state it, with dates, and does not adjudicate between them.

One episode in 2023 should be named here so that it is not confused with the subject of this Part. ### 42.2 Lessors

An operating lease specifies the state in which the aircraft or engine must be returned, and those return conditions, which Part II §13 treats in full and Part I §9 glossed, are where a lessor's view of alternative parts is written down. One lease-management primer calls return conditions "the most important part of any lease", since they, not the rent, protect the lessor's next placement. <span class="cite">[Sofema Online, undated]</span>

<div class="defn"><b>OEM-only clause (no-PMA / no-DER clause; TCH-configuration clause)</b><p>An OEM-only clause is a lease return condition requiring that at redelivery the engine contain only parts produced by the type-certificate holder and only repairs from the type-certificate holder's manuals. A lessee that used PMA parts or DER repairs during the term must then either remove them at the last heavy check before return or pay compensation. The sources describe both the clause and the workaround: "when permitted by the lease, lessees can ... use PMA throughout the term of the lease and ... remove PMA parts during the heavy check prior to the return", while for engines "returning a 'PMA-heavy' engine to strict TCH configuration carries significant financial burdens". A lease that requires TRUEngine status (Part I §3) is an OEM-only clause by another name. Engine lessors were reported to forbid PMA and DER "in the vast majority of cases" in 2016; aircraft-lease clauses were reported to be loosening in 2015; practice in 2024–2026 is unknown. <span class="cite">[Aviation Suppliers Association blog, undated; Engine Lease Finance Corporation (ELFC), mid-2016; MARPA, 10 Nov 2015; D4 §2.9, U1]</span></p></div>

### 42.3 The manufacturer's responses

CFM International's recorded responses fall into three kinds: commitments it made in 2018 about warranty and service, a configuration designation it markets to lessors and appraisers, and commercial responses in supply, repair and agreements. Each is presented as CFM states it.

<div class="defn"><b>Conduct Policies (CFM–IATA, 2018)</b><p>The Conduct Policies are the commitments CFM International published in 2018 to settle a complaint the International Air Transport Association (IATA) had brought to the European Commission about CFM's aftermarket conduct. Under them, installing non-CFM parts or repairs "does not in itself render the warranty void"; CFM honours its warranty on its own parts and repairs in an engine that contains non-CFM parts; and CFM "does not refuse to service engines because they contain non-OEM parts or repairs". The policies also cover CFM's licensing of its shop manual to third-party shops and its sale of CFM parts to them; the research did not re-read those clauses. Trade associations summarised the agreement as CFM agreeing "to permit the use of PMA parts and DER repairs". <span class="cite">[IATA, "IATA–CFM Agreement on Engine Maintenance", 2018; IATA, "CFM Conduct Policies and Implementation Measures"; CFM, "External Guidance", Mar 2019; MARPA, 1 Aug 2018; Aviation Suppliers Association bulletin, Oct 2018]</span></p></div>

The second response is the designation Part I §3 defined. TRUEngine designates an engine as maintained to CFM-approved configuration, meaning CFM parts and CFM-approved repairs; CFM markets it as supporting residual value and resale, extended it in 2012 to cover subsequent buyers, and lessors can require it in return conditions. <span class="cite">[GE Aerospace, TrueEngine presentation; CFM release on the TRUEngine expansion; Aviation International News, 12 Sept 2012]</span> The 2018 policies and the designation are both CFM positions and address different levers: one is a contractual commitment about warranty and service, the other a marketing statement about resale value. The primer records both without treating either as the "real" position. <span class="cite">[D4 T8]</span>

Chromalloy's announcement of 30 October 2025 said that orders covered "all planned production for the rest of 2025" and that "many customers [were] now reserving manufacturing slots well into 2026", without naming any customer. <span class="cite">[Chromalloy release, 30 Oct 2025; Newswire copy; AviTrader, 31 Oct 2025]</span> In this primer's vocabulary that is a production slot, a reserved place in a parts maker's output, which is distinct from the induction slot of an engine shop (Part IV §27) and the manufacturing-slot reservation of a turbine maker (Part IX). <span class="cite">[registry C-18]</span> Two things the statement does not give are the ones that would size it: Chromalloy's production capacity in blade sets a year, and FTAI's share of that capacity. <span class="cite">[D4 U12]</span> The limiting process is single-crystal casting and coating, which §38.2 named; no capacity figure was disclosed. <span class="cite">[D4 §5]</span> Customer uptake beyond "sold out" was not found: no named airline or shop, and no count of sets delivered. <span class="cite">[D4 U15]</span> If the arrangement is exclusive for the five parts, as commentary alone says, then other shops' access to those parts, and at what price, is a constraint on market-wide penetration that the sources do not describe. <span class="cite">[D4 §5, U3]</span>

### 43.4 What FTAI's management has said

The company's own quantified statements about the venture are few, and they are set beside the commentary's figures so that the gap is visible.

On the Q4 2025 earnings call of 26 February 2026, asked how the Aerospace Products segment's margin would move from the mid-30s toward 40%, chief executive Joe Adams "cited approval of a PMA high-pressure turbine blade as a key factor, along with expanded access to lower-cost parts supply, including used serviceable material and a materials agreement with CFM that includes parts and repairs"; the company said it "can approach 40% aerospace margins in 2026 via parts approvals, lower-cost supply and expanded repair capabilities". <span class="cite">[MarketBeat call highlights, 26 Feb 2026; D5 §2.7]</span> The segment's Q4 2025 Adjusted EBITDA was $195M at a 35% margin, as call coverage reported it, and FY2025 segment Adjusted EBITDA was $671.3M on segment revenue of $1,936.2M, a derived 34.7%. <span class="cite">[MarketBeat call highlights, 26 Feb 2026; FTAI 10-K FY2025 via orchestrator notes; D5 §4.1]</span> No dollar figure for the PMA saving per shop visit or per module, no PMA share of parts consumed, and no equity-method earnings line for the venture were retrieved. <span class="cite">[D5 U11]</span> The one FTAI-quantified savings lever found anywhere in the research is the Bristol accessories venture, "expected to deliver up to $75,000 in average savings per shop visit", said on the Q3 2025 call. <span class="cite">[Insider Monkey, FTAI Q3 2025 call transcript, Oct 2025; D5 §2.6]</span>

Against that, investor blogs state "up to $2 million per shop visit" from five parts supplied at cost, and "5–10 percentage points of additional margin". <span class="cite">[Komodo Capital; Kairos Research; Yahoo Finance digest; reconciliation R-027]</span> The reconciliation records this as a confidence mismatch: a blog dollar figure against a qualitative management statement. <span class="cite">[reconciliation R-027]</span> §44 tests the dollar figure against the parts arithmetic; the worked example here only places the two claims against the segment's reported scale.

## 44. Worked example: the parts bill with and without PMA

This section does what the Part has been building toward: it puts a CFM56-7B performance restoration's hot-section parts bill on the page, prices it with the manufacturer's new parts and then with alternative parts, shows the saving as a function of a discount no source discloses, and tests the claimed "$2 million per shop visit" against the arithmetic. Every tension that touches the numbers is presented in full, and nothing is resolved.

### 44.1 The setting and the inputs

The visit is a performance restoration, in which the core is opened and the high-pressure compressor, combustor and high-pressure turbine are restored to recover exhaust-gas temperature (EGT) margin (Part I §6). The cadence from §40.3 decides whether new blades are bought at all: at a first visit, around 20,000 cycles, blades are typically repaired; at a second, around 30,000, they typically cannot be and new ones are needed. <span class="cite">[In Practise, undated, interview digest]</span> The example is therefore a second or later visit, which under Part III's context is exactly the visit an engine kept past its planned retirement undergoes. No shop-level price for a blade or a set is used, because the one fixed-price CFM56-7B catalogue the research located, that of the Zurich engine shop SR Technics (SR), dated 3 June 2026, was not read. <span class="cite">[SR Technics catalogue, 3 Jun 2026, located not read; D4 U2]</span> Alternative parts touch only the on-condition hot-section parts of §38.1; they touch no life-limited part, so the life-limited-part line, 40–60% of parts cost when replaced, is unchanged in every case below. <span class="cite">[Safe Fly Aviation, 2026, consultancy blog; D4 U7]</span>

### 44.2 The bill with the manufacturer's new parts, and the blade-price tension

<div class="worked"><div class="h">Worked example 6.6 — A CFM56-7B performance restoration's HPT parts bill: manufacturer's new parts, then PMA parts at an undisclosed discount</div>
<p>Step 1, the manufacturer's blade set, priced per blade. Inputs: 80 blades (unverified parameter) at $20,000 (sourced: interview digest) or at about $38,500 (sourced: weak web listing, condition unclear, dossier's conversion of €35,000).</p>
<div class="eqblock">at the interview price:  80 × 20,000 = 1,600,000
at the listing price:    80 × 38,500 = 3,080,000</div>
<p>Step 2, the same blades and vanes priced from the consultancy shares instead. Inputs: HPT blades and vanes 15–25% of parts cost (sourced: Safe Fly Aviation); material 60–70% of a visit (sourced: Air Cargo Week); visit $1.2–1.6M or $1.8–2.5M (sourced: Safe Fly Aviation, two pages). Part I §7.2 applied the share to the visit total as an approximation; the stricter reading applies it to the material line.</p>
<div class="eqblock">share of the visit total (Part I's approximation):
   0.15 × 1,200,000 = 180,000;  0.25 × 1,600,000 = 400,000
   0.15 × 1,800,000 = 270,000;  0.25 × 2,500,000 = 625,000
share of the material line (material 60–70% of the visit):
   0.15 × 0.60 × 1,200,000 = 108,000;  0.25 × 0.70 × 1,600,000 = 280,000
   0.15 × 0.60 × 1,800,000 = 162,000;  0.25 × 0.70 × 2,500,000 = 437,500</div>
<p>This is the tension in full. Priced per blade, the blade set alone is $1.6M to $3.1M, which is 100% to 260% of a $1.2–1.6M restoration and 64% to 171% of a $1.8–2.5M one. Priced as a share, the blades and vanes together are $0.11M to $0.63M. The two routes cannot both describe the same visit at the same prices. The research recorded the possible reasons without establishing any: the 80-blade count is a recollection; the per-blade prices come from an interview and a listing of unclear condition rather than a catalogue; and a share figure drawn from visits at which blades are repaired rather than replaced would be far smaller than a visit at which the whole set is bought new. The primer carries both routes and stops (R-015). <span class="cite">[D4 §2.8, T5, U16; Safe Fly Aviation, 2026; Air Cargo Week, 2025/26; Part I §7.2; reconciliation R-015]</span></p>
<p>Step 3, the PMA blade set at a discount d to the manufacturer's price, on the $1.6M set. The discount is undisclosed (D4 U2); the three rates are illustrative assumptions.</p>

| Assumed PMA discount d | PMA set cost ($1.6M base) | Saving on the blade set | PMA set cost ($3.08M base) | Saving on the blade set |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 20% | $1,280,000 | $320,000 | $2,464,000 | $616,000 |
| 30% | $1,120,000 | $480,000 | $2,156,000 | $924,000 |
| 40% | $960,000 | $640,000 | $1,848,000 | $1,232,000 |

<p>Step 4, adding the stage-1 vane set. If blades and vanes together are "up to a few million dollars" (sourced phrase), taken as $2.5–3.0M (assumed within the phrase), the same discounts give savings on the two part families together of:</p>
<div class="eqblock">at 20%: 0.20 × 2,500,000 = 500,000  to  0.20 × 3,000,000 = 600,000
at 30%: 0.30 × 2,500,000 = 750,000  to  0.30 × 3,000,000 = 900,000
at 40%: 0.40 × 2,500,000 = 1,000,000  to  0.40 × 3,000,000 = 1,200,000</div>
<p>Step 5, the saving as a share of the visit, divided by both performance-restoration ranges as reconciliation rule E.1.4 requires.</p>
<div class="eqblock">blade set only, $1.6M base, d = 20–40%: saving 320,000–640,000
   ÷ visit of 1,200,000–1,600,000 = 20% to 53%
   ÷ visit of 1,800,000–2,500,000 = 13% to 36%
blades and vanes, $2.5–3.0M, d = 20–40%: saving 500,000–1,200,000
   ÷ visit of 1,200,000–1,600,000 = 31% to 100%
   ÷ visit of 1,800,000–2,500,000 = 20% to 67%</div>
<p>So on the per-blade inputs, alternative hot-section parts at a 20–40% discount would save 13% to 53% of a restoration's cost on the blades alone and 20% to 100% on blades and vanes together, depending on which visit range is used. A saving approaching or exceeding the whole visit is itself a sign that the per-blade inputs and the visit totals are not on one basis, which is the tension of step 2 seen from the other side. The savings apply only at visits where the blades and vanes are scrapped rather than repaired, which the interview places at second and later visits; at a first visit the lever is a repair (§40). Limits of the example: the blade count, the per-blade price, the vane-set price and the discount are respectively unverified, interview-sourced, assumed within a phrase and assumed; the visit totals are two contested consultancy ranges; and no manufacturer's catalogue price for a blade or a set was read (D4 U16). <span class="cite">[D4 §2.8; Safe Fly Aviation, 2026; In Practise, undated; reconciliation R-013, R-015; E.1 rule 4]</span></p></div>

### 44.3 Testing the claimed $2 million against the arithmetic

<div class="worked"><div class="h">Worked example 6.7 — What "up to $2 million per shop visit" requires of the five parts</div>
<p>Inputs: the claim, "up to $2 million per shop visit" from five CFM56 hot-section parts supplied to FTAI "at cost" (sourced: investor blogs; not in FTAI filings retrieved). The manufacturer's prices from worked example 6.6: blade set $1.6M or $3.08M; blades and vanes $2.5–3.0M (assumed within the sourced phrase). The other three parts (a low-pressure turbine vane, a shroud by one reading, and a fifth part the commentary does not name): no price retrieved.</p>
<p>Step 1, the effective discount the claim implies if the saving came from the named parts alone.</p>
<div class="eqblock">blade set at $1.6M:        2,000,000 ÷ 1,600,000 = 125%   (not reachable from the blade set alone)
blade set at $3.08M:       2,000,000 ÷ 3,080,000 = 65%
blades and vanes at $2.5M: 2,000,000 ÷ 2,500,000 = 80%
blades and vanes at $3.0M: 2,000,000 ÷ 3,000,000 = 67%</div>
<p>Step 2, what is left for the other parts at a market-style discount. At d = 40% the blade set saves $640,000 ($1.6M base), leaving $1,360,000 to come from the other four parts; at the same discount blades and vanes together save $1.0–1.2M, leaving $0.8–1.0M for the low-pressure turbine vane, the shroud and the fifth part, whose prices the research did not reach.</p>
<p>Step 3, the "at cost" reading. If "at cost" means FTAI pays roughly the manufacturing cost rather than a market PMA price, FTAI's saving per part is the manufacturer's list price less the maker's cost, which is larger than the discount a third-party buyer of the same part would receive. Whether that difference is large enough to reach the effective discounts in step 1 depends on the maker's cost, which no source states; the primer shows the required effective discount and does not assume a cost.</p>
<p>Step 4, the claim against the visit totals and the only filed-level comparators.</p>
<div class="eqblock">2,000,000 ÷ 1,200,000–1,600,000 = 125% to 167% of a restoration (lower range)
2,000,000 ÷ 1,800,000–2,500,000 = 80% to 111% of a restoration (upper range)
2,000,000 ÷ 75,000 = 27 times FTAI's quantified Bristol saving per visit
Southwest, fleet-wide: 54,000,000 ÷ 341 = 158,358 per part number per year
   (not convertible to a per-visit figure)</div>
<p>So the claim is reachable from the blade set alone only at the higher per-blade price and a 65% effective discount, is not reachable from the blade set alone at the interview price, and on either visit range is of the same size as, or larger than, the whole restoration as the consultancy prices it. The arithmetic shows that the claim is sensitive to which per-blade price is right (R-015), to what "at cost" means (R-026), to what the other three parts cost (unknown), and to what "shop visit" the blogs mean, since a heavy visit is larger than a restoration (R-013). The example neither confirms nor refutes the claim; it sets out what would have to be true. Limits of the example: every price input is third-party, unverified or assumed; the claim's own basis (against list price, at which visit, "up to") is not stated by its sources; and FTAI has published no per-visit PMA saving. <span class="cite">[Komodo Capital; Kairos Research; D4 §2.8 step 4, T1, T5; Insider Monkey, Oct 2025; Aviation Week, c. 2023; reconciliation R-013, R-015, R-026, R-027]</span></p></div>

<div class="box"><div class="h">What the PMA lever does not touch</div><p>Whatever the right per-blade price, an approved alternative part changes only the on-condition parts line of a shop visit. Life-limited parts, 40–60% of parts cost when they are replaced, remain single-source at the manufacturer's escalating list price, because no PMA exists for any CFM56 disk, spool or shaft. Labour, test and the shop's margin are untouched. And at a visit where the blades can still be repaired, no new blade is bought from anyone, so the lever is a repair, not a part. The reach of the PMA lever is therefore the new hot-section parts bought at second and later visits, which is a real and growing share of visits on a fleet kept flying past its planned retirement (Part III §19), and no more than that. <span class="cite">[Safe Fly Aviation, 2026; D4 U7, §2.7; In Practise, undated]</span></p></div>
