<div class="part-divider"><div class="pn">Part VIII</div><h1>FTAI Aviation Leasing and the Strategic Capital Initiative</h1><p>This Part covers the half of FTAI that owns things: the Aviation Leasing segment, with its aircraft, engines, rent, reserves and the Russian assets it lost, and the Strategic Capital Initiative, through which FTAI has begun to move aircraft ownership into vehicles funded by other people's money while keeping the servicing fee, a minority stake and the engine work. A reader who finishes it will be able to read FTAI's leasing disclosures line by line, trace every dollar that moves between FTAI and the vehicles it manages, and see where each lands in the accounts.</p></div>

### Translation-table rows answered

Part III §24 turns each demand driver into a requirement on the aftermarket. This Part answers the rows that bear on who owns mid-life aircraft and their engines, and on what terms. Lessors have been extending leases rather than taking aircraft back, which cuts the flow of aircraft into the used-sale market and leaves fewer acquisition candidates for mid-life buyers; the vehicles described in §61 and §62 buy aircraft with their leases attached and so compete for that reduced flow (Part III §24, row: lease extensions). The cap on 737 MAX output and the shortfall in Airbus deliveries keep 737NGs and A320ceos flying longer, which holds up the values and rents of exactly the aircraft the vehicles buy; the carriers that are retiring 737NGs regardless, WestJet among them, are the source of the off-lease aircraft FTAI buys for their engines in §67 (Part III §24, row: 737 MAX production cap; row: Airbus delivery shortfall; row: GTF groundings). Shop capacity is short and turnaround times are long, so the vehicles' engines are powered by module exchanges rather than conventional shop visits, which is the maintenance stream worked in §66 (Part III §24, row: MRO capacity shortfall). Low retirements starve the teardown pool and keep engines flying toward their life limits inside the vehicles' holding period (Part III §24, row: low retirements). And the freighter conversion row is the AEI collaboration in §67 (Part III §24, row: freighter conversion).

## 58. The Aviation Leasing segment: fleet, utilization, income

FTAI has two reportable segments. Part VII covered Aerospace Products, the segment that sells engines, modules and parts. This section covers the other one. Aviation Leasing is the segment that owns aircraft and engines and rents them out, and it is the part of FTAI that looks most like a conventional lessor. Part II §12 to §17 explain how an operating lease, a maintenance reserve, a securitization and a warehouse facility work; this Part does not repeat those mechanics beyond a sentence where one is needed.

<div class="defn"><b>Aviation Leasing segment (AL segment)</b><p>FTAI's reportable segment that, in the FY2025 10-K's words, "owns and manages aircraft and engines and leases and sells them, directly and through an equity-method investment". At 31 December 2025 it held 290 assets, 47 aircraft and 243 engines, including 8 aircraft and 17 engines in Russia; 37 aircraft and 143 engines were on lease and utilization was about 77% on the company's definition. Its revenue lines are lease income, maintenance revenue and asset sales revenue; it also records gains on sale of leasing equipment and its share of an equity-method investment's earnings. Segment Adjusted EBITDA was $500.1M in 2024 and $608.9M in 2025. <span class="cite">[FTAI 10-K FY2025, segment description and segment table]</span></p></div>

The company's own one-line description of what the segment does is worth quoting, because it explains why a maintenance company owns aircraft at all. FTAI "owns and maintains commercial jet engines with a focus on CFM56 and V2500 engines" and "also owns and leases jet aircraft which often facilitates the acquisition of engines at attractive prices". <span class="cite">[FTAI Q2 2026 release, 29 Jul 2026]</span> The aircraft is bought for the two engines hanging under its wings. Part II §15 explains why that is arithmetically sensible for a mid-life narrowbody, where the engines carry most of the value. The leasing book is therefore both a source of rent and a feedstock pipeline for the Module Factory of Part VII.

Two things in that table need explaining. The first is that the count of assets on lease and the utilization figure measure different things. By count, only 59% of the engines were on lease at the year end. By the company's measure, utilization was about 77%. The gap is not an error; it is a definition.

<div class="defn"><b>Utilization (FTAI definition)</b><p>FTAI's utilization is "the percent of days on-lease in the quarter weighted by the monthly average equity value of aviation leasing equipment, excluding airframes". Each engine counts in proportion to its book equity value, not one-for-one, and airframes are left out. A run-out engine awaiting a shop visit, or one written to zero in Russia, drags the count ratio down but barely moves the value-weighted figure. Willis Lease measures utilization as the share of its lease portfolio by book value that is on lease, 84.9% for 2025; the two companies' figures are not directly comparable, and Part XI §88 says so when it sets them side by side. <span class="cite">[FTAI 10-K FY2025; Willis Lease 10-K FY2025; registry C-3]</span></p></div>

So 77% on FTAI's definition is consistent with 59% of engines on lease by count if the off-lease engines are low in equity value. The research recorded this as a definitional gap, not a contradiction, and this Part quotes the 10-K definition rather than the count ratio whenever it uses the word. <span class="cite">[D6 T6]</span> The ratio also hints at where the yield of §58.4 comes from: if about a quarter of the value-weighted book earns nothing in a quarter, the engines that are on lease must earn roughly 1 ÷ 0.77, or about 1.3 times, the portfolio-wide rate.

The second point is the remaining term. Aircraft had 44 months of lease left on average and engines 38 months, at 31 December 2025. <span class="cite">[FTAI 10-K FY2025]</span> Those are the periods over which the rent in §58.2 is contracted. New-lease terms at signing, and the split between short-term and long-term engine leases in FTAI's book, were not retrieved. <span class="cite">[D6 U15]</span>

### 58.2 Lease income by period, and two tensions in the record

Lease income is the rent line. It is the first of three revenue lines the segment reports.

<div class="defn"><b>Lease income, maintenance revenue and asset sales revenue</b><p>The Aviation Leasing segment's three income-statement lines. Lease income is rent from operating leases of aircraft and engines ($235.2M for 2025). Maintenance revenue is maintenance reserves and end-of-lease compensation recognised as revenue when FTAI is no longer obliged to reimburse them, typically at lease end or on sale ($218.5M for 2025); Part II §13 explains the reserve mechanism. Asset sales revenue is the gross proceeds of aircraft and engine sales, with the asset's carrying value in cost of sales ($106.9M for 2025). Gains on sale of leasing equipment and the equity-method pick-up sit outside these three lines. <span class="cite">[FTAI 10-K FY2025, consolidated statement of operations, via the orchestrator's search; D10 §2.4]</span></p></div>

The reconciliation pass noted that the same search also returned a figure of "70,800" three times for different 2021 lines, which it judged a probable extraction artefact, and it recorded the 2021 segment figures as not reliably retrieved. <span class="cite">[orchestrator notes; reconciliation D.3]</span> The 2022 figures from the same filing sit in the expected order: $179,314K of lease income inside $199,040K of segment revenue. <span class="cite">[FTAI 10-K FY2022]</span> What would resolve 2021 is a direct read of the FY2022 10-K's segment note.

The second tension concerns 2024. The FY2025 10-K's segment table, as returned by one search, gives 2024 lease income of $234,411K. An earlier search of the same filing returned $255,338K. <span class="cite">[FTAI 10-K FY2025, two readings; reconciliation R-052]</span> The orchestrator's note on the discrepancy offers two possibilities: the two searches may have hit different lines, a total lease-income line against a segment lease-income line, or one of them may be an extraction error. Both figures come from the same filing and the 2025 figure of $235,210K is the same in both readings. <span class="cite">[orchestrator notes]</span> This Part shows the pair wherever 2024 lease income appears and does not average them.

Beyond the tensions, the direction of the series is not in doubt. Rent was flat to rising from 2021 through 2025, roughly $174M to $235M, and then fell by almost half in the first half of 2026. The 10-Q attributes the fall to one cause: the sale of the Seed Assets to the 2025 Partnership, which §60 describes. <span class="cite">[FTAI 10-Q Q1 2026]</span> Quarterly rent went from $68.4M in the first quarter of 2025 to $39.9M a year later, and from $62.4M in the second quarter of 2025 to $27.8M a year later. <span class="cite">[FTAI 10-Q Q1 2026; FTAI Q2 2026 release]</span> The 2025 annual figure of $235.2M averaged $58.8M a quarter; the 2026 run-rate in the second quarter was less than half of that.

### 58.4 What the book yields: a worked example with its bounds

A lessor's rent divided by the cost of what it rents out is its gross yield. Part II §14 defines the lease rate factor, the monthly version of the same ratio, and Part II §12 defines the gross leasing yield. Neither FTAI nor Willis Lease states a portfolio lease rate factor in any filing the research reached, so every figure below is derived. <span class="cite">[D6 U5; D11 U1; reconciliation K-14]</span>

<div class="defn"><b>Net book value (NBV)</b><p>Cost less accumulated depreciation and impairments. FTAI's "leasing equipment, net" was $1,545.8M at 31 December 2025 against $2,057.6M at cost. A yield on cost strips out depreciation policy; a yield on net book value shows the return on the carrying amount. Part XI §88 uses the same denominator for Air Lease's $29.1B of flight equipment. <span class="cite">[FTAI 10-K FY2025; D11 §2.2]</span></p></div>

<div class="worked"><div class="h">Worked example 8.1 — FTAI's implied gross leasing yield for 2025, three ways</div>
<p>Inputs. Leasing equipment at cost $2,963.452M at 31 December 2024 and $2,057.624M at 31 December 2025; net $2,373.730M and $1,545.804M (sourced: FTAI 10-K FY2025). FY2025 lease income $235.210M (sourced: FTAI 10-K FY2025, the same in both readings). 1H 2025 lease income $130.879M and 1H 2026 lease income $67.657M (sourced: 10-Q Q1 2026 and Q2 2026 release, summed). Utilization about 77% in Q4 2025 (sourced: 10-K).</p>
<div class="eqblock">average 2025 cost base = (2,963.452 + 2,057.624) ÷ 2 = 2,510.538

<p>Reading (i) is new to this Part: the research derived (ii) and (iii) before the FY2025 lease-income figure was available and asked that the 10-K figure be substituted, which (i) does. Reading (ii) overstates 2025 because rent fell in the second half after the Seed Asset sales; reading (iii) understates the yield if the cost base kept falling through the first half of 2026, which the guidance language implies. Three things depress the on-cost figure: the Russian assets, which sit at zero net book value but may still sit in gross cost (not retrieved, D6 U4); off-lease engines; and aircraft bought for their engines and parked or in work rather than leased. Limits of the example: average balances are two-point averages; no 30 June 2026 balance sheet was retrieved; and no external lease rate factor was retrieved for FTAI to check the result against. The derived figures that exist elsewhere in the research are 0.70% a month (a green-time rent on a half-life value, Part II §14), about 1.0% a month for Willis Lease's on-lease book (Part XI §88) and 0.9% a month assumed in the fee example of §66; none is a stated figure. <span class="cite">[D6 §2.7; D11 §2.2; reconciliation R-030]</span></p></div>

The yield is the bridge between this section and the next three. A book earning 9 to 12 cents a year on each dollar of cost, with about a quarter of its value idle at any time and some of it unrecoverable in Russia, is the asset FTAI chose in 2025 to start selling into vehicles funded by others. The rent fell by half as a result. What replaced it is the subject of §60 to §63.

## 59. Russia

In 2022, after Russia's invasion of Ukraine and the sanctions that followed, lessors worldwide lost access to aircraft and engines leased to Russian airlines. FTAI's exposure was small in asset count and material in money, and the filings record it in enough detail to follow from loss to recovery.

<div class="defn"><b>Russia assets, impairment and insurance recoveries (contingent cover)</b><p>In 2022 FTAI "terminated all lease agreements with Russian airlines" and recorded a $120.0M impairment, "net of maintenance deposits, to write-off the entire carrying value of leasing equipment assets that the company did not expect to recover from Ukraine and Russia". At 31 December 2025, 8 aircraft and 17 engines were "still located in Russia" with an insured value of $210.7M. Lessees must insure leased assets with FTAI named as insured for total loss, and FTAI buys contingent cover for off-lease periods and for cases where a lessee's policy fails to pay. Recoveries of $54.3M (nine months to 30 September 2025) and $44.6M (first quarter of 2026) have been disclosed. <span class="cite">[FTAI 10-K FY2022; 10-K FY2025; 10-Q Q3 2025; 10-Q Q1 2026]</span></p></div>

### 59.3 What remains, and what is unknown

Eight aircraft and seventeen engines were still in Russia at the end of 2025. <span class="cite">[FTAI 10-K FY2025]</span> They are the same counts the FY2022 10-K gave for Russia three years earlier, so none of those assets has been recovered physically. They sit in the fleet count of 290 assets in Exhibit 8.1 at a carrying value of zero. Whether they still sit in the $2,057.6M of leasing equipment at cost, which would depress the on-cost yield in worked example 8.1, was not retrieved. <span class="cite">[D6 U4]</span>

The four aircraft and one engine in Ukraine at the end of 2022 do not reappear in anything the research retrieved from later filings. Whether they were recovered, written off within the same $120.0M, or remain in Ukraine is unknown; the FY2023 and FY2024 10-Ks' Russia and insurance paragraphs would resolve it. <span class="cite">[D6 U4]</span> Part X §85 carries the Russia item among the risk factors the 10-K lists.

## 60. The shift to asset-light: Seed Assets and the 2025 Partnership

On 30 December 2024 FTAI announced that it would stop being the main owner of the aircraft it manages. The announcement launched the Strategic Capital Initiative, which §61 describes as a structure, and it named the first transaction: FTAI would sell 46 of its own on-lease aircraft into the first vehicle. This section follows what that did to the leasing book, to the gains line and to guidance.

### 60.1 The Seed Assets

<div class="defn"><b>Seed Assets</b><p>FTAI's term for the on-lease aircraft sold from its own balance sheet to start the 2025 Partnership: 46 aircraft "at an estimated net purchase price of $549 million" in the launch release, about $11.9M each. The 10-Qs attribute the fall in aircraft lease revenue in 2026 to "the sale of Seed Assets to the 2025 Partnership". The sales cut leasing equipment at cost by $906M during 2025 and produced a gain of $46.4M for the year. <span class="cite">[FTAI release, 30 Dec 2024; FTAI 10-Q Q1 2026; FTAI 10-K FY2025]</span></p></div>

A seed portfolio does for a new fund what it says. A vehicle that has raised commitments but owns nothing has no rent to show investors and no track record. Selling it a portfolio of aircraft already on lease to airlines gives it, on day one, contracted rent, a diversified set of lessees and an asset base against which to borrow. The sponsor, here FTAI, gets cash for assets it was carrying at historical cost, books the difference as a gain, and keeps a minority stake in the buyer. Part II §17 describes the same move in a securitization, where a lessor sells a portfolio to a special-purpose vehicle and keeps the equity and the servicing.

### 60.2 The gain, and a tension in the record

<div class="defn"><b>Gain on sale of leased equipment, and the gain on sale to the 2025 Partnership</b><p>The sale price of an asset sold from the leasing portfolio minus its net book value. FTAI recorded $46.4M of such gains on sales to the 2025 Partnership in FY2025; the three quarterly gains disclosed for 2025 sum to $50.1M for nine months, which is a tension (§60.2). Willis Lease recorded $54.0M of gains on sale of leased equipment in 2025, which Part XI §88 sets beside FTAI's figure. <span class="cite">[FTAI 10-K FY2025; FTAI 10-Qs 2025; Willis Lease 10-K FY2025]</span></p></div>

The gain line carries the Part's first arithmetic tension. The 10-Qs for the first three quarters of 2025 disclose gains on sales to the 2025 Partnership of $10.9M, $34.6M and $4.6M, on 4, 33 and 8 aircraft. Those sum to $50.1M on 45 aircraft for nine months. The FY2025 10-K states a full-year gain of $46.4M. <span class="cite">[FTAI 10-Qs Q1 to Q3 2025; FTAI 10-K FY2025; reconciliation R-054]</span> A nine-month sum $3.7M above the full-year figure means either that the fourth quarter recorded a net loss on sales to the Partnership of about $3.7M, or that the quarterly and annual figures count different things, for instance gross gains in one and gains net of some adjustment in the other. The filings as retrieved do not say which. Worked example 8.8 in §66 reconciles the seed sale with this tension in view.

What the quarterly figures do give is one data point on the cost basis, which the research otherwise could not find. <span class="cite">[D6 U12]</span> In the first quarter of 2025, proceeds of $58.9M on four aircraft produced a gain of $10.9M. The net book value of those four aircraft was therefore $48.0M, or $12.0M each, and the sale price was $14.7M each. The gain was 18.5% of proceeds. <span class="cite">[FTAI 10-Q Q1 2026, comparative; derived in this Part]</span> Whether those four were typical of the 46 is not known; the seed average of $11.9M per aircraft sits below the $14.7M those four fetched.

### 60.3 Where the gain sits in the accounts

The 10-K says the 2025 Partnership "acquires on-lease narrowbody aircraft from the Company ... with aircraft being sold to the 2025 Partnership for a gain", and that FTAI accounts for those sales under a standard for disposals outside ordinary revenue, describing them as "non-recurring in nature and not considered part of the Company's ordinary activities". <span class="cite">[FTAI 10-K FY2025]</span> Under that treatment the gain appears on a gain line rather than inside asset sales revenue, and the Aviation Leasing Adjusted EBITDA measure includes it. <span class="cite">[D7 §2.5; D6 §2.6]</span> §64 defines the standard and sets the "non-recurring" description beside the company's other statements about how often it expects to do this.

## 61. SCI structure: vehicles, two layers of debt, the Servicer role

<div class="defn"><b>Strategic Capital Initiative (SCI)</b><p>FTAI's asset-management arm, launched 30 December 2024. It raises third-party capital into closed-end vehicles that buy on-lease, mid-life 737NG and A320ceo aircraft; FTAI manages each vehicle as Servicer, co-invests as a limited partner, and supplies every engine shop visit for the fleet through module and engine exchanges with its Maintenance, Repair and Exchange business (Part VII §46). The Q2 2026 10-Q calls it "an asset management business that manages third-party capital to invest in on-lease aircraft" and says FTAI expects its primary investment activities going forward to run through it. It is not a reportable segment. <span class="cite">[FTAI release, 30 Dec 2024; FTAI 10-Q Q2 2026]</span></p></div>

The initiative has three parts that are easy to confuse: the vehicles, the debt under them, and FTAI's roles around them. This section takes them in that order and ends with the arithmetic that connects them.

### 61.1 The vehicle: a partnership on top of asset-owning SPVs

<div class="defn"><b>2025 Partnership (2025 SPV; FTAI SCI I; the inaugural Strategic Capital vehicle)</b><p>The first SCI vehicle, launched 30 December 2024: a closed-end partnership whose asset-owning special-purpose vehicles buy on-lease mid-life 737NG and A320ceo aircraft. It closed on 27 October 2025 at a $2.0B equity hard cap against a $1.5B target; a $2.5B asset-level debt commitment was arranged in February 2025; by August 2026 it had "approximately $6.0 billion of total capital across over 300 aircraft", was fully committed, had paid its first quarterly distribution on 30 June 2026 and was in "harvest phase". FTAI is its Servicer and holds a 19% limited-partner interest (20% in the Q3 2025 10-Q). Its engines are powered "exclusively via engine and module exchanges" with FTAI. <span class="cite">[FTAI releases, 30 Dec 2024, 26 Feb 2025, 27 Oct 2025, 17 Aug 2026; FTAI 10-K FY2025; FTAI 10-Qs Q3 2025, Q1 and Q2 2026]</span></p></div>

The filings call the first vehicle the "2025 Partnership" and say it acquires aircraft through "special purpose entities (SPVs) of the 2025 Partnership". <span class="cite">[FTAI 10-Q Q1 2026]</span> So there are two levels. At the top is a partnership, in which the investors hold interests. Below it are special-purpose vehicles, each a company that exists only to own one aircraft or a small pool of them and the debt secured on them. Part II §17 defines the special-purpose vehicle; aircraft lessors are usually organised this way so that each lender and each airline deals with a clean owner.

<div class="defn"><b>Limited partner (LP) and general partner (GP)</b><p>A limited partner is an investor who commits capital to a partnership, has limited liability and takes no part in managing it. A general partner manages the partnership and bears liability. The 2025 Partnership's limited partners are described as "asset managers, insurance companies, public pensions, foundations, endowments and family offices", plus FTAI at 19%. Which entity acts as general partner, and FTAI's relationship to it, were not located in anything the research reached. <span class="cite">[FTAI release, 27 Oct 2025; FTAI 10-K FY2025; D7 §2.1, §3.1]</span></p></div>

<div class="defn"><b>Closed-end vehicle</b><p>A fund that raises a fixed amount of equity commitments up to a hard cap, invests them during a deployment period, then stops buying and enters a harvest phase in which it collects rent, distributes cash to its investors and sells assets until it winds up. The 2025 Partnership is one. Its term, the length of its harvest phase, any extension rights and its exit route (a portfolio sale, a securitization, or piecemeal sales) were not located. <span class="cite">[D7 §2.1, U15]</span></p></div>

<div class="defn"><b>Equity commitment and hard cap</b><p>An equity commitment is an investor's promise to fund capital when the vehicle calls it; capital is drawn as assets are bought, not on signing. A hard cap is the maximum amount of commitments the sponsor will accept. The 2025 Partnership's target was $1.5B and it closed at an upsized hard cap of $2.0B; 19% of $2.0B is about $380M, FTAI's full commitment if drawn in proportion (derived in the research). <span class="cite">[FTAI release, 27 Oct 2025; D7 §2.5]</span></p></div>

<div class="defn"><b>Deployment, fully committed, and harvest phase</b><p>Deployment is the buying phase, during which commitments are called and aircraft acquired. "Fully committed" means every dollar of equity has been allocated to signed acquisitions, even if some closings are still pending; it is a different state from "fully deployed", which means closed and funded, and the releases use both words at different dates (§62.1). Harvest phase is what follows: the vehicle collects rent, pays distributions and sells assets until it winds up. The 2025 Partnership was described as "fully committed" in the 29 July 2026 release and as in "harvest phase" in the 17 August 2026 release. <span class="cite">[FTAI Q2 2026 release, 29 Jul 2026; FTAI release, 17 Aug 2026; registry C-21]</span></p></div>

<div class="defn"><b>On-lease</b><p>Already under an operating lease to an airline. A buyer of an on-lease aircraft acquires a contracted rent stream and a return-condition obligation rather than an empty aircraft, and lenders can underwrite the contracted rent. Everything the SCI vehicles buy is on-lease; Part II §15 treats the same idea under lease-attached trading. <span class="cite">[FTAI releases, 2025–2026; D7 §2.4]</span></p></div>

### 61.2 The two layers of debt

A vehicle of this kind borrows at the asset level, and it borrows in two forms at different stages of its life. Part II §17 explains both instruments; here the point is which one each vehicle used and what it tells the reader.

<div class="defn"><b>Asset-level debt (non-recourse debt)</b><p>Borrowing by the asset-owning SPVs, secured on the aircraft and their leases, with no recourse to the sponsor. If the aircraft and their rent do not cover the loan, the lenders' claim stops at the SPV; FTAI's own balance sheet is not exposed. The 2025 Partnership's $2.5B commitment of February 2025 was asset-level debt, with ATLAS SP Partners committing $2.0B directly and Deutsche Bank AG, New York Branch co-leading. <span class="cite">[FTAI release, 26 Feb 2025]</span></p></div>

<div class="defn"><b>Syndicated loan, structuring agent and lead arranger</b><p>A syndicated loan is one shared across many lenders under a single agreement, so no bank carries the whole exposure; the 2026 warehouse is syndicated among 13 banks. The structuring agent designs the deal; the lead arranger assembles the lender group. ATLAS SP Partners (a structured-products business majority owned by Apollo funds) was structuring agent and lead arranger of the 2025 facility, and ATLAS SP and Deutsche Bank were co-structuring agents of the 2026 warehouse. <span class="cite">[FTAI releases, 26 Feb 2025 and 17 Aug 2026]</span></p></div>

The first vehicle's debt was arranged as a $2.5B commitment in February 2025, eight months before its equity closed. <span class="cite">[FTAI release, 26 Feb 2025]</span> The second vehicle's debt is a warehouse facility: a revolving line that funds aircraft one at a time as the vehicle buys them, until the pool is large enough to refinance in a term deal, typically a securitization (Part II §17). It closed on 14 August 2026 at $2.0B with 13 lenders and an option to grow to $3.0B. <span class="cite">[FTAI release, 17 Aug 2026]</span> The rates, advance rates, tenors and covenants of both facilities were not disclosed in anything the research reached, nor was whether the 2025 facility was later upsized. <span class="cite">[D7 U11]</span>

<div class="box"><div class="h">Which layer the sponsor sees</div><p>FTAI's consolidated balance sheet shows none of this debt. The $3.45B of long-term debt it reported at 31 March 2026 (Part X §82) is FTAI's own senior notes; the vehicles' asset-level debt and warehouse borrowings sit inside unconsolidated entities and reach FTAI only through its 19% share of the Partnership's results. That is what "non-recourse" and "unconsolidated" together mean for a reader of FTAI's statements: the leverage described in worked example 8.3 is real, but it is the Partnership's, and whether FTAI was required to consolidate it is the question §64.3 records as open. <span class="cite">[FTAI 10-Q Q1 2026; D7 §2.6]</span></p></div>

### 61.3 FTAI's three roles

FTAI stands in three relationships to each vehicle. It is a limited partner, with 19% of the 2025 Partnership. It is the Servicer. And it is the exclusive supplier of engine maintenance to the vehicle's fleet.

<div class="defn"><b>Servicer</b><p>The party contracted to manage a vehicle's assets: leasing and remarketing the aircraft, collecting rent and reserves, and overseeing their technical condition, for a fee. In a securitization the lessor that sold the assets is usually the servicer (Willis Lease for its WEST trusts, Part II §17). FTAI "as the Servicer, provides aircraft management services to the 2025 Partnership, and receives customary, market-based compensation for providing such services". The compensation's rate and base are not disclosed (§63.2). <span class="cite">[FTAI 10-K FY2025; D6 §2.5]</span></p></div>

The third role is stated in the same sentence in every SCI release: engines owned by the vehicle "will be powered exclusively via engine and module exchanges with FTAI's Maintenance, Repair and Exchange (MRE) business". <span class="cite">[FTAI releases, 26 Feb 2025, 25 Mar 2025, 17 Aug 2026]</span> Part VII §46 explains what an exchange is: FTAI removes the module that triggered the removal, fits a serviceable one from its inventory, and takes the run-out unit as feedstock. For the vehicle this replaces a 90 to 120 day shop visit with an exchange; for FTAI it makes every engine in a 300-aircraft fleet a customer of the Module Factory for as long as the vehicle owns it. In February 2025 FTAI described the market it addresses this way as $22B, with its own share about 5% as of 2024. <span class="cite">[FTAI release, 26 Feb 2025]</span> The accounting consequences of selling maintenance to an entity one partly owns are in §63.3 and §63.4.

<div class="box"><div class="h">Why the buyer of the aircraft and the supplier of the engines' maintenance are tied together</div><p>A mid-life narrowbody's value is mostly in its engines (Part II §15), and the largest cost its owner faces is the engines' next shop visits (Part I §7). A vehicle that buys 300 such aircraft is buying 600 engines approaching or past those visits. Contracting all of that work to one supplier in advance fixes the vehicle's largest cost line with a party that also holds 19% of its equity and manages its assets. For the supplier, it converts a one-off aircraft sale into a maintenance relationship lasting the vehicle's life. The releases describe the arrangement; the contract's pricing terms are not disclosed (§63.3). <span class="cite">[FTAI releases, 2025–2026; D7 §2.4]</span></p></div>

### 61.4 The leverage arithmetic, worked

<div class="defn"><b>Loan-to-cost</b><p>A vehicle's debt divided by its total capital (equity plus debt). It is distinct from loan-to-value, which divides debt by the appraised value of the assets (Part II §17). For the 2025 Partnership it is derived rather than stated: about 67% if $6.0B of total capital sits on $2.0B of equity, or 56% if only the $2.5B debt commitment is drawn. <span class="cite">[D7 §2.3]</span></p></div>

<div class="worked"><div class="h">Worked example 8.3 — Leverage of the 2025 Partnership from the disclosed figures</div>
<p>Inputs: equity commitments $2.0B (sourced: release, 27 Oct 2025); asset-level debt commitment $2.5B (sourced: release, 26 Feb 2025); "approximately $6.0 billion of total capital across over 300 aircraft" (sourced: release, 17 Aug 2026). Total capital is taken as equity plus debt (assumed: the release does not define it).</p>

### 62.2 OneIM and the other investors

<div class="defn"><b>OneIM (One Investment Management)</b><p>An investment manager announced on 25 March 2025 as FTAI's partner "on the inaugural vehicle", in a release that described more than $4 billion of deployment for the vehicle. OneIM's commitment size, and whether its role is that of an anchor limited partner or a co-general partner, are not disclosed. The ">$4 billion" is the vehicle's deployment capacity, not OneIM's commitment. <span class="cite">[FTAI release, 25 Mar 2025; reconciliation R-059]</span></p></div>

The research's chain map and the company dossier's timeline both carried the March 2025 event as "OneIM partnership, $4B", which reads as if OneIM committed $4B. The release does not say that. It describes a partnership on the vehicle and a deployment figure for the vehicle as a whole, which the February release had already given as "$4.0+ billion". <span class="cite">[FTAI releases, 26 Feb 2025 and 25 Mar 2025; reconciliation R-059; E.1 rule 31]</span> This Part therefore writes it as the box does: more than $4 billion of deployment for the inaugural vehicle, with OneIM's commitment and role undisclosed.

### 63.4 Profit eliminations: two readings of one sentence

<div class="defn"><b>Profit elimination (intra-entity profit elimination)</b><p>When a company sells to an entity it accounts for under the equity method, the profit on the sale is not all earned yet from the group's point of view, because the company still owns a share of the buyer and so a share of the asset. The profit is removed from the equity-method earnings and recognised later as the investee uses or sells the asset. FTAI: "the profit from the MRE Contract revenue is eliminated through equity method earnings and will be recognized over time as the 2025 Partnership generates income from leasing and sales activities". Eliminations were $10.0M in the first quarter of 2026 and $6.6M in the second, $16.6M for the half. Whether the eliminated amount is 19% of the profit or all of it is not stated. <span class="cite">[FTAI 10-Qs Q1 and Q2 2026; reconciliation R-047]</span></p></div>

The method matters because the same $16.6M implies very different margins on the $404.0M of related-party sales, and the filing sentence supports either reading. <span class="cite">[reconciliation R-047; E.1 rule 12]</span>

<div class="worked"><div class="h">Worked example 8.4 — What the eliminations imply under each method</div>
<p>Inputs: eliminations $10.0M (Q1 2026), $6.597M (Q2 2026), $16.597M (1H 2026) (sourced: 10-Qs); MRE Contract revenue to and from the Partnership $221.2M (Q1), $182.8M (Q2), $404.0M (1H) (sourced: 10-Qs via the orchestrator notes); stake 19% (sourced: 10-K and 2026 10-Qs) or 20% (sourced: Q3 2025 10-Q).</p>
<div class="eqblock">reading A, proportional to the stake: gross profit = 16.597 ÷ 0.19 = $87.4M for 1H 2026 (about $174M annualised); at 20%, 16.597 ÷ 0.20 = $83.0M
   implied margin on MRE sales to the Partnership: 87.4 ÷ 404.0 = 21.6% (Q1: 52.6 ÷ 221.2 = 23.8%; Q2: 34.7 ÷ 182.8 = 19.0%)

   implied margin: 16.6 ÷ 404.0 = 4.1% (Q1: 10.0 ÷ 221.2 = 4.5%; Q2: 6.6 ÷ 182.8 = 3.6%)</div>
<p>Reading A is the usual treatment of profit on sales to an equity-method investee: the investor eliminates its own share and leaves the rest, because the other owners have paid for it at arm's length. Reading B would mean FTAI defers all of it until the Partnership earns it back. The 10-Q sentence, "recognized over time as the 2025 Partnership generates income", is consistent with either. Under reading A, the margin on related-party sales (about 22%) would sit below the segment's 28.5% Adjusted EBITDA margin for the second quarter (Part VII §53); under reading B it would be far below it. Neither is a reported figure. Limits of the example: the margin lines compare a profit measure of unknown definition with a revenue figure; the second-quarter MRE revenue includes "purchase" as well as "sale" transactions; and the deferred profit will be recognised in later periods on a schedule the filing does not give. <span class="cite">[FTAI 10-Qs Q1 and Q2 2026; D7 §2.5, T7; reconciliation R-047, R-048]</span></p></div>

The reported effect, whichever reading is right, is the one the research recorded as a constraint: profit on MRE sales to the Partnership is deferred, so the Aerospace Products margin reported on this volume in the period of sale is lower than on the same volume sold to a third party. <span class="cite">[D7 §6]</span>

## 64. Accounting and segment placement

Three accounting choices shape how everything in §63 appears in FTAI's statements: the Partnership is not consolidated; the aircraft sales into it are booked as disposals outside ordinary revenue; and the related-party streams are disclosed in the 10-Q notes rather than on the face of the statements. This section states each as the filings do and records the questions the research could not close.

### 64.1 The equity method, and why 19% qualifies

<div class="defn"><b>Equity method (equity-method investment; equity pick-up; equity in earnings of unconsolidated entities)</b><p>An accounting method for an investment over which the investor has significant influence but not control. The investment is carried on the balance sheet at cost, increased by the investor's share of the investee's earnings and decreased by distributions received and by eliminated profit; the share of earnings appears on one income-statement line, and none of the investee's revenue, assets or debt is consolidated. A 20% to 50% holding is the conventional range, but the test is influence, not a percentage floor: FTAI's 19% limited-partner interest, held alongside its role as Servicer, is accounted for this way. FTAI's other equity-method investments at 31 December 2025 were the Advanced Engine Repair JV (25%, $22.4M), QuickTurn Europe (50%, $10.0M) and Prime Engine Accessories (50%, carrying value not returned), treated in Part VII §50. <span class="cite">[FTAI 10-K FY2025; FTAI 10-Q Q2 2026; registry C-9; orchestrator notes]</span></p></div>

Two of the research dossiers defined the method by the 20% to 50% range and a third observed that FTAI's 19% interest uses it. The registry resolves the conflict in the box above: significant influence is the test, and a Servicer with a 19% stake has it. <span class="cite">[registry C-9]</span> The consequence for a reader is the one the box states. The Partnership's roughly $6.0B of total capital, its 300-plus aircraft, its asset-level debt and its rent appear nowhere in FTAI's consolidated revenue, assets or liabilities. What appears is a single investment line of $365.5M and a single earnings line whose amount was not located.

One further equity-method investment is referred to without being named. The segment description says Aviation Leasing leases and sells assets "directly and through an equity-method investment". <span class="cite">[FTAI 10-K FY2025]</span>

<div class="defn"><b>Equity-method investment in Aviation Leasing</b><p>The unnamed investment through which the Aviation Leasing segment "leases and sells" assets, in the FY2025 10-K's segment description. Its counterparty, FTAI's ownership percentage, the assets inside it and the income it has contributed by year were not retrieved. Whether it is the 19% interest in the 2025 Partnership, which would be the obvious candidate since the Partnership leases aircraft FTAI formerly owned, is not established in anything the research reached and is carried as an unknown. <span class="cite">[FTAI 10-K FY2025; D6 U11; reconciliation K-07]</span></p></div>

If the Leasing segment's equity-method investment is the Partnership interest, then the segment's Adjusted EBITDA includes FTAI's share of the Partnership's results, and the Leasing guidance of §60.4 includes that share. If it is not, the Partnership's contribution sits in Corporate and Other or elsewhere. The FY2025 10-K's investments note would settle it; the research did not retrieve the sentence that does. <span class="cite">[reconciliation K-07]</span>

### 64.2 "Non-recurring" sales under a recurring programme

<div class="defn"><b>ASC 610-20</b><p>The US accounting standard for gains and losses on the derecognition of nonfinancial assets that are not part of a company's ordinary revenue-generating activities. FTAI books aircraft sales to the 2025 Partnership under it, describing them as "non-recurring in nature and not considered part of the Company's ordinary activities". The gain ($46.4M for FY2025) therefore appears on a gain line rather than inside asset sales revenue, while the segment's Adjusted EBITDA includes it. <span class="cite">[FTAI 10-K FY2025; D7 §2.5]</span></p></div>

The reconciliation recorded a tension between that accounting description and the company's description of its strategy, and asks that both be set out. On one side, the launch release frames the market as supporting "$3.0+ billion of capital annually", the Q2 2026 10-Q says FTAI expects its primary investment activities going forward to run through SCI, and a second vehicle began buying in August 2026. On the other, the 10-K describes the aircraft sales into the first vehicle as "non-recurring in nature and not considered part of the Company's ordinary activities". <span class="cite">[FTAI release, 30 Dec 2024; FTAI 10-Q Q2 2026; FTAI 10-K FY2025; reconciliation R-057]</span> Both statements are as made. The accounting description determines which line the gain sits on; the strategy description determines how often a reader should expect such gains. Part X §80 treats what the distinction does to the composition of Adjusted EBITDA.

### 64.3 Consolidation, and the question not answered

<div class="defn"><b>Variable interest entity (VIE) and primary beneficiary</b><p>Under US GAAP, an entity whose equity holders lack the power to direct its activities, or whose equity is too thin to fund it without support, is a variable interest entity, and the party with the power to direct its most significant activities and exposure to its economics (the primary beneficiary) must consolidate it even without a majority of the votes. A sponsor that manages a partnership's assets, supplies its largest service contract and holds 19% of its equity would be expected to have performed this analysis. FTAI does not consolidate the 2025 Partnership; the analysis and its reasoning were not located. <span class="cite">[D7 §2.6, U18]</span></p></div>

The result is known: the Partnership is unconsolidated and FTAI records an equity-method investment. The basis for it is not. The accounting policies note that would state whether the Partnership was assessed as a variable interest entity and why FTAI is not its primary beneficiary was not reached. <span class="cite">[D7 U18]</span>

### 64.4 Related-party disclosure, and what is not disclosed

<div class="defn"><b>Related-party transaction</b><p>A transaction between a company and an entity it controls, manages or significantly influences, which accounting rules require to be disclosed because it is not necessarily on arm's-length terms. Every SCI stream in §63 is one: FTAI sells aircraft to, services, sells engines and modules to, and holds equity in the same partnership. The 10-Qs disclose the servicing fees received, the aircraft sales and proceeds, the engine and module sales and their profit eliminations, and the carrying value. The pricing policy for aircraft and for exchanges is not disclosed, and no published critique specific to SCI's related-party pricing was located. <span class="cite">[FTAI 10-Qs Q1 and Q2 2026; D7 §2.6, U5, U6, U19]</span></p></div>

The disclosure that exists is in the 10-Q notes and is the source of nearly every figure in Exhibit 8.10. The items it does not contain are the ones a reader would need to judge the terms: how aircraft are priced when they move from FTAI's book to the Partnership's, how exchanges under the MRE Contract are priced, the servicing fee's rate and base, and whether a promote exists. <span class="cite">[D7 U1, U2, U5, U6]</span> The agreements that would answer these were listed as exhibits 10.8 and 10.9 to the Q2 2026 10-Q in the filing index; they were not read. <span class="cite">[D7 U1]</span>

The research also looked for outside commentary on these terms and found none. The Muddy Waters short report of January 2025 concerned the reporting of Aerospace Products, treated in Part VII §54 and Part X §85; a broker's comment reported by The Fly at the time said the report "does not appear to consider" SCI. <span class="cite">[TipRanks/The Fly, Jan 2025; D7 T9, U19]</span> Sell-side notes, later short-seller updates and the financial press were not reached within the research budget, so the absence of a located critique is a fact about the search, not about the record.

One last placement question is shared with Part VII. The MRE Contract revenue line sits, by arithmetic, inside the Aerospace Products segment: segment revenue equals the products line plus the MRE line in three documents that do not quote each other. The segment note's explicit mapping sentence was not retrieved, and the research treats it as open. <span class="cite">[reconciliation B-1, R-043; E.1 rule 8]</span> For this Part the consequence is that the related-party share of Aerospace Products revenue computed in §63.3 rests on that arithmetic rather than on a quoted sentence.
