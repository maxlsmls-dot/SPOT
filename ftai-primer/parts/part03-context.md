<div class="part-divider"><div class="pn">Part III</div><h1>The context: fleet, retirements, new engines, and the translation table</h1><p>This Part explains why the CFM56 aftermarket is where it is in 2026: how many engines there are, why far fewer of them retired than their makers planned, what went wrong with the engines meant to replace them, how few new aircraft the airframers delivered, and how lessors and airlines behaved as a result. It ends with a translation table that turns each of those facts into a specific requirement on the aftermarket. A reader who finishes it will be able to name the driver behind any shop-visit, used-part, lease or capital figure in Parts IV to XI.</p></div>

### Where this Part sits in the build

The primer is built in layers. Parts I and II are first principles: the engine as a machine, and the money that moves around it through leases, reserves and financing. Part III is the context shock. It takes the fleet those first principles describe and asks what happened to it between 2019 and 2026: which engines retired and which did not, why the replacement engines and airframes arrived late, and how owners behaved when they could not get new aircraft. None of that changes how a turbofan works or how a lease is priced. It changes the quantities: how many engines need a shop visit, how many are torn down, how many spares are needed and for how long, and what an extension costs. Section 24 turns each documented driver into a numbered row stating the requirement it creates and what it leaves untouched. Parts IV to XI, the segments, open by naming the rows they answer. This Part depends on Parts I and II for its terms and on nothing written later.

## 18. The fleet and the installed base

Every shop visit, every used part and every lease described in this primer is sold against an engine that already exists. So the first question is how many engines exist, which state each is in, and how hard it flies. This section defines demand and the states a fleet can be in, then sets out every count of the CFM56 fleet the research reached, with what each count covers. It closes with how mature that fleet is, meaning how many of its engines have never been to a shop.

<div class="defn"><b>Aftermarket demand</b><p>Aftermarket demand for an engine type is the sum of the work and assets needed to keep that type flying: shop visits, modules, spare engines, used serviceable material and leases of engines and aircraft. It is generated by the installed base multiplied by how hard that base flies, net of the engines that leave. Every row of the translation table in §24 names which of these requirements a driver raises. <span class="cite">[D9 §2.1, definition assembled in the research]</span></p></div>

Part I §2.2 boxed the installed base as the population of engines fitted to aircraft in the operating fleet. It noted that the published "in service" counts may include spare and stored engines, since no source states its basis. That reminder matters here. A spare engine in a warehouse and an engine on a parked aircraft generate no flight cycles and so no maintenance need. The firms that count fleets, Cirium (a data provider) and IBA (an appraiser that also publishes fleet data), sort aircraft into three states.

<div class="defn"><b>Fleet states: in service, parked (stored), retired (withdrawn)</b><p>An aircraft in service is assigned to an operator and flying, or in short-term maintenance. A parked or stored aircraft is not flying but has not been formally withdrawn; an aircraft parked for more than about 90 days usually counts as stored, and a stored aircraft can return to service or go on to retirement. A retired or withdrawn aircraft has formally left the operating fleet: it is scrapped whole, parted out so its engines and components can be sold separately (Part V §32), or converted to another use, most commonly a passenger-to-freighter (P2F) conversion (Part II §15). <span class="cite">[D9 §2.1]</span></p></div>

The states move in both directions except the last. Stored aircraft returned to service in volume after 2021. A retired aircraft does two opposite things to the aftermarket at once. It removes a future customer for shop visits, and it adds supply of used parts and of engines with life remaining. That double effect is why the retirement rate in §19 appears on both sides of the translation table.

<div class="defn"><b>Average age</b><p>The average age of a fleet is the mean number of years since delivery of its aircraft. Maintenance need rises with age in steps rather than smoothly: the first shop visit, the first life-limited-part replacement, the first heavy airframe check. The retirement decision is largely age-related cost set against what the aircraft is worth kept (§19.1). The average age of the world's commercial aircraft was about 15 years at December 2025, per the airline trade body IATA (the International Air Transport Association) as cited in trade press. <span class="cite">[D9 §2.1; IATA Global Outlook, Dec 2025, as cited by Aviation Business News]</span></p></div>

### 18.3 How mature the fleet is

A second way to describe a fleet is by how many shop visits its engines have had. The research used the phrase "fleet maturity" for this without defining it; here it means the share of the installed base by the number of shop visits endured, none, one, or two or more. It matters because an engine that has not yet had a first visit is a visit still to come, and because the first visit on a mature engine is usually the heaviest event of its life so far. Part I §6.2 explained that first runs are long, with the latest -5B and -7B variants often flown almost to their life-limited-part limit before the first removal.

## 19. Retirements: how the decision is made and what happened 2019–2026

Part I §2.3 boxed the retirement rate as the share of a fleet withdrawn in a year, and gave GE's falling planning assumption in one sentence. This section is the full treatment. It explains how an owner decides to retire an aircraft, sets out what actually retired between 2019 and 2025 with the two data providers' conflicting counts, follows GE's planning number down from 3–4% to "below 1%", and works the arithmetic of what one and three-quarter points of retirement rate mean in engines a year.

### 19.1 How the decision is made

An aircraft is retired by its owner, an airline or a lessor, when keeping it in service costs more than it is worth kept, and when the alternatives of selling it on, converting it to a freighter or parting it out are better. Five inputs enter that decision. <span class="cite">[D9 §2.3]</span>

The first input is age, and the way maintenance cost arrives in lumps rather than smoothly. An engine performance-restoration shop visit opens the core to restore exhaust-gas-temperature margin (Part I §5 and §6). A life-limited-part (LLP) replacement forces the engine open when a rotating part reaches its certified cycle limit (Part I §4). A heavy airframe check does the same to the airframe. Each is a multi-million-dollar event. Acumen Aviation's December 2025 note on the 737-700 states that "as the 737-700 fleet ages, heavy checks and major engine shop visits increasingly exceed the residual value of the aircraft, driving earlier retirements or conversions". <span class="cite">[Acumen Aviation, 18 Dec 2025]</span> A 2023 analysis found that aircraft become vulnerable to retirement at 16 to 18 or more years, particularly if the owner does not want to pay for the next heavy check or shop visit, and that the roughly 570 A320ceo-family aircraft then stored for 90 or more days had an average age of 17.1 years. <span class="cite">[Naveo, AFRA panel, 6 Jun 2023]</span>

<div class="defn"><b>Maintenance cliff</b><p>The maintenance cliff is the point at which the next heavy maintenance event, an engine shop visit, a life-limited-part replacement or a heavy airframe check, costs more than the aircraft is worth kept. An owner at the cliff retires, converts or sells the aircraft rather than pay for the event. Aircraft become vulnerable to it at about 16 to 18 years and older. Aviation Week's rule of thumb that a CFM56 needs a shop visit roughly every eight years makes 24 years a de facto retirement date, because a third full overhaul is "deemed too expensive". <span class="cite">[D9 §2.3; Naveo, 6 Jun 2023; Aviation Week, "Retirement Uptick Will Restock Used Engine Parts", date not captured]</span></p></div>

The second input is lease economics. At the end of an operating lease (Part II §12) the lessor chooses between extending with the current airline, moving the aircraft to a new lessee, or selling or parting it out. The airline, for its part, chooses between extending and returning. Both choices turn on whether a replacement aircraft is available, and between 2023 and 2026 it usually was not (§22).

<div class="defn"><b>Lease transition</b><p>A lease transition is the move of an aircraft from one lessee to the next. Between the two leases the lessor pays for reconfiguration of the cabin, for the maintenance needed to meet the next lessee's delivery condition, and for the downtime while the aircraft earns no rent. Transition is the alternative to extension (§22.3), and its cost is one reason a lessor extends when it can. <span class="cite">[D9 §2.3]</span></p></div>

The third input is freighter conversion, which Part II §15 owns. A passenger-to-freighter conversion gives a 20-plus-year-old airframe a second life. Cargo Facts reports narrowbody freighters, the 737 and 757, being converted at 21 to 22 years of age on average. <span class="cite">[Cargo Facts, date not captured]</span> More than 260 737-800 freighters were in service as of mid-2025 with a conversion pipeline exceeding 300, against fewer than 50 737-700 conversions. <span class="cite">[Acumen Aviation, 18 Dec 2025; Cargo Facts]</span> A conversion candidate still needs engines with life on them. So conversion is a source of demand for engines with green time (the remaining usable life on an engine before its next mandatory shop visit, Part II §16) or for shop visits, and not a source of teardown supply.

The fourth input is part-out value (Part V §32). When the value of an aircraft as a sum of parts exceeds its value flying, the owner parts it out. For a mid-life CFM56-powered narrowbody the engines are the dominant share of that sum, and Part V §32 works the numbers. The consultancy figures the research reached are that a run-out CFM56 is bought as feedstock for $0.8–1.2M, yields $1.6–2.2M of used material, and nets $0.4–0.8M before repair cost. <span class="cite">[Safe Fly Aviation, 2026, consultancy blog]</span> Rising used-part prices raise part-out value and so, at the margin, encourage retirement. That is a stabilising feedback. The research records that it was not strong enough to lift retirements in 2024 to 2026. <span class="cite">[D9 §2.3]</span>

The fifth input is replacement availability, and it governs the other four.

<div class="box"><div class="h">Replacement availability is the gate</div><p>None of the first four inputs triggers a retirement if the airline cannot get a replacement aircraft. An airline at the maintenance cliff with no new aircraft to fly pays for the shop visit anyway, because the alternative is to shrink. A lessor whose lessee cannot find a replacement extends the lease rather than take the aircraft back into a market with no buyer-operators. This is the single mechanism through which the new-engine and airframer problems of §20 and §21 act on the old fleet: they close the gate. Cirium Ascend's count that only about a dozen mid-life 737NGs and fewer than 25 A320ceos were available to rent anywhere in the world is the measure of how closed it was. <span class="cite">[D9 §2.3; Cirium Ascend via Bloomberg, 2023–24 context]</span></p></div>

The 2024 count is the tension. <div class="defn"><b>Global Replacement Ratio</b><p>The Global Replacement Ratio is the number of deliveries needed to replace retirements, expressed as a share of total deliveries over a period. Cirium put it at 46.1% for 2025 on 2015–2024 data: fewer than half of new deliveries were replacing a retired aircraft, and the rest were growth. Named programmes show the decision being made both ways at once. United, WestJet and Copa have started retiring 737NGs in favour of the 737 MAX 8. <span class="cite">[Acumen Aviation, 18 Dec 2025]</span> Wizz Air announced a gradual phase-out of its A321ceo fleet on 24 March 2026. <span class="cite">[Wizz Air release, 24 Mar 2026]</span> Against those, Lauda Europe, part of the Ryanair group, is extending A320 leases through the early 2030s because the transition to new aircraft proved too costly, and Lufthansa and Ryanair were reported holding onto older aircraft to fill the gap. <span class="cite">[Travel Extra, 2026; Bloomberg via AJOT]</span> IBA noted at the same time that delivery delays had led operators to maximise daily utilisation across all age groups, with mature passenger-narrowbody utilisation "rising significantly". <span class="cite">[IBA via AviTrader, 24 Sep 2024]</span> That utilisation effect has its own row in the translation table, because it adds cycles without adding engines.

### 19.4 Both sides of the ledger

The retirement rate is one number with two effects, and the translation table carries them as separate rows. Lower retirements mean more engines accruing cycles, so more shop visits, more life-limited-part replacements as retained engines reach their limits, and more full restorations in place of green-time run-outs; that is row T-1 in §24. Lower retirements also mean fewer teardowns, so less used material and fewer green-time engines, and higher prices for both; that is row T-9. The shop-visit research describes the second effect from the shop's side: "the supply of used serviceable material remains thin, leaving shops more reliant on newly manufactured core parts". <span class="cite">[AVM (Aviation Maintenance magazine), 2025, via D2 §2.4]</span> The used-material research describes it from the trader's side, with AerSale reporting a "hypercompetitive feedstock environment" and its whole-asset sales falling from 20 engines and one aircraft in 2024 to 13 engines in 2025. <span class="cite">[AerSale, 5 Mar 2026, as relayed in search results; D3 §2.6]</span> Part V §33 carries the supply side in full.

## 20. New-generation engines: GTF and LEAP durability, and what they did to the old fleet

The A320neo and the 737 MAX (the re-engined 737) were designed to replace the A320ceo and the 737NG. Two things slowed the replacement. The new engines spend less time on wing than planned, time on wing being the interval from an engine's installation to its first removal (Part I §5). And the airframers delivered fewer aircraft than planned (§21). Both keep older aircraft flying longer and harder. This section takes the engines. Part I §10.2 boxed the LEAP (CFM's new-generation engine, the LEAP-1A on the A320neo family and the LEAP-1B as the sole engine of the 737 MAX) and its durability kit; this section carries what each new engine's problem did to the old fleet, which Part I left to it.

Two new engines are involved, and they are not symmetric. The LEAP comes from the same manufacturer as the CFM56 and sits on both airframe families. The other comes from Pratt & Whitney and sits on one.

<div class="defn"><b>Geared turbofan (GTF)</b><p>The geared turbofan is Pratt & Whitney's new-generation engine. A reduction gearbox between the fan and the low-pressure turbine lets each turn at its own best speed, which the CFM56's direct-drive design does not allow. The PW1100G is one of two engine options on the Airbus A320neo family, the other being the LEAP-1A; the PW1500G and PW1900G are the sole engines of the Airbus A220 and the Embraer E2. Pratt & Whitney's parent is RTX. <span class="cite">[D9 §2.5]</span></p></div>

### 20.1 The Pratt & Whitney GTF powder-metal issue

<div class="defn"><b>GTF powder-metal issue</b><p>Certain turbine discs in the GTF are made by a powder-metallurgy route, in which metal powder is consolidated into a disc. Powder produced over a period of years was found to be contaminated. The affected discs must be inspected or replaced, which can be done only with the engine off the wing and opened. RTX disclosed the defect in July to September 2023, estimated that 600 to 700 engines would be pulled for inspection, took a charge of about $3 billion on 11 September 2023, and later estimates put the total programme cost at about $7 billion. <span class="cite">[D9 §2.5; BusinessDay, 11 Sep 2023; Mobility Outlook, date not captured]</span></p></div>

The planning figure and the outturn diverged. RTX's planning figure at the time of disclosure was that around 350 aircraft on average would be grounded across 2024 to 2026. <span class="cite">[The Flying Engineer, citing RTX, 2026]</span> Cirium data cited in trade press put 835 PW1100G-powered aircraft grounded or in storage globally as of late 2025. <span class="cite">[Cirium via The Flying Engineer, 2026]</span> The same source gives "720 out of 1,912 A320neo family aircraft, nearly four in ten, currently out of service due to engine issues"; the denominator is not defined in the material reached, and the research judged it most plausibly to be the Pratt & Whitney-powered A320neo sub-fleet rather than all A320neos. <span class="cite">[D9 T7, U5]</span> An aircraft unable to fly for lack of a serviceable part or engine is said to be on the ground, abbreviated AOG (aircraft on ground), a term Part II §14 carries because short-term engine leases exist to cover such events.

RTX's own framing at its full-year 2025 results on 27 January 2026 was that 2026 is "the third year of elevated AOG levels"; that AOG levels were down more than 20% from their 2025 peak; that PW1100 AOG was down 25% year to date, enabled by a 40% increase in maintenance output and a 23% reduction in turnaround time; that fourth-quarter 2025 GTF maintenance output was up 39% year on year; and that customer-compensation cash outflows were about $1 billion in 2025, about $700 million expected in 2026, and about $2.8 billion cumulative by the end of 2026. <span class="cite">[RTX FY2025 results via Leeham, 27 Jan 2026]</span> Trade commentary expects the issue to be fully worked through only in 2027 to 2028. <span class="cite">[The Flying Engineer, 2026]</span> Named effects include Turkish Airlines grounding more than 40 aircraft and Wizz Air signing a compensation agreement with Pratt & Whitney. <span class="cite">[Aviacionline; Shares Magazine; dates not captured]</span>

<div class="defn"><b>GTF Advantage</b><p>The GTF Advantage is Pratt & Whitney's upgraded PW1100G, certified by EASA in 2026 for entry into service later in 2026. The mechanism that connects a grounded A320neo to the CFM56 aftermarket is replacement flying. Every PW1100G-powered A320neo on the ground needs its schedule flown by something else. ### 20.2 LEAP durability and the July 2026 kit

The LEAP-1A and -1B have had shorter time on wing than the CFM56 they replaced, particularly in hot and harsh environments such as the Middle East and India, driven by high-pressure turbine (HPT) blade and fuel-nozzle issues. <span class="cite">[ePlaneAI, two articles, dates not captured; D9 §2.5]</span> Part I §10.2 recorded operators reporting removals at 2,000 to 6,000 cycles with "a planning base of 4,000", against a mature CFM56's restoration interval of 10,000 to 15,000 cycles. <span class="cite">[Visual Approach, undated; Leeham News, 8 Mar 2024]</span> The HPT is the hottest rotating part of the core and the usual limiter of time on wing (Part I §5).

The fix is the Durability Improvement Package (DIP), which Part I §10.2 boxed as the LEAP durability kit: a new HPT stage-1 blade, a new HPT stage-1 nozzle and a forward inner nozzle support. On 18 July 2026 CFM announced certification of the LEAP-1B HPT durability kit by the US Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) and by EASA, stating that it "will double time on wing in hot and harsh environments", that industrialisation was beginning, and that full production cutover is expected in early 2027. <span class="cite">[CFM International / Safran / GE Aerospace release, 18 Jul 2026]</span> The same release notes initial engine-level certification of a second change for the LEAP-1B.

<div class="defn"><b>Reverse bleed system (RBS)</b><p>The reverse bleed system is a LEAP cooling arrangement that reduces the need for on-wing fuel-nozzle replacements, which CFM describes as "bringing on-wing maintenance needs in line with CFM56 standards". It received initial engine-level certification for the LEAP-1B alongside the HPT durability kit on 18 July 2026, and was already being deployed on the LEAP-1A. <span class="cite">[CFM International release, 18 Jul 2026; D9 §2.5]</span></p></div>

The LEAP-1A was ahead of the -1B. The new HPT blades and the reverse bleed fix were already being deployed on A320neo-family aircraft, with approximately one-third of the LEAP-1A fleet updated at the time of the report. <span class="cite">[ePlaneAI, date not captured]</span> By May 2026 GE Aerospace was stating that LEAPs "being shipped now will match the durability of the venerable CFM56". <span class="cite">[Leeham News, 27 May 2026]</span> Both the problem and the claimed fix are recorded; whether delivered LEAPs reach CFM56-level time on wing in service is not something the research could observe.

Scale and contract structure differ from the CFM56. About 5,500 LEAPs were in service at the date of one trade-press piece, with about 50% covered by long-term service agreements against about 15% of the CFM56 fleet. <span class="cite">[Aviation Week, undated]</span>

<div class="defn"><b>Long-term service agreement (LTSA)</b><p>A long-term service agreement is a multi-year contract under which the airline pays a fixed amount per engine flight hour and the manufacturer or shop commits to perform or pay for the shop visits, taking the volume and cost risk and controlling the workscope, the parts sourcing and the shop. The research's dossiers call it an LTSA, an LTA, a rate-per-flight-hour agreement or a flight-hour agreement; one concept, several names. It matters here because about half the LEAP fleet is under one and about 15% of the CFM56 fleet is, so a LEAP shop visit mostly flows to the manufacturer's network while a CFM56 visit is mostly bought transactionally. Part IV §28 carries the full treatment. <span class="cite">[D2 §2.3; D9 §2.5; registry C-12]</span></p></div>

CFM has outlined investments to support growth in LEAP shop visits, and Safran opened a new maintenance shop in Querétaro, Mexico, on 30 June 2026. <span class="cite">[CAPA (Centre for Aviation); Safran release, 30 Jun 2026]</span>

The mechanism for the CFM56 aftermarket is indirect, and that is the point of recording it. A LEAP that comes off wing early needs a shop visit in a LEAP-capable shop, mostly within the manufacturer's network under a long-term agreement, and a spare LEAP while it is away. It does not generate a CFM56 shop visit, a CFM56 module or any CFM56 used material. Its effect on the old fleet runs through the gate described in §19.1: an airline whose A320neo or 737 MAX fleet is less available than planned slows its A320ceo or 737NG retirements. Willis Lease's FY2025 10-K lists "shorter on-wing engine time of most new technology engines" alongside airframer and engine-maker delays as compounding factors in the tight market. <span class="cite">[Willis Lease Finance 10-K FY2025]</span>

The timing of the fix bears on the shape of the CFM56 forecast, and here the research holds two readings. Safran's earlier framing, from about 2023 or 2024, had CFM56 shop visits peaking at about 2,500 a year in 2025 to 2026. GE's 2026 framing has a plateau of 2,300 to 2,400 a year through 2028 with the apex around 2027 to 2028 and a gradual fade after. <span class="cite">[Safran via Aviation Week, c. 2023/24; GE via Aviation Week, 2026; reconciliation R-006, R-007]</span> The LEAP-1B kit's full cutover in early 2027 is the date from which, on CFM's own statement, new 737 MAX engines should stop coming off wing early, and so the date from which this indirect support for the 737NG fleet begins to fade. That date sits inside GE's plateau and after Safran's earlier peak. The primer records the two curves and the cutover date beside each other and does not say which curve the cutover favours; §23.1 presents both curves in full.

## 21. Airframer output: Boeing and Airbus

The second half of the replacement shortfall is airframes. Even where the new engines performed, there were fewer new aircraft to put them on than the airlines had ordered and planned for. This section gives the production and delivery figures for the two airframers with dates, and the arithmetic that connects a monthly rate to a year of undelivered replacements.

<div class="defn"><b>Production rate cap (737 MAX)</b><p>The production rate cap is the monthly ceiling on 737 output imposed by the FAA after the Alaska Airlines door-plug event of January 2024: 38 aircraft a month, raised to 42 in October 2025, with the review to move to 47 completed in May 2026. Boeing's 2018 peak rate was 52 a month. The cap sets the maximum number of 737NG replacements that can be built in a year, and so the minimum number of 737NGs that cannot yet be released. <span class="cite">[D9 §2.6; TravelPulse, Oct 2025; CAPA, May 2026]</span></p></div>

<div class="defn"><b>Backlog</b><p>Backlog is the count of firm orders not yet delivered. Airbus's stood at a record 8,754 aircraft at the end of December 2025, more than ten years of production at 2026 delivery rates; the 737 MAX backlog exceeds 4,800 aircraft. A large backlog means that each aircraft not delivered this year is still owed, so the replacement is deferred rather than cancelled. <span class="cite">[D9 §2.6; All About Industries; CAPA, May 2026; AirInsight]</span></p></div>

<div class="defn"><b>Glider</b><p>A glider is a completed airframe parked at the factory awaiting engines. Airbus had about 60 at the end of the first half of 2025 and 32 at the end of the third quarter, and its chief executive said the number would reach zero by year-end. Gliders are the visible form of an engine-limited production line: the airframe exists, the delivery does not. <span class="cite">[Leeham News, 30 Oct 2025; Air Data News; D9 §2.6]</span></p></div>

### 21.1 Boeing and the 737 MAX

After the January 2024 door-plug event the FAA capped 737 production at 38 aircraft a month. <span class="cite">[TravelPulse, Oct 2025]</span> The FAA raised the cap to 42 a month in October 2025. <span class="cite">[TravelPulse, Oct 2025]</span> In May 2026 Boeing's chief executive, Kelly Ortberg, said the company had completed what he called the "capstone" review with the FAA, the final review in the agency's sequence, to move from 42 to 47 a month, and that the stabilisation at 42 had not taken long. <span class="cite">[CAPA, May 2026]</span> Boeing's stated plan was a further step in September 2026 and another in late 2026, potentially reaching 52 to 53 a month by year-end; Ortberg said he remained cautious about the step to 52 because of inventory concerns. <span class="cite">[Travel Extra; AirInsight; CAPA, May 2026]</span>

Deliveries followed the cap with a lag. 737 MAX deliveries were 387 in 2023, 260 in 2024 and 440 in 2025, and the cumulative total reached 2,399 by the date of the compilation the research used. <span class="cite">[Wikipedia, 737 MAX orders and deliveries, 2026]</span> Boeing's total deliveries of all types were 348 in 2024; by August 2025 it had delivered 385 aircraft, already more than in all of 2024. <span class="cite">[Air Data News, Sep 2025]</span> The compilation notes that Boeing exceeded 38 deliveries in some months by delivering from stored inventory, which is why 2025 deliveries exceed a full year at the capped build rate. <span class="cite">[Wikipedia, 2026]</span>

<div class="worked"><div class="h">Worked example 3.4 — What a monthly rate cap means in 737NG replacements a year</div>
<p>Inputs (all sourced from §21.1): caps of 38, 42 and 47 a month and a 2018 peak of 52; MAX deliveries of 260 in 2024 and 440 in 2025. Assumption: every MAX delivered replaces one 737NG. That is the ceiling, since part of the backlog is growth rather than replacement (Cirium's Global Replacement Ratio of 46.1% in §19.2 says fewer than half of deliveries replace a retirement).</p>
<p>Step 1, a full year at each rate.</p>
<div class="eqblock">38 × 12 = 456      42 × 12 = 504      47 × 12 = 564      52 × 12 = 624</div>
<p>Step 2, the delivery shortfall against the capped rate.</p>
<div class="eqblock">2024: 456 − 260 = 196 aircraft short of a full year at 38 a month
2025: 440, roughly a full year at 38 plus deliveries from storage</div>
<p>Step 3, the gap between the 2018 rate and the cap, as a rough measure of the replacement that did not happen for production-cap reasons alone.</p>
<div class="eqblock">(52 − 38) × 12 = 168 aircraft a year, or 336 CFM56-7B engine positions not released</div>
<p>So the cap alone held back on the order of 170 737NG replacements a year in 2024 and 2025, before counting the 2019–20 grounding and the 2021–23 ramp. At the replacement ratio of 46.1%, the figure would be about 77 aircraft and 155 engines. Limits of the example: the one-for-one assumption is the ceiling; the replacement ratio is a global all-type figure, not a 737 figure; and the cap is lifting, so the figure shrinks each year. <span class="cite">[D9 §2.6, arithmetic on sourced figures; Wikipedia, 2026; CAPA, May 2026; Cirium via Informa]</span></p></div>

### 21.2 Airbus and the A320neo family

Airbus originally targeted 820 deliveries in 2025, later guided to about 790, and delivered 793. <span class="cite">[Argus Media; Air Data News; D9 T9]</span> Both the original and the revised targets are recorded, since the figure was revised during the year. It targets around 870 deliveries in 2026; in August 2026 it delivered 57 aircraft, described as leaving "a steep climb" to that target. <span class="cite">[Air Data News; Aerocorner, Sep 2026]</span> The engine shortfall behind the gliders came mainly from Pratt & Whitney, whose powder-metal production and inspection load competed with new-engine output, and earlier in 2026 from CFM, which fell behind before catching up. <span class="cite">[Air Data News]</span> Airbus was still negotiating Pratt & Whitney engine volumes for 2026 production in late 2025. <span class="cite">[Air Data News]</span>

The rate ambition moved. Airbus now expects A320neo-family output to reach 70 to 75 a month by the end of 2027 and to stabilise at 75 thereafter, instead of reaching that rate sooner as earlier plans had it. <span class="cite">[Air Data News; Mexico Business News]</span> The backlog was a record 8,754 aircraft at the end of December 2025. <span class="cite">[All About Industries]</span> The A320 family, ceo and neo combined, had 20,378 orders, 12,798 deliveries and 11,308 aircraft in service as of July 2026; the ceo/neo split of the in-service figure was not reached. <span class="cite">[Wikipedia, July 2026; D9 U1]</span>

### 21.3 What airframer output does to the old fleet

The mechanism is the gate of §19.1 again, seen from the factory. Each A320neo or 737 MAX not delivered in a year is an A320ceo or 737NG that its airline cannot release that year. The airline that waits for a late new aircraft still pays for the old one's maintenance in the meantime. So a delivery shortfall converts the airline's planned capital spending on a new aircraft into aftermarket operating spending on an old one, for as long as the delay lasts. Because the backlogs are owed rather than cancelled, the conversion is a deferral: when the rate rises, from 42 toward 52 at Boeing and toward 75 at Airbus, the deferred replacements arrive and the deferred retirements with them. That is why the translation table's rows for the two airframers (T-4 and T-5 in §24) carry a reversal in their third column. Air Lease's FY2025 10-K puts the lessor's view of the same mechanism in one sentence: "As global air traffic expands and aircraft production volumes remain constrained, lessors are experiencing strong demand for aircraft through new lease requests and lease extension requests." <span class="cite">[Air Lease Corp 10-K FY2025]</span>
