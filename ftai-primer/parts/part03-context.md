<div class="part-divider"><div class="pn">Part III</div><h1>The context: fleet, retirements, new engines, and the translation table</h1><p>This Part explains why the CFM56 aftermarket is where it is in 2026: how many engines there are, why far fewer of them retired than their makers planned, what went wrong with the engines meant to replace them, how few new aircraft the airframers delivered, and how lessors and airlines behaved as a result. It ends with a translation table that turns each of those facts into a specific requirement on the aftermarket. A reader who finishes it will be able to name the driver behind any shop-visit, used-part, lease or capital figure in Parts IV to XI.</p></div>

### Where this Part sits in the build

The primer is built in layers. Parts I and II are first principles: the engine as a machine, and the money that moves around it through leases, reserves and financing. Part III is the context shock. It takes the fleet those first principles describe and asks what happened to it between 2019 and 2026: which engines retired and which did not, why the replacement engines and airframes arrived late, and how owners behaved when they could not get new aircraft. None of that changes how a turbofan works or how a lease is priced. It changes the quantities: how many engines need a shop visit, how many are torn down, how many spares are needed and for how long, and what an extension costs. Section 24 turns each documented driver into a numbered row stating the requirement it creates and what it leaves untouched. Parts IV to XI, the segments, open by naming the rows they answer. This Part depends on Parts I and II for its terms and on nothing written later.

## 18. The fleet and the installed base

Every shop visit, every used part and every lease described in this primer is sold against an engine that already exists. So the first question is how many engines exist, which state each is in, and how hard it flies. This section defines demand and the states a fleet can be in, then sets out every count of the CFM56 fleet the research reached, with what each count covers. It closes with how mature that fleet is, meaning how many of its engines have never been to a shop.

<div class="defn"><b>Aftermarket demand</b><p>Aftermarket demand for an engine type is the sum of the work and assets needed to keep that type flying: shop visits, modules, spare engines, used serviceable material and leases of engines and aircraft. It is generated by the installed base multiplied by how hard that base flies, net of the engines that leave. Every row of the translation table in §24 names which of these requirements a driver raises. <span class="cite">[D9 §2.1, definition assembled in the research]</span></p></div>

Part I §2.2 boxed the installed base as the population of engines fitted to aircraft in the operating fleet. It noted that the published "in service" counts may include spare and stored engines, since no source states its basis. That reminder matters here. A spare engine in a warehouse and an engine on a parked aircraft generate no flight cycles and so no maintenance need. The firms that count fleets, Cirium (a data provider) and IBA (an appraiser that also publishes fleet data), sort aircraft into three states.

<div class="defn"><b>Fleet states: in service, parked (stored), retired (withdrawn)</b><p>An aircraft in service is assigned to an operator and flying, or in short-term maintenance. A parked or stored aircraft is not flying but has not been formally withdrawn; an aircraft parked for more than about 90 days usually counts as stored, and a stored aircraft can return to service or go on to retirement. A retired or withdrawn aircraft has formally left the operating fleet: it is scrapped whole, parted out so its engines and components can be sold separately (Part V §32), or converted to another use, most commonly a passenger-to-freighter (P2F) conversion (Part II §15). <span class="cite">[D9 §2.1]</span></p></div>

The states move in both directions except the last. Stored aircraft returned to service in volume after 2021. A retired aircraft does two opposite things to the aftermarket at once. It removes a future customer for shop visits, and it adds supply of used parts and of engines with life remaining. That double effect is why the retirement rate in §19 appears on both sides of the translation table.

<div class="defn"><b>Average age</b><p>The average age of a fleet is the mean number of years since delivery of its aircraft. Maintenance need rises with age in steps rather than smoothly: the first shop visit, the first life-limited-part replacement, the first heavy airframe check. The retirement decision is largely age-related cost set against what the aircraft is worth kept (§19.1). The average age of the world's commercial aircraft was about 15 years at December 2025, per the airline trade body IATA (the International Air Transport Association) as cited in trade press. <span class="cite">[D9 §2.1; IATA Global Outlook, Dec 2025, as cited by Aviation Business News]</span></p></div>

### 18.3 How mature the fleet is

A second way to describe a fleet is by how many shop visits its engines have had. The research used the phrase "fleet maturity" for this without defining it; here it means the share of the installed base by the number of shop visits endured, none, one, or two or more. It matters because an engine that has not yet had a first visit is a visit still to come, and because the first visit on a mature engine is usually the heaviest event of its life so far. Part I §6.2 explained that first runs are long, with the latest -5B and -7B variants often flown almost to their life-limited-part limit before the first removal.

## 19. Retirements: how the decision is made and what happened 2019–2026

Part I §2.3 boxed the retirement rate as the share of a fleet withdrawn in a year, and gave GE's falling planning assumption in one sentence. This section is the full treatment. It explains how an owner decides to retire an aircraft, sets out what actually retired between 2019 and 2025 with the two data providers' conflicting counts, follows GE's planning number down from 3–4% to "below 1%", and works the arithmetic of what one and three-quarter points of retirement rate mean in engines a year.

### 19.1 How the decision is made

An aircraft is retired by its owner, an airline or a lessor, when keeping it in service costs more than it is worth kept, and when the alternatives of selling it on, converting it to a freighter or parting it out are better. Five inputs enter that decision. <span class="cite">[D9 §2.3]</span>

The first input is age, and the way maintenance cost arrives in lumps rather than smoothly. An engine performance-restoration shop visit opens the core to restore exhaust-gas-temperature margin (Part I §5 and §6). A life-limited-part (LLP) replacement forces the engine open when a rotating part reaches its certified cycle limit (Part I §4). A heavy airframe check does the same to the airframe. Each is a multi-million-dollar event. Acumen Aviation's December 2025 note on the 737-700 states that "as the 737-700 fleet ages, heavy checks and major engine shop visits increasingly exceed the residual value of the aircraft, driving earlier retirements or conversions". <span class="cite">[Acumen Aviation, 18 Dec 2025]</span> A 2023 analysis found that aircraft become vulnerable to retirement at 16 to 18 or more years, particularly if the owner does not want to pay for the next heavy check or shop visit, and that the roughly 570 A320ceo-family aircraft then stored for 90 or more days had an average age of 17.1 years. <span class="cite">[Naveo, AFRA panel, 6 Jun 2023]</span>

<div class="defn"><b>Maintenance cliff</b><p>The maintenance cliff is the point at which the next heavy maintenance event, an engine shop visit, a life-limited-part replacement or a heavy airframe check, costs more than the aircraft is worth kept. An owner at the cliff retires, converts or sells the aircraft rather than pay for the event. Aircraft become vulnerable to it at about 16 to 18 years and older. Aviation Week's rule of thumb that a CFM56 needs a shop visit roughly every eight years makes 24 years a de facto retirement date, because a third full overhaul is "deemed too expensive". <span class="cite">[D9 §2.3; Naveo, 6 Jun 2023; Aviation Week, "Retirement Uptick Will Restock Used Engine Parts", date not captured]</span></p></div>

The second input is lease economics. At the end of an operating lease (Part II §12) the lessor chooses between extending with the current airline, moving the aircraft to a new lessee, or selling or parting it out. The airline, for its part, chooses between extending and returning. Both choices turn on whether a replacement aircraft is available, and between 2023 and 2026 it usually was not (§22).

<div class="defn"><b>Lease transition</b><p>A lease transition is the move of an aircraft from one lessee to the next. Between the two leases the lessor pays for reconfiguration of the cabin, for the maintenance needed to meet the next lessee's delivery condition, and for the downtime while the aircraft earns no rent. Transition is the alternative to extension (§22.3), and its cost is one reason a lessor extends when it can. <span class="cite">[D9 §2.3]</span></p></div>

The third input is freighter conversion, which Part II §15 owns. A passenger-to-freighter conversion gives a 20-plus-year-old airframe a second life. Cargo Facts reports narrowbody freighters, the 737 and 757, being converted at 21 to 22 years of age on average. <span class="cite">[Cargo Facts, date not captured]</span> More than 260 737-800 freighters were in service as of mid-2025 with a conversion pipeline exceeding 300, against fewer than 50 737-700 conversions. <span class="cite">[Acumen Aviation, 18 Dec 2025; Cargo Facts]</span> A conversion candidate still needs engines with life on them. So conversion is a source of demand for engines with green time (the remaining usable life on an engine before its next mandatory shop visit, Part II §16) or for shop visits, and not a source of teardown supply.

The fourth input is part-out value (Part V §32). When the value of an aircraft as a sum of parts exceeds its value flying, the owner parts it out. For a mid-life CFM56-powered narrowbody the engines are the dominant share of that sum, and Part V §32 works the numbers. The consultancy figures the research reached are that a run-out CFM56 is bought as feedstock for $0.8–1.2M, yields $1.6–2.2M of used material, and nets $0.4–0.8M before repair cost. <span class="cite">[Safe Fly Aviation, 2026, consultancy blog]</span> Rising used-part prices raise part-out value and so, at the margin, encourage retirement. That is a stabilising feedback. The research records that it was not strong enough to lift retirements in 2024 to 2026. <span class="cite">[D9 §2.3]</span>

The fifth input is replacement availability, and it governs the other four.

<div class="box"><div class="h">Replacement availability is the gate</div><p>None of the first four inputs triggers a retirement if the airline cannot get a replacement aircraft. An airline at the maintenance cliff with no new aircraft to fly pays for the shop visit anyway, because the alternative is to shrink. A lessor whose lessee cannot find a replacement extends the lease rather than take the aircraft back into a market with no buyer-operators. This is the single mechanism through which the new-engine and airframer problems of §20 and §21 act on the old fleet: they close the gate. Cirium Ascend's count that only about a dozen mid-life 737NGs and fewer than 25 A320ceos were available to rent anywhere in the world is the measure of how closed it was. <span class="cite">[D9 §2.3; Cirium Ascend via Bloomberg, 2023–24 context]</span></p></div>

The 2024 count is the tension. <div class="defn"><b>Global Replacement Ratio</b><p>The Global Replacement Ratio is the number of deliveries needed to replace retirements, expressed as a share of total deliveries over a period. Cirium put it at 46.1% for 2025 on 2015–2024 data: fewer than half of new deliveries were replacing a retired aircraft, and the rest were growth. Named programmes show the decision being made both ways at once. United, WestJet and Copa have started retiring 737NGs in favour of the 737 MAX 8. <span class="cite">[Acumen Aviation, 18 Dec 2025]</span> Wizz Air announced a gradual phase-out of its A321ceo fleet on 24 March 2026. <span class="cite">[Wizz Air release, 24 Mar 2026]</span> Against those, Lauda Europe, part of the Ryanair group, is extending A320 leases through the early 2030s because the transition to new aircraft proved too costly, and Lufthansa and Ryanair were reported holding onto older aircraft to fill the gap. <span class="cite">[Travel Extra, 2026; Bloomberg via AJOT]</span> IBA noted at the same time that delivery delays had led operators to maximise daily utilisation across all age groups, with mature passenger-narrowbody utilisation "rising significantly". <span class="cite">[IBA via AviTrader, 24 Sep 2024]</span> That utilisation effect has its own row in the translation table, because it adds cycles without adding engines.

### 19.4 Both sides of the ledger

The retirement rate is one number with two effects, and the translation table carries them as separate rows. Lower retirements mean more engines accruing cycles, so more shop visits, more life-limited-part replacements as retained engines reach their limits, and more full restorations in place of green-time run-outs; that is row T-1 in §24. Lower retirements also mean fewer teardowns, so less used material and fewer green-time engines, and higher prices for both; that is row T-9. The shop-visit research describes the second effect from the shop's side: "the supply of used serviceable material remains thin, leaving shops more reliant on newly manufactured core parts". <span class="cite">[AVM (Aviation Maintenance magazine), 2025, via D2 §2.4]</span> The used-material research describes it from the trader's side, with AerSale reporting a "hypercompetitive feedstock environment" and its whole-asset sales falling from 20 engines and one aircraft in 2024 to 13 engines in 2025. <span class="cite">[AerSale, 5 Mar 2026, as relayed in search results; D3 §2.6]</span> Part V §33 carries the supply side in full.

## 20. New-generation engines: GTF and LEAP durability, and what they did to the old fleet

The A320neo and the 737 MAX (the re-engined 737) were designed to replace the A320ceo and the 737NG. Two things slowed the replacement. The new engines spend less time on wing than planned, time on wing being the interval from an engine's installation to its first removal (Part I §5). And the airframers delivered fewer aircraft than planned (§21). Both keep older aircraft flying longer and harder. This section takes the engines. Part I §10.2 boxed the LEAP (CFM's new-generation engine, the LEAP-1A on the A320neo family and the LEAP-1B as the sole engine of the 737 MAX) and its durability kit; this section carries what each new engine's problem did to the old fleet, which Part I left to it.

Two new engines are involved, and they are not symmetric. The LEAP comes from the same manufacturer as the CFM56 and sits on both airframe families. The other comes from Pratt & Whitney and sits on one.

<div class="defn"><b>Geared turbofan (GTF)</b><p>The geared turbofan is Pratt & Whitney's new-generation engine. A reduction gearbox between the fan and the low-pressure turbine lets each turn at its own best speed, which the CFM56's direct-drive design does not allow. The PW1100G is one of two engine options on the Airbus A320neo family, the other being the LEAP-1A; the PW1500G and PW1900G are the sole engines of the Airbus A220 and the Embraer E2. Pratt & Whitney's parent is RTX. <span class="cite">[D9 §2.5]</span></p></div>

### 20.1 The Pratt & Whitney GTF powder-metal issue

<div class="defn"><b>GTF powder-metal issue</b><p>Certain turbine discs in the GTF are made by a powder-metallurgy route, in which metal powder is consolidated into a disc. Powder produced over a period of years was found to be contaminated. The affected discs must be inspected or replaced, which can be done only with the engine off the wing and opened. RTX disclosed the defect in July to September 2023, estimated that 600 to 700 engines would be pulled for inspection, took a charge of about $3 billion on 11 September 2023, and later estimates put the total programme cost at about $7 billion. <span class="cite">[D9 §2.5; BusinessDay, 11 Sep 2023; Mobility Outlook, date not captured]</span></p></div>

The planning figure and the outturn diverged. RTX's planning figure at the time of disclosure was that around 350 aircraft on average would be grounded across 2024 to 2026. <span class="cite">[The Flying Engineer, citing RTX, 2026]</span> Cirium data cited in trade press put 835 PW1100G-powered aircraft grounded or in storage globally as of late 2025. <span class="cite">[Cirium via The Flying Engineer, 2026]</span> The same source gives "720 out of 1,912 A320neo family aircraft, nearly four in ten, currently out of service due to engine issues"; the denominator is not defined in the material reached, and the research judged it most plausibly to be the Pratt & Whitney-powered A320neo sub-fleet rather than all A320neos. <span class="cite">[D9 T7, U5]</span> An aircraft unable to fly for lack of a serviceable part or engine is said to be on the ground, abbreviated AOG (aircraft on ground), a term Part II §14 carries because short-term engine leases exist to cover such events.

RTX's own framing at its full-year 2025 results on 27 January 2026 was that 2026 is "the third year of elevated AOG levels"; that AOG levels were down more than 20% from their 2025 peak; that PW1100 AOG was down 25% year to date, enabled by a 40% increase in maintenance output and a 23% reduction in turnaround time; that fourth-quarter 2025 GTF maintenance output was up 39% year on year; and that customer-compensation cash outflows were about $1 billion in 2025, about $700 million expected in 2026, and about $2.8 billion cumulative by the end of 2026. <span class="cite">[RTX FY2025 results via Leeham, 27 Jan 2026]</span> Trade commentary expects the issue to be fully worked through only in 2027 to 2028. <span class="cite">[The Flying Engineer, 2026]</span> Named effects include Turkish Airlines grounding more than 40 aircraft and Wizz Air signing a compensation agreement with Pratt & Whitney. <span class="cite">[Aviacionline; Shares Magazine; dates not captured]</span>

<div class="defn"><b>GTF Advantage</b><p>The GTF Advantage is Pratt & Whitney's upgraded PW1100G, certified by EASA in 2026 for entry into service later in 2026. The mechanism that connects a grounded A320neo to the CFM56 aftermarket is replacement flying. Every PW1100G-powered A320neo on the ground needs its schedule flown by something else. ### 20.2 LEAP durability and the July 2026 kit

The LEAP-1A and -1B have had shorter time on wing than the CFM56 they replaced, particularly in hot and harsh environments such as the Middle East and India, driven by high-pressure turbine (HPT) blade and fuel-nozzle issues. <span class="cite">[ePlaneAI, two articles, dates not captured; D9 §2.5]</span> Part I §10.2 recorded operators reporting removals at 2,000 to 6,000 cycles with "a planning base of 4,000", against a mature CFM56's restoration interval of 10,000 to 15,000 cycles. <span class="cite">[Visual Approach, undated; Leeham News, 8 Mar 2024]</span> The HPT is the hottest rotating part of the core and the usual limiter of time on wing (Part I §5).

The fix is the Durability Improvement Package (DIP), which Part I §10.2 boxed as the LEAP durability kit: a new HPT stage-1 blade, a new HPT stage-1 nozzle and a forward inner nozzle support. On 18 July 2026 CFM announced certification of the LEAP-1B HPT durability kit by the US Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) and by EASA, stating that it "will double time on wing in hot and harsh environments", that industrialisation was beginning, and that full production cutover is expected in early 2027. <span class="cite">[CFM International / Safran / GE Aerospace release, 18 Jul 2026]</span> The same release notes initial engine-level certification of a second change for the LEAP-1B.

<div class="defn"><b>Reverse bleed system (RBS)</b><p>The reverse bleed system is a LEAP cooling arrangement that reduces the need for on-wing fuel-nozzle replacements, which CFM describes as "bringing on-wing maintenance needs in line with CFM56 standards". It received initial engine-level certification for the LEAP-1B alongside the HPT durability kit on 18 July 2026, and was already being deployed on the LEAP-1A. <span class="cite">[CFM International release, 18 Jul 2026; D9 §2.5]</span></p></div>

The LEAP-1A was ahead of the -1B. The new HPT blades and the reverse bleed fix were already being deployed on A320neo-family aircraft, with approximately one-third of the LEAP-1A fleet updated at the time of the report. <span class="cite">[ePlaneAI, date not captured]</span> By May 2026 GE Aerospace was stating that LEAPs "being shipped now will match the durability of the venerable CFM56". <span class="cite">[Leeham News, 27 May 2026]</span> Both the problem and the claimed fix are recorded; whether delivered LEAPs reach CFM56-level time on wing in service is not something the research could observe.

Scale and contract structure differ from the CFM56. About 5,500 LEAPs were in service at the date of one trade-press piece, with about 50% covered by long-term service agreements against about 15% of the CFM56 fleet. <span class="cite">[Aviation Week, undated]</span>

<div class="defn"><b>Long-term service agreement (LTSA)</b><p>A long-term service agreement is a multi-year contract under which the airline pays a fixed amount per engine flight hour and the manufacturer or shop commits to perform or pay for the shop visits, taking the volume and cost risk and controlling the workscope, the parts sourcing and the shop. The research's dossiers call it an LTSA, an LTA, a rate-per-flight-hour agreement or a flight-hour agreement; one concept, several names. It matters here because about half the LEAP fleet is under one and about 15% of the CFM56 fleet is, so a LEAP shop visit mostly flows to the manufacturer's network while a CFM56 visit is mostly bought transactionally. Part IV §28 carries the full treatment. <span class="cite">[D2 §2.3; D9 §2.5; registry C-12]</span></p></div>

CFM has outlined investments to support growth in LEAP shop visits, and Safran opened a new maintenance shop in Querétaro, Mexico, on 30 June 2026. <span class="cite">[CAPA (Centre for Aviation); Safran release, 30 Jun 2026]</span>

The mechanism for the CFM56 aftermarket is indirect, and that is the point of recording it. A LEAP that comes off wing early needs a shop visit in a LEAP-capable shop, mostly within the manufacturer's network under a long-term agreement, and a spare LEAP while it is away. It does not generate a CFM56 shop visit, a CFM56 module or any CFM56 used material. Its effect on the old fleet runs through the gate described in §19.1: an airline whose A320neo or 737 MAX fleet is less available than planned slows its A320ceo or 737NG retirements. Willis Lease's FY2025 10-K lists "shorter on-wing engine time of most new technology engines" alongside airframer and engine-maker delays as compounding factors in the tight market. <span class="cite">[Willis Lease Finance 10-K FY2025]</span>

The timing of the fix bears on the shape of the CFM56 forecast, and here the research holds two readings. Safran's earlier framing, from about 2023 or 2024, had CFM56 shop visits peaking at about 2,500 a year in 2025 to 2026. GE's 2026 framing has a plateau of 2,300 to 2,400 a year through 2028 with the apex around 2027 to 2028 and a gradual fade after. <span class="cite">[Safran via Aviation Week, c. 2023/24; GE via Aviation Week, 2026; reconciliation R-006, R-007]</span> The LEAP-1B kit's full cutover in early 2027 is the date from which, on CFM's own statement, new 737 MAX engines should stop coming off wing early, and so the date from which this indirect support for the 737NG fleet begins to fade. That date sits inside GE's plateau and after Safran's earlier peak. The primer records the two curves and the cutover date beside each other and does not say which curve the cutover favours; §23.1 presents both curves in full.

## 21. Airframer output: Boeing and Airbus

The second half of the replacement shortfall is airframes. Even where the new engines performed, there were fewer new aircraft to put them on than the airlines had ordered and planned for. This section gives the production and delivery figures for the two airframers with dates, and the arithmetic that connects a monthly rate to a year of undelivered replacements.

<div class="defn"><b>Production rate cap (737 MAX)</b><p>The production rate cap is the monthly ceiling on 737 output imposed by the FAA after the Alaska Airlines door-plug event of January 2024: 38 aircraft a month, raised to 42 in October 2025, with the review to move to 47 completed in May 2026. Boeing's 2018 peak rate was 52 a month. The cap sets the maximum number of 737NG replacements that can be built in a year, and so the minimum number of 737NGs that cannot yet be released. <span class="cite">[D9 §2.6; TravelPulse, Oct 2025; CAPA, May 2026]</span></p></div>

<div class="defn"><b>Backlog</b><p>Backlog is the count of firm orders not yet delivered. Airbus's stood at a record 8,754 aircraft at the end of December 2025, more than ten years of production at 2026 delivery rates; the 737 MAX backlog exceeds 4,800 aircraft. A large backlog means that each aircraft not delivered this year is still owed, so the replacement is deferred rather than cancelled. <span class="cite">[D9 §2.6; All About Industries; CAPA, May 2026; AirInsight]</span></p></div>

<div class="defn"><b>Glider</b><p>A glider is a completed airframe parked at the factory awaiting engines. Airbus had about 60 at the end of the first half of 2025 and 32 at the end of the third quarter, and its chief executive said the number would reach zero by year-end. Gliders are the visible form of an engine-limited production line: the airframe exists, the delivery does not. <span class="cite">[Leeham News, 30 Oct 2025; Air Data News; D9 §2.6]</span></p></div>

### 21.1 Boeing and the 737 MAX

After the January 2024 door-plug event the FAA capped 737 production at 38 aircraft a month. <span class="cite">[TravelPulse, Oct 2025]</span> The FAA raised the cap to 42 a month in October 2025. <span class="cite">[TravelPulse, Oct 2025]</span> In May 2026 Boeing's chief executive, Kelly Ortberg, said the company had completed what he called the "capstone" review with the FAA, the final review in the agency's sequence, to move from 42 to 47 a month, and that the stabilisation at 42 had not taken long. <span class="cite">[CAPA, May 2026]</span> Boeing's stated plan was a further step in September 2026 and another in late 2026, potentially reaching 52 to 53 a month by year-end; Ortberg said he remained cautious about the step to 52 because of inventory concerns. <span class="cite">[Travel Extra; AirInsight; CAPA, May 2026]</span>

Deliveries followed the cap with a lag. 737 MAX deliveries were 387 in 2023, 260 in 2024 and 440 in 2025, and the cumulative total reached 2,399 by the date of the compilation the research used. <span class="cite">[Wikipedia, 737 MAX orders and deliveries, 2026]</span> Boeing's total deliveries of all types were 348 in 2024; by August 2025 it had delivered 385 aircraft, already more than in all of 2024. <span class="cite">[Air Data News, Sep 2025]</span> The compilation notes that Boeing exceeded 38 deliveries in some months by delivering from stored inventory, which is why 2025 deliveries exceed a full year at the capped build rate. <span class="cite">[Wikipedia, 2026]</span>

### 21.3 What airframer output does to the old fleet

The mechanism is the gate of §19.1 again, seen from the factory. Each A320neo or 737 MAX not delivered in a year is an A320ceo or 737NG that its airline cannot release that year. The airline that waits for a late new aircraft still pays for the old one's maintenance in the meantime. So a delivery shortfall converts the airline's planned capital spending on a new aircraft into aftermarket operating spending on an old one, for as long as the delay lasts. Because the backlogs are owed rather than cancelled, the conversion is a deferral: when the rate rises, from 42 toward 52 at Boeing and toward 75 at Airbus, the deferred replacements arrive and the deferred retirements with them. That is why the translation table's rows for the two airframers (T-4 and T-5 in §24) carry a reversal in their third column. Air Lease's FY2025 10-K puts the lessor's view of the same mechanism in one sentence: "As global air traffic expands and aircraft production volumes remain constrained, lessors are experiencing strong demand for aircraft through new lease requests and lease extension requests." <span class="cite">[Air Lease Corp 10-K FY2025]</span>

## 22. Lessors and airlines: extensions, availability, lease rates

The owners of the old fleet responded to the closed gate in ways that show up directly in aftermarket demand. This section takes the airline's maintenance decision at fleet level, then the decision to extend or return a leased aircraft, then what the lessors did between 2023 and 2026 and what it did to lease rates. Part II owns the operating lease (§12), maintenance reserves and return conditions (§13), engine leasing (§14) and green time (§16); this section uses those terms and adds the context.

### 22.1 The airline's maintenance decision at fleet level

An airline's engineering and fleet-planning functions do not wait for engines to fail. They run a rolling forecast of when each engine will have to come off.

<div class="defn"><b>Engine removal forecast</b><p>The engine removal forecast is an airline's rolling schedule of each installed engine's projected date of removal for a shop visit. Each engine's date is set by whichever of its limits is reached first: remaining exhaust-gas-temperature margin (Part I §5), remaining life-limited-part cycles (Part I §4), or a hard inspection. From the forecast the airline books shop slots, in 2025 and 2026 some 12 to 18 months ahead, because the wait for a slot runs two to six months. Part IV §27 owns the slot and induction. <span class="cite">[D9 §2.7; Air Cargo Week, 2026; Locatory, Mar 2026]</span></p></div>

Which of the three clocks runs out first is itself contested in the research, and Part I §5.2 carries the tension. Older trade-press analyses found exhaust-gas-temperature margin erosion "generally the most influential factor" in short-haul removal intervals; later analyses of the latest -5B and -7B variants found margin lasting to the life-limited-part limit, so that removals are LLP-driven, with "especially first-run engines" flown almost to the limit. <span class="cite">[Aircraft Commerce, 2003 and 2008; Aircraft Commerce Issue 50; FlightGlobal; reconciliation R-011]</span> The demand research lists the three clocks without ranking them, and so does this Part.

For each removal the airline chooses a workscope (Part I §6), meaning how much of the engine to open and restore. The choice depends on how long the airline expects to keep the aircraft. If the aircraft will be retired or returned to its lessor within a few years, the airline avoids a full performance restoration and buys green time instead: it installs an engine or module with enough margin and LLP life to reach the retirement date, then returns or sells it at or near the end of its life. The green-time engine comes from the used-material market or from an engine lessor. Trade press describes airlines "replacing LLP-expired modules with green-time units to maintain engines on wing while awaiting new aircraft or engine deliveries", through quick-turn or hospital shop visits that do only the essential work (Part I §6; Part VII §46). <span class="cite">[Air Cargo Week, 2026; Aviation Business News]</span> If the aircraft will stay for a long time, the airline does the full restoration and replaces the LLPs so the engine runs another full interval.

Green time is a finite pool, and the research records its limit. Bain's 2024 brief on the engine-maintenance capacity crunch notes that at 80% fleet utilisation green-time capacity becomes tight, and that "there is no huge buffer if airlines rely on green time and are getting to 80%". <span class="cite">[Bain, 2024]</span> The pool is fed by retirements. When retirements are low the pool shrinks exactly when demand for it is highest, which is the mechanism behind row T-9 of the translation table.

Spare engines are the other quantity the airline manages (Part II §14). An airline carries spares to cover removals; when shop turnaround lengthens, the same removal rate ties up more spares, and the airline buys or leases more. Two filings give the scale at airline level. Spirit Aviation Holdings had 16 spare engines financed under operating leases and owned 18 at 31 December 2025. <span class="cite">[Spirit Aviation Holdings 10-K FY2025]</span> American Airlines had agreements for 47 spare engines to be delivered in 2026 and beyond. <span class="cite">[American Airlines 10-K FY2025]</span>

### 22.2 The extend-or-return decision

When a lease expires the airline weighs three things. The first is the extension rent the lessor asks. The second is the cost of meeting return conditions (Part II §13), the contractual minimum remaining life on engines, LLPs and airframe checks at redelivery, which can force a shop visit just before return. The third is the availability and price of a replacement. <span class="cite">[D9 §2.7]</span> Between 2023 and 2026 the third has usually been decisive. With new aircraft late and used aircraft scarce, extending has been the only way to keep capacity. So airlines extend and perform the maintenance the extended term needs, rather than returning aircraft at the end of their useful life and leaving the shop visit to the next owner. The lessor, facing the same scarcity from the other side, prefers extension to a transition it would have to pay for (§19.1).

### 22.3 How lessor behaviour changed, 2023–2026

<div class="defn"><b>Lease extension and the extension share</b><p>A lease extension renews an expiring operating lease with the incumbent airline instead of taking the aircraft back for transition, sale or part-out. The extension share is the proportion of scheduled lease expiries that are extended rather than returned. Cirium Ascend's head of consultancy, Rob Morris, said 75 to 90% of scheduled expirations were being extended; IBA found extension terms on legacy single-aisle aircraft stretching to four to six years, with extension rents in some cases more than 40% above the age-specific rates agreed just before the spike. Each extension keeps an aircraft and its two engines out of the used market and inside the maintenance cycle for the term. <span class="cite">[Cirium Ascend via Bloomberg/AJOT, 2023–24 context; IBA via Key.Aero and IBA Weekly, Nov 2023; D9 §2.8]</span></p></div>

The availability figures measure how far the market tightened. Cirium Ascend estimated that only about a dozen mid-life 737NGs and fewer than 25 A320ceos were available to rent anywhere in the world at the time of its count, and that lease rates on ten-year-old 737-800s were 44% above January 2022 levels. <span class="cite">[Cirium Ascend via Bloomberg/AJOT; date not visible, context 2023–24]</span> IBA's research found lease lengths being extended, terms growing to four to six years, extension deals running out to 2026 and beyond, and in extreme cases aircraft due back at year 12 not expected to return until 2032, at 18 years of age. <span class="cite">[IBA via Key.Aero; IBA Weekly, 24 Nov 2023]</span>

The lessors' own filings describe the same market. Air Lease's FY2025 10-K: "The strong demand for aircraft, combined with elevated interest rates and inflation, helped increase lease rates on new lease agreements and lease extensions during 2025. Lease rates are expected to remain strong as the supply and demand environment for commercial aircraft remains tight." <span class="cite">[Air Lease Corp 10-K FY2025]</span> Willis Lease's FY2025 10-K: "Both Airbus and Boeing have delivery delays which have been compounded by engine manufacturer delays, shorter on-wing engine time of most new technology engines, and ongoing supply chain challenges." <span class="cite">[Willis Lease Finance 10-K FY2025]</span>

<div class="worked"><div class="h">Worked example 3.5 — A 100-aircraft lease cohort, and what an extension does to the maintenance calendar</div>
<p>Inputs. Cohort: 100 A320ceo leases expiring in one year (assumed, for scale). Extension share: 75–90% (sourced: Cirium Ascend). Extension term: 4–6 years (sourced: IBA, Nov 2023). Utilisation: 1,000–1,500 cycles a year per engine (sourced: Aircraft Value News). Mature restoration interval: 10,000–15,000 cycles (sourced: Leeham, Mar 2024). Stock available to rent: fewer than 25 A320ceos worldwide (sourced: Cirium Ascend).</p>
<p>Step 1, the aircraft that come back.</p>
<div class="eqblock">returns: 100 − 75 = 25 aircraft at a 75% share; 100 − 90 = 10 aircraft at 90%
engines returned with them: 20 to 50</div>
<p>Those 10 to 25 aircraft are the entire flow into the used market from that cohort for transition, sale or part-out. Step 2, the stock against the flow. Cirium's count of fewer than 25 A320ceos available to rent worldwide is of the same order as one year's returns from a single 100-aircraft cohort. That is the arithmetic behind a 40%-plus move in used rents: the stock was one cohort's returns deep (D9 §2.8).</p>
<p>Step 3, cycles added inside the extension.</p>
<div class="eqblock">4 years × 1,000 = 4,000 cycles, to 6 years × 1,500 = 9,000 cycles per engine</div>
<p>Step 4, whether the next shop visit falls inside the term. Take an engine at expiry with 3,000 cycles of life remaining to its next restoration (assumed). Any extension in the range forces that restoration inside the term, and the incumbent airline pays for it rather than leaving it to redelivery. Take instead an engine with 10,000 cycles remaining (assumed). A four-year extension at 1,000 cycles a year, 4,000 cycles, avoids the visit; a six-year extension at 1,500, 9,000 cycles, nearly reaches it.</p>
<div class="eqblock">engines whose next event now sits inside an extended term: 75 to 90 aircraft × 2 = 150 to 180 engines</div>
<p>So in a 100-aircraft cohort, extension moves the next maintenance event of 150 to 180 engines from a future owner's calendar into the incumbent's, and holds 20 to 50 engines back from the used and teardown market. Limits of the example: the cohort size is assumed; the remaining life at expiry is assumed and in practice is spread across the cohort; return conditions (Part II §13) may already require the visit at redelivery, in which case the extension changes who pays and when, not whether; and the V2500's interval is applied from the CFM56's. <span class="cite">[Cirium Ascend via Bloomberg/AJOT; IBA via Key.Aero, Nov 2023; Aircraft Value News, undated; Leeham News, 8 Mar 2024; D9 §2.8]</span></p></div>

### 22.4 FTAI's position in this chain

FTAI's FY2025 10-K describes the 2025 Partnership, the first vehicle of its Strategic Capital Initiative (Part VIII §60 to §62), as focused on acquiring 737NG and A320ceo aircraft and as the primary buyer of all future on-lease narrowbody aircraft. <span class="cite">[FTAI Aviation 10-K FY2025]</span> The lessor behaviour described above cuts two ways for a buyer of mid-life narrowbodies, and the research records both without weighing them. ## 23. Forecasts: shop-visit curves and the engine MRO market

The quantities in §18 to §22 feed forecasts. This section sets out every forecast curve the research reached, the measure each uses, and its date, and then reconciles the headline shop-visit count against the installed base. Part IV §26 carries the market's reading of the same curve as "shop-visit demand"; the two sections cross-reference and do not duplicate.

<div class="defn"><b>Shop-visit forecast and first shop visit</b><p>A shop-visit forecast counts the expected engine inductions per year for a type. A first shop visit is the first removal of an engine for shop maintenance since new; first runs are usually the longest, so a fleet's first-visit peak comes years after its delivery peak. The two measures can peak in different years: GE and Safran forecast total CFM56 visits of about 2,300 to 2,400 a year in 2026 to 2028, while an independent research house dates the peak in first visits for the CFM56 and V2500 to 2027. <span class="cite">[D9 §2.9; GE via Aviation Week, 2026; Visual Approach Analytics]</span></p></div>

<div class="defn"><b>MRO market and engine MRO</b><p>MRO is maintenance, repair and overhaul, the generic term for aircraft and engine upkeep (Part IV §25). The MRO market is the dollar value of that spending in a year, and engine MRO is the engine share of it. Oliver Wyman put engine MRO at $70 billion in 2026, rising to $102.6 billion by 2036. Aviation Week counts the same market in events: about 9,000 engine shop visits of all types in 2025, about 2,300 of them CFM56. <span class="cite">[Oliver Wyman via AviationPros and Air Cargo Week, 2026; Aviation Week 2026 Fleet & MRO Forecast via ePlaneAI; D9 §2.9]</span></p></div>

<div class="defn"><b>Unconditional MRO demand</b><p>Aviation Week's 2026 forecast gives "unconditional MRO demand of $853bn over ten years". The forecast's own definition was not retrieved. Read from first principles, unconditional demand is the maintenance the fleet would require if every scheduled event were performed when due, before any constraint of shop capacity, parts or labour is applied; it is the quantity Bain's capacity model is compared against. The primer uses the phrase only in that sense and flags that the publisher's definition is unverified. <span class="cite">[Aviation Week 2026 Fleet & MRO Forecast via ePlaneAI; registry section 2]</span></p></div>

### 23.4 Capacity, turnaround and stands

Part IV §27 owns capacity. Four of its figures are needed here because the translation table's row T-8 depends on them, and because the research holds two readings of whether the constraint is easing. Trade press puts a full CFM56 overhaul at 90 to 120 days against about 60 before the pandemic, light work at about 45 days, legacy-engine turnaround times up 35% and new-generation times up more than 150% against five years earlier, and slot waits at two to six months. <span class="cite">[Air Cargo Week, 2026; Aviation Business News / AVM, 2025]</span> GE's own filing and conference material read the other way: turnaround for the LEAP, CFM56 and GE90 improved by more than 10% year on year in the fourth quarter of 2025, material input from priority suppliers rose more than 40%, and CFM56 turnaround was "sustaining about 90 days" in May 2026. <span class="cite">[GE 10-K FY2025; GE Aerospace, Bernstein conference, 27 May 2026; reconciliation R-019, R-020]</span> Oliver Wyman's April 2026 survey has two-thirds of MRO operators unable to find enough technicians, labour-rate inflation at 5.5 to 6.0%, and more than half expecting attrition to worsen. <span class="cite">[Oliver Wyman, Apr 2026]</span> A filing and a conference deck against trade press and a consultancy survey; the primer carries both, and Part IV §27 works them through.

<div class="defn"><b>Engine stand</b><p>An engine stand is the wheeled fixture that holds an engine or a module during work, storage and transport. Because an engine off the wing must sit on one, stands are a physical limit on work in progress, and their utilisation is a measure of how full the system is: legacy-engine stand use rose by a third in 2025 over 2024, and CFM56-5A/-5B stand use rose 60%. Part IV §27 carries the full treatment. <span class="cite">[AviationPros, Engine Stand Utilization Report; D2 §2.4; D9 §6]</span></p></div>

Third-party test and repair waits of four to six months, and advice to buyers to plan procurement early for LLPs and critical rotables and to use exchange pools, used material and diversified suppliers, complete the picture the trade press gives. <span class="cite">[Locatory, Mar 2026; AVM, 2025]</span> Aero Norway, an independent CFM56 shop, said the long lead times for induction slots were driving interest in quick-turn services, "especially in the case of older-generation engines, such as the CFM56 and V2500". <span class="cite">[Aero Norway in AVM, May 2024]</span>

<div class="worked"><div class="h">Worked example 3.6 — Reconciling 2,300 shop visits a year with about 24,000 engines</div>
<p>Inputs. Visits: 2,300 a year, the low end of GE's 2,300–2,400 (sourced: GE via Aviation Week, 2026). Bases (sourced, Exhibit 3.1): about 24,000 (trade press, Sept 2025); more than 22,800 (Safran, end-2025); more than 19,000 "flying" and about 14,200 "active" (consultancy blog, labelled). Interval: a mature performance restoration every 10,000–15,000 cycles (sourced: Leeham, Mar 2024) at 1,000–1,500 cycles a year (sourced: Aircraft Value News), which is 6.7 to 15 years with the central cases, 10,000 at 1,000 to 15,000 at 1,500, at 10 years; an alternative rule of thumb of roughly every eight years (sourced: Aviation Week). Spare ratio: about 10% of installed engines now, from about 15% historically (sourced: MTU AEROREPORT, via Part II's research).</p>
<p>Step 1, the visit rate and the implied interval on each base.</p>
<div class="eqblock">on 24,000: 2,300 ÷ 24,000 = 9.6% a year → 1 ÷ 0.096 = 10.4 years between visits
on 22,800: 2,300 ÷ 22,800 = 10.1% → 9.9 years
on 19,000: 2,300 ÷ 19,000 = 12.1% → 8.3 years
on 14,200: 2,300 ÷ 14,200 = 16.2% → 6.2 years</div>
<p>Step 2, the same at the top of GE's range.</p>
<div class="eqblock">2,400 ÷ 24,000 = 10.0% → 10.0 years;  2,400 ÷ 14,200 = 16.9% → 5.9 years</div>
<p>Step 3, removing spares from the manufacturer-level base. If about 10% of the 24,000 are spare engines that fly only when installed, the flying base is about 21,600.</p>
<div class="eqblock">2,300 ÷ 21,600 = 10.6% → 9.4 years</div>
<p>Step 4, comparison with the sourced intervals. An implied interval of about ten years on the 24,000 base matches the mature restoration interval only at the long end of its range, 10,000 cycles at 1,000 a year or 15,000 at 1,500. An interval of 8.3 years on the 19,000 base matches Aviation Week's eight-year rule of thumb. An interval of about six years on the 14,200 base is shorter than any sourced CFM56 interval unless engines fly more than about 1,600 cycles a year (10,000 ÷ 6.2 = 1,613), which is above the sourced utilisation range.</p>
<p>Step 5, the maturity effect. Worked example 3.1 showed about 70% of the fleet with at most one visit in 2024. First runs are longer than mature runs, with the latest variants often flown almost to the LLP limit of 20,000 cycles or more before the first removal (Part I §6.2). A fleet in which most engines are still on a long first run generates fewer visits a year than the mature interval alone would predict, which pushes the implied interval on any base upward. First-run intervals by thrust rating were not retrieved (Part I's unknown U-D1-06).</p>
<p>So the 24,000 base is consistent with the sourced mature interval only if it includes a few thousand spare, stored or green-time engines, or if the long first runs of a still-maturing fleet are depressing the visit rate, or both. The 14,200 base is consistent only with an interval shorter than any the research sourced. The research recorded this and did not resolve it; which base is right is the single most consequential unknown in this Part, because every per-engine figure in Parts IV to IX divides by it. Limits of the example: the visit count and the base come from different publishers and may define "CFM56" differently (all variants or -5B and -7B only); the 10% spare ratio is an industry generality; and the arithmetic assumes every engine is on a regular cycle, which green-time engines with no further visit planned are not. <span class="cite">[GE via Aviation Week, 2026; Aviation Business News, Sept 2025; Safran, 13 Feb 2026; Safe Fly Aviation, 2026; Leeham News, 8 Mar 2024; Aircraft Value News, undated; Aviation Week, "Retirement Uptick Will Restock Used Engine Parts"; MTU AEROREPORT; D9 T1; reconciliation R-001, R-002, R-008]</span></p></div>

The paragraphs below explain the mechanism in each row, in the same order.

**T-1, low CFM56 retirements.** The retirement rate drives both sides of the market at once (§19.4). On the demand side, an engine not retired keeps flying and keeps accruing cycles, so it reaches its next performance restoration and, eventually, its LLP limits. Worked example 3.2 put the gap between the planned 3–4% and the realised 1.5–2% at about 400 engines a year on any manufacturer-level base, and about 1,200 engines over 2024 to 2026. Those engines are why GE and Safran's 2,300–2,400 visits a year hold through 2028 rather than fading from 2026. An engine kept past its planned retirement also changes workscope: an owner who had planned a green-time run-out to retirement now needs a full restoration and a new or used LLP set to run another interval, which is Part IV §30's four-option problem. On the supply side the same engine is one not torn down, so the row's third column hands over to T-9. The row does not touch the new engines' spare pools and does not add a single shop slot.

**T-2, GTF powder-metal groundings.** Every Pratt & Whitney-powered A320neo on the ground needs its flying replaced, and the practical replacement is an A320ceo on the same type rating (§20.1). The ceo that replaces it flies cycles it would not otherwise have flown. Worked example 3.3 put the ceo-engine visits brought forward at 47 to 250 a year depending on whether the grounded count is RTX's planning average of 350 or Cirium's late-2025 point count of 835. Those visits fall on the V2500 and the CFM56-5B in proportion to the ceo fleet's engine mix, which is unknown (§18.2). The row also raises ceo lease extensions, short-term ceo leases and spare -5B and V2500 engines, and it creates demand for green-time -5B and V2500 modules from airlines that had planned to retire ceos and now must keep them through one more interval. It does not touch the -7B except through general narrowbody scarcity, and because it suppresses ceo retirements it removes ceo teardown feedstock rather than adding it.

**T-3, LEAP durability and the July 2026 kit.** A LEAP that comes off wing at 4,000 cycles instead of 10,000 generates a LEAP shop visit and needs a LEAP spare, and about half the LEAP fleet is under a long-term agreement, so most of that work flows to the manufacturer's network (§20.2). None of it is CFM56 work. The row's effect on the CFM56 aftermarket is entirely indirect: an operator whose A320neo or 737 MAX fleet is less available than planned keeps its ceo or 737NG flying, which Willis Lease's filing names as a compounding factor. The LEAP-1B kit's certification on 18 July 2026 and its production cutover in early 2027 are the dates at which, on CFM's statement, new MAX engines stop coming off early. From then the indirect support for the 737NG fades, which is why the row's third column carries its own reversal. The LEAP-1A was about a third upgraded at the research date, so the A320ceo side of this row was already fading.

**T-4, the 737 MAX production rate cap.** Worked example 3.4 showed that the cap alone held back on the order of 170 737NG replacements a year in 2024 and 2025 at a one-for-one replacement ceiling, or about 77 at Cirium's replacement ratio. Each 737NG not replaced is extended, flown hard and maintained: CFM56-7B shop visits, LLP replacements, spare -7B engines, and green-time -7B modules for carriers deferring retirement. This row is the -7B's counterpart to T-2 and T-5, which are -5B and V2500 rows, and the three must not be fused. The cap is lifting, from 42 toward 47 and 52, and the backlog of more than 4,800 is owed rather than cancelled, so the row reverses: more NG retirements, more -7B teardown supply, and fewer -7B visits later in the decade. United, WestJet and Copa have already begun the retirements that the reversal implies.

**T-5, the Airbus delivery shortfall.** Airbus delivered 793 aircraft in 2025 against an original target of 820 and now expects A320neo-family rate 75 only by the end of 2027 (§21.2). Each undelivered neo is a ceo its airline cannot release, so the row raises A320ceo extensions, ceo utilisation, -5B and V2500 shop visits and ceo spare engines, and it keeps ceo part-out low. The airline that waits for a late neo still pays for ceo maintenance in the meantime, which is the conversion of planned capital spending into aftermarket operating spending described in §21.3. The row does not stress the -7B. Like T-4 it reverses as the rate rises, because the 8,754-aircraft backlog is a deferral of replacement, not a cancellation.

**T-6, lessor extension behaviour.** When 75 to 90% of expiries are extended, the maintenance events that would have fallen at a lease transition fall inside the extended term instead, and the incumbent airline pays for them (worked example 3.5). The row therefore raises shop visits and LLP replacements inside extended terms, raises lease rates on mid-life aircraft and engines, and holds lessors' capital in aircraft that stay on lease rather than being sold. Its third column is the one that matters for the mid-life buyer and the teardown trader: only 10 to 25 aircraft of every 100 come back, so the flow of acquisition candidates and teardown feedstock shrinks to that trickle. The row does not change the total number of cycles the fleet flies; it changes who pays for the maintenance those cycles cause and when. The direction of mid-life rents is contested between a new-aircraft lessor's filing and an appraiser's commentary (R-031), and the row carries that tension.

**T-7, OEM parts-price escalation.** Part I §4 and Part IV §28 own this driver; it is in the table because it acts on every row above it. New spare parts rose about 10% a year in 2022 and 2023, with a double-digit increase on 1 November 2022 and a high-single-digit increase in August 2023, and Safran's stated rule is pricing "above inflation by 3 to 4 points ... at least for some years"; the -7B LLP set compounded about 7% a year from 2008 to 2025 on the figures Part I assembled. <span class="cite">[Aviation Week, late 2023; D1 example C]</span> Because 60 to 70% of a shop visit's bill is material priced off the manufacturer's catalogue, escalation flows into every visit whoever performs it (Part I §7). The requirement it creates is for substitutes on the material line: used serviceable material (Part V), approved alternative parts (Part VI), repair instead of replacement, and module exchange instead of overhaul (Part VII). It does not change how many engines come off wing, it does not stress labour or slots, and it leaves the LLP line unsubstituted, since no PMA exists for a CFM56 life-limited part. <span class="cite">[D4 U7]</span>

**T-8, MRO capacity shortfall and turnaround.** A slot wait of two to six months plus a turnaround of 90 to 120 days means an engine removed today returns in five to ten months, and for that whole period a spare must fly in its place (§23.4). So the first requirement this row creates is spare engines and spare-engine leases, in proportion to the delay rather than to the removal count. The second is substitution of form: a module exchange or a quick-turn visit returns the aircraft to service in weeks rather than months, so the shortage of full-overhaul slots is itself demand for exchanges, exchange pools and the inventory of serviceable modules that an exchange requires, which is capital. The row does not change the number of removals the fleet generates and does not add a single teardown. GE's filing reports turnaround improving and material input up more than 40%, against trade press and a consultancy survey that report the constraint persisting (R-019, R-020); the row carries both.

**T-9, used-material and feedstock scarcity.** This row is a consequence of T-1, T-4, T-5 and T-6, recorded as its own row because it creates its own requirements and because Part V answers it directly. Fewer retirements mean fewer teardowns; fewer teardowns mean less used material and fewer green-time engines; and the shops that would have used them buy new parts instead, at escalating catalogue prices (T-7). The requirements are higher prices for used parts and green-time engines, more new-parts purchases, approved alternative parts as the other substitute, and capital to acquire what feedstock exists, including whole aircraft bought for their engines. The observable figures are consultancy and trade-press ones and are labelled so: run-out -7Bs bought at $0.8–1.2M, -7B rents about 34% above 2019, CFM56 values up as much as 50% in two years, and AerSale's whole-asset sales falling from 20 engines to 13. <span class="cite">[Safe Fly Aviation, 2026; ePlaneAI; AerSale, 5 Mar 2026]</span> The row reverses when its causes do, which the third columns of T-4, T-5 and T-13 describe.

**T-10, data-center power demand and the gas-turbine shortage.** Part IX owns this driver. It is in the table because it is the one driver that removes CFM56 engines from aviation for a reason unrelated to aviation. The International Energy Agency (IEA) expects global data-center electricity use to more than double to about 945 terawatt-hours by 2030; GE Vernova's gas-turbine backlog plus reservations reached 116 gigawatts (GW) at the second quarter of 2026 with slots being sold for 2031; Siemens Energy reports lead times of three years and more. <span class="cite">[IEA, Apr 2025; Power Engineering, Jul 2026; Oilprice, 2026]</span> Into that shortage FTAI Power sells Mod-1, and 100 units in 2027 would consume about 100 CFM56 engines a year if each unit uses one core, which FTAI has not stated. <span class="cite">[D8 §2.10, U3]</span> The requirement created is competition for run-out engines: a core that becomes a generator set is a core that is not torn down for parts and not rebuilt for exchange. Worked example 3.2 gave the scale on both bases the reconciliation requires: 100 engines are about 21 to 28% of a year's retirements on a manufacturer-level base at 1.5 to 2%, and about 35% on the consultancy base of 14,200 at 2%. <span class="cite">[reconciliation C.8]</span> The row creates no aviation shop visit and adds no used material.

**T-11, freighter conversion.** A conversion candidate is a 20-plus-year-old airframe that needs engines with life on them (§19.1). So conversion creates demand for green-time -7B modules and for shop visits on engines that would otherwise have been retired with their passenger airframes, and it keeps those engines on wing past passenger retirement age. Its third column is the teardown trader's: each converted 737-800 is an airframe and two engines removed from the part-out pool. The row is specific to the -7B, because A320ceo conversion is a smaller programme that the research did not quantify, and it is contested at the research date, with more than 260 737-800 freighters in service and more than 300 in the pipeline on one side and IBA's September 2026 warning of oversupply on the other (R-034).

**T-12, higher utilisation of mature narrowbodies.** IBA observed in September 2024 that delivery delays had led operators to maximise daily utilisation across all age groups, with mature passenger-narrowbody utilisation "rising significantly". <span class="cite">[IBA via AviTrader, 24 Sep 2024]</span> Utilisation here means flying intensity, cycles and hours per aircraft per period, not the lessor's on-lease share that Part VIII §58 uses; the registry records three meanings of the word and this row uses only the first. <span class="cite">[registry C-3]</span> An engine flown 1,500 cycles a year reaches a 10,000-cycle interval in under seven years; at 1,000 cycles a year it takes ten. The row therefore brings shop visits and LLP replacements forward in calendar time and erodes exhaust-gas-temperature margin faster, without adding a single engine to the base or a single teardown to supply. It ends when replacement aircraft arrive and utilisation normalises.

**T-13, GTF groundings easing.** This is the reversal of T-2, recorded separately because it was already under way at the research date. RTX reported PW1100 AOG down 25% year to date, maintenance output up 40% and turnaround down 23% in January 2026, with the GTF Advantage certified for entry into service later in 2026 (§20.1). Each neo that returns to service releases the ceo that was flying in its place. Over 2026 to 2028 that softens ceo extension rates and used ceo lease rates and allows the ceo retirements that T-2 and T-5 suppressed, which in turn adds -5B and V2500 teardown supply later in the decade. The row does not reduce the ceo shop visits already induced, since an engine restored in 2025 has been restored, and its pace is uncertain, with trade commentary putting full resolution at 2027 to 2028.

**T-14, the CFM56 plateau-then-decline and LEAP parity.** GE's 2026 curve holds 2,300 to 2,400 CFM56 visits a year through 2028 with the apex around 2027 to 2028 and a gradual fade after; Safran's earlier framing implied about 2,000 CFM56 visits in 2030 and LEAP aftermarket revenue at parity with the CFM56 by 2028 (§23.1). The long-run requirement is a reallocation: shop capacity moves to the LEAP, and teardown supply rises as retirements return toward McKinsey's about 2.7% of the aircraft fleet a year from 2028. The row does not stress 2026 to 2027 CFM56 demand. Its timing is the least certain figure in the table, because it depends on the retirement rate, which GE revised three times in one year, and because the two CFM partners' curves disagree on level and shape (R-004, R-006, R-007).
