<div class="part-divider"><div class="pn">Part IV</div><h1>The shop-visit market and the OEM aftermarket</h1><p>This Part describes the market that Part I's engine creates: who takes CFM56 engines apart for a living, how many engines come through the doors each year, what stops the shops doing more, and how the engine's manufacturer earns money from every visit whoever performs it. A reader who finishes it will be able to read a capacity announcement, a shop-visit forecast and a parts-price statement with every unit and caveat understood, and will be able to lay out the four choices an operator has when an engine runs out of life.</p></div>

### Translation-table rows answered

Part III §24 turns each fact about the fleet into a requirement on the aftermarket, one row per driver. This Part answers five of those rows. The row on low retirements (Part III §24, row T-x: low retirements) asks how many shop visits a year the retained fleet generates and for how long; §26 gives the forecasts, the base year and the retirement assumption that moves them. The row on the MRO capacity shortage (Part III §24, row T-x: MRO capacity shortfall and turnaround) asks what limits the shops' output and how long an engine waits; §25 names the shops and their announced additions, and §27 gives the slot, labour, parts, stand and turnaround figures. The row on OEM parts escalation (Part III §24, row T-x: OEM parts escalation) asks how the manufacturer's pricing reaches every visit; §28 gives the price steps and the service menu, and §29 shows which slice of a visit's bill they land on. The rows on LEAP durability (Part III §24, row T-x: LEAP durability shortfall) and on the GTF groundings (Part III §24, row T-x: GTF powder-metal groundings) create no CFM56 shop visits directly; they matter here because they keep old aircraft flying and because the new engines are sold under a different commercial model, which §28 sets beside the CFM56's. Part III is being written in parallel with this Part, so the row identifiers above are placeholders to be filled when its table is final. §30 closes with the four options an operator has for an engine at its life limit, which is where the rows meet.

## 25. Who performs shop visits: the OEM network, the independents, airline shops, FTAI

Part I §6 defined a shop visit as any removal of an engine for work in an engine shop, and an engine shop as a facility holding a Part 145 repair-station approval and, in practice, the manufacturer's data and tooling (Part I §9 has the approval). This section asks who owns those shops. The research sorted them into four groups: the manufacturer's own network, the independents, the airlines' in-house shops, and FTAI, which fits none of the first three neatly. The groups are described in turn, and the section ends with the one number the research most wanted and did not find, the manufacturer's share of CFM56 visits.

### 25.1 The OEM network

Part I §2 introduced CFM International as the joint venture of GE Aerospace and Safran Aircraft Engines, the manufacturer and type-certificate holder of the CFM56, and boxed the term "original equipment manufacturer", or OEM. Within the venture GE builds the core and Safran builds the fan, booster, low-pressure turbine and gearbox, and each partner sells spare parts for its own content. <span class="cite">[D2 §3.1; CFM website, undated]</span> Both partners also run shop networks of their own, and both place their names on the services menus described in §28.

GE reports the aftermarket inside one segment.

<div class="defn"><b>Commercial Engines &amp; Services (CES)</b><p>Commercial Engines &amp; Services is the GE Aerospace segment that sells new commercial engines and spare parts and performs shop visits, in GE's own shops and under long-term service agreements. In 2025 it reported revenue of $33,314 million, of which $25,010 million (75.1%) was services, and segment profit of $8,861 million, a 26.6% margin. The share of its services revenue earned on the CFM56 is not disclosed. Part XI §87 reuses these figures beside the other peers. <span class="cite">[GE Aerospace, Form 10-K FY2025; D11 §3.8]</span></p></div>

The segment's growth tells the reader how fast the shop-visit market has been moving at the manufacturer. GE's 10-K attributes 2025 CES revenue growth of $6.4 billion (24%) to "increased spare parts volume, internal shop visit volume and workscopes, increased engine deliveries and pricing". <span class="cite">[GE Aerospace, Form 10-K FY2025]</span> Internal shop-visit revenue grew 24% in 2025, 19% in 2024 and 27% in 2023, and services revenue grew 26% in 2025. <span class="cite">[GE Aerospace, Form 10-K FY2025]</span> GE's first-quarter 2026 release headlined services revenue growth of 39%. <span class="cite">[GE Aerospace, 1Q 2026 earnings release, Apr 2026]</span> The 10-K also records a long-term materials agreement with Ryanair covering its entire fleet of about 2,000 CFM56 and LEAP engines (LEAP is CFM's new-generation engine, Part I §10), which §28.3 places on GE's services menu. <span class="cite">[GE Aerospace, Form 10-K FY2025]</span>

Safran reports the other half. Its 2025 group revenue was €31.3 billion, up 14.7%, with recurring operating income of €5.2 billion at a 16.6% margin. <span class="cite">[Safran, 13 Feb 2026]</span> Civil engine spare-parts revenue, which Safran tracks in US dollars, grew 17.6% in 2025, "driven primarily by the CFM56", on "a higher number of shop visits" and "a higher workscope content". <span class="cite">[Safran, 13 Feb 2026; Forecast International, 13 Feb 2026]</span> Safran had raised its civil aftermarket guidance twice in 2023 to growth in the "low-30% range", and raised full-year guidance again in October 2025 on what Leeham News called a "booming engine aftermarket". <span class="cite">[Aviation Week, late 2023; Leeham News, 24 Oct 2025]</span> Safran's installed base at end-2025 included "more than 22,800 CFM56s", from which it says it "can expect sustained aftermarket revenue from this fleet until 2030". <span class="cite">[Safran, FY2025 results presentation, 13 Feb 2026]</span>

The network is wider than the two partners' own buildings.

<div class="defn"><b>Licensed shop; joint-venture shop</b><p>A licensed shop is an engine shop that performs CFM56 work under a licence from the manufacturer; a joint-venture shop is one in which the manufacturer holds equity, often alongside an airline. Both operate with guaranteed access to the manufacturer's parts and technical data, and both are counted as part of the OEM network rather than as independents. The research did not retrieve a list of CFM56 licensed and joint-venture shops or their throughput. <span class="cite">[D2 §3.1, §8]</span></p></div>

GE's own description of the CFM56 market is that "an open network exists with multiple providers who compete and invest to win shop visits", and that GE will "continue to strengthen MRO access to OEM materials to support further CFM56 longevity". <span class="cite">[GE Aerospace investor-relations page, 2026]</span> Specific CFM56 shop-visit counts for the OEM network, and therefore the network's share of the market, were not found. <span class="cite">[D2 U3]</span> §25.6 returns to that gap.

### 25.2 The large independents

Part I §6 boxed "MRO" as the industry's generic term for maintenance, repair and overhaul and "independent MRO" as an engine shop not owned by the engine's manufacturer. Three kinds of company fit that definition. Stand-alone companies such as StandardAero, Aero Norway and Magnetic MRO do nothing else. Airline subsidiaries such as Lufthansa Technik, AFI KLM E&M, Delta TechOps, Turkish Technic, GMF AeroAsia and EgyptAir Maintenance &amp; Engineering maintain their parent's fleet and sell to third parties. And one engine maker's shop is independent on this engine: MTU Maintenance belongs to MTU Aero Engines, which is a partner in the V2500 (the engine that competes with the CFM56-5B on the A320ceo, Part I §10) but not in CFM, so on the CFM56 it is a customer of the manufacturer like any other shop. <span class="cite">[D2 §3.2]</span>

StandardAero is the independent the research knows most about, because it files with the SEC. Its Engine Services segment reported 2025 revenue of $5,354.0 million, up 15.3%, and segment Adjusted EBITDA of $706.9 million, a 13.2% margin; the segment covers commercial, business-aviation and military engines, and the filing does not give a CFM56 share. <span class="cite">[StandardAero, Form 10-K FY2025; D11 §3.6]</span> Its first-half 2026 report gave revenue of about $3.2 billion, up 8.8%, and Adjusted EBITDA of $433.0 million, up 12.3%. <span class="cite">[StandardAero, Form 10-Q for the quarter ended 30 Jun 2026]</span>

<div class="defn"><b>Center of Excellence</b><p>A Center of Excellence is a shop or line dedicated to one engine type, so that tooling, training, test-cell correlation and parts flow are specialised to it. StandardAero's CFM56-7B Center of Excellence in Dallas, Texas, opened in the second half of 2024 "to service the growing demand on that platform". Part XI §87 uses the term when it profiles StandardAero. <span class="cite">[StandardAero, Form 10-K FY2025]</span></p></div>

StandardAero states that it has spent "more than $100 million since 2022 to expand" and to "more than double our shop visit capacity on the CFM56-7B" through the Dallas Center of Excellence, and more than $100 million on a LEAP line at its 810,000-square-foot San Antonio facility. <span class="cite">[StandardAero, Form 10-K FY2025]</span> Its Winnipeg expansion added 70,000 square feet, about 40% more footprint, and came online on 3 September 2025 with capacity for the CF34 (a regional-jet engine) and the CFM56. <span class="cite">[GlobalAir, Sept 2025]</span> The LEAP and CFM56 Dallas programmes "reached profitability" in a 2025 quarter, according to the company's third-quarter 2025 filing. <span class="cite">[StandardAero, Form 10-Q for the quarter ended 30 Sep 2025]</span> Its chief commercial officer named labour as the leading constraint. <span class="cite">[Aviation Week, "Narrowbody Engine Demand Drives MRO Capacity Additions", 2025]</span>

MTU Maintenance overhauls the CFM56 and the V2500 at Hannover and at Zhuhai in China, and has a Berlin-Brandenburg shop; Aviation Week reports it "invested heavily in ramping up capacity, with expansions" at all three. <span class="cite">[Aviation Week, 2025]</span> MTU's commercial MRO business reported 2025 adjusted revenue of €6.0 billion, up 18%, and adjusted EBIT of €478 million, an 8.0% margin, with geared-turbofan work about 40% of commercial maintenance; no CFM56 share was returned. <span class="cite">[MTU Aero Engines, 24 Feb 2026; D11 §3.9]</span> EME Aero, the MTU and Lufthansa Technik joint venture, is a geared-turbofan shop only and targets 500 interventions a year from 2028. <span class="cite">[Aviation Week, 2025]</span>

Lufthansa Technik runs a CFM56 engine shop at Hamburg. Its 2025 revenue was €8.049 billion, up 12%, and adjusted EBIT was €603 million, a 7.5% margin; Engine Services "generates almost half" of revenue. No CFM56 capacity or shop-visit figure was returned. <span class="cite">[Lufthansa Technik, FY2025 results release, early 2026; D11 §3.9]</span> ST Engineering, the Singapore-based group, named skills shortages and supply chain as its biggest challenges and gave no capacity figure. <span class="cite">[Aviation Week, 2025]</span> The three margins just quoted sit on three different bases, segment Adjusted EBITDA for StandardAero and adjusted EBIT for MTU and Lufthansa Technik, and Part XI §88 explains why they cannot be set side by side without saying so. <span class="cite">[D11 §2.3]</span>

The smaller specialists fill in the picture. Aero Norway, a CFM56 specialist at Stavanger, wrote in May 2024 that long lead times for induction slots were driving interest in quick-turn services, "especially in the case of older-generation engines, such as the CFM56 and V2500", and it has awarded a repair-management contract to GE (§28.5). <span class="cite">[Aero Norway in AVM Magazine (a trade publication), May 2024; GE Aerospace release, undated]</span> FL Technics expanded its Kaunas engine shop for CFM56 demand and reports that about 70% of its work is on the -7B, the remainder on the -5B and the older -3. <span class="cite">[FL Technics release, 2025]</span> Magnetic MRO, based in Tallinn, discussed CFM56 "market challenges, opportunities" for 2024 in the trade press. <span class="cite">[Aviation Week, 2024]</span> SR Technics in Zurich publishes a CFM56-7B capabilities and price catalogue; a version dated 3 June 2026 was located and not read, which is why no shop's price list appears anywhere in this primer. <span class="cite">[SR Technics, 3 Jun 2026; D2 §3.2]</span>

For AFI KLM E&amp;M, Delta TechOps, Turkish Technic, GMF AeroAsia, EgyptAir Maintenance &amp; Engineering and GA Telesis the research established only that each operates a CFM56 shop or a related business. GMF AeroAsia and EgyptAir are FTAI's capacity partners (Part VII §50). EgyptAir holds a GE TrueChoice overhaul-consulting agreement (§28.5). GA Telesis is primarily a trader in used material with an engine shop attached (Part V §35). No capacity or shop-visit figure was returned for any of the six. <span class="cite">[D2 §3.2, U5; Amwal Al Ghad, undated]</span>

### 25.3 Airline shops

The third group is the hardest to see. Many airlines maintain their own engines in their own shops and never sell a visit to anyone. Where an airline's maintenance arm has become a business in its own right it appears in the previous subsection, since Lufthansa Technik, AFI KLM E&amp;M, Delta TechOps, Turkish Technic, GMF AeroAsia and EgyptAir Maintenance &amp; Engineering are all airline subsidiaries. The in-house-only shops behind them were not profiled. The research retrieved no count of airline CFM56 engine shops, no estimate of the share of visits performed in-house, and no figure for how much of that work has moved to third parties as fleets have aged. <span class="cite">[D2 §3.2, §3.4]</span> The reader should treat the airline group as present in every forecast of §26 and absent from every capacity table of this Part.

### 25.4 FTAI

FTAI's business on the CFM56 is the Module Factory, which Part I §3 quoted from the FY2025 annual report and which Part VII §46 treats in full. It is listed here as a performer of shop work because its shops take engines and modules apart, repair them and test them, and because its capacity statements sit in the trade press beside the independents'. It differs from the other three groups in what it sells: a serviceable module or engine by exchange or sale, rather than a visit priced to a customer's own engine. <span class="cite">[FTAI 10-K FY2025; D5 §2.2]</span>

<div class="defn"><b>LMCES; Montréal</b><p>Lockheed Martin Commercial Engine Solutions (LMCES) was the Montréal engine shop that FTAI acquired from Lockheed Martin Canada, closing on 9 September 2024 for total consideration of $170.0 million; the shop is about 500,000 square feet in the annual report and 526,000 in the closing release. It is the base of the Module Factory. Part VII §50 carries the facility in full. <span class="cite">[FTAI release, 9 Sep 2024; FTAI 10-K FY2025; reconciliation R-038, R-039]</span></p></div>

<div class="defn"><b>QuickTurn; QuickTurn Europe</b><p>QuickTurn is the brand of FTAI's Miami engine business, formed from the assets of iAero Thrust acquired on 4 January 2023 with Unical Aviation as partner; FTAI bought Unical's remaining interest on 1 December 2023 for $30.3 million in cash. QuickTurn Europe is the 50% joint venture at Rome Fiumicino Airport, Italy, in the former IAG Engine Center, announced 26 February 2025 and closed 5 June 2025 for $10.5 million, with 200,000 square feet. Lower-case "quick-turn" is a workscope (Part I §6); the capitalised names are these businesses. Part VII §50 carries both. <span class="cite">[FTAI releases, 1 Dec 2023, 26 Feb 2025 and 5 Jun 2025; FTAI 10-K FY2025; registry C-20]</span></p></div>

FTAI's annual report lists 100%-owned maintenance sites at Montréal, Miami, Lisbon and Orange, the Rome joint venture, and a total of "over one million square feet"; its capacity partnerships with GMF AeroAsia in Indonesia and with EgyptAir were described on calls and their terms were not retrieved. <span class="cite">[FTAI 10-K FY2025; D5 U8]</span> Part VII §50 carries what is known about Lisbon and Orange; their deal terms, sizes and opening dates were not returned from any filing or release.

FTAI's capacity has been stated four times in four units, and each statement covers a different footprint. The annual report still carries language first used in 2022 describing Montréal as a facility "with a capacity for up to 300 shop visits per year". <span class="cite">[FTAI 10-K FY2025; FTAI 10-K FY2022]</span> At the Montréal closing FTAI said the Montréal and Miami shops together gave "capacity for 1,350 CFM56 module overhauls and >500 engine tests per year". <span class="cite">[FTAI release, 9 Sep 2024]</span> With the Rome agreement it said QuickTurn Europe "adds capacity for 450 modules (150 engines) per year, taking FTAI's total to 1,800 modules (600 engines)" and more than 600 engine tests a year. <span class="cite">[FTAI release, 26 Feb 2025]</span> On the second-quarter 2026 call management said it had increased CFM56 module production capacity to 3,000 a year, "supporting its 25% market share goal and 100 Mod-1 units annually"; that figure comes from call coverage and was not returned from the release text. <span class="cite">[GuruFocus and Quartr coverage of the FTAI call of 30 Jul 2026; orchestrator notes]</span> Lisbon's "113,000 square feet, 300+ modules a year" is also call coverage and unverified against any primary source; the annual report lists Lisbon as owned and the July 2026 call described it as "still ramping". <span class="cite">[reconciliation R-037, R-041]</span>

Two cautions govern how these figures are read. First, a shop visit, a module overhaul and a module are different units; FTAI's own releases convert at three modules per engine (450 modules for 150 engines, 1,800 for 600), which matches the three gas-path modules of Part I §3, and no Part of this primer compares the 300-visit figure with the module figures directly. <span class="cite">[reconciliation R-035, B-3; registry C-4]</span> Second, the figures are dated statements about growing footprints, not a single capacity revised over time. Exhibit 4.2 carries them beside the independents' statements with the unit each one counts.

Output is reported in modules. FTAI refurbished 757 CFM56 modules in 2025 against a target of 750, 184 in the second quarter of 2025, 228 in the fourth, 296 in the second quarter of 2026 (up 61% year on year) and 566 in the first half of 2026. <span class="cite">[FTAI releases, 29 Jul 2025, 25 Feb 2026 and 29 Jul 2026; call coverage, Feb 2026]</span> The 2026 target was set at 1,050 modules with the full-year 2025 results in February 2026 and was raised to 1,200 on the July 2026 call according to call coverage. <span class="cite">[FTAI release, 25 Feb 2026; GuruFocus, Nasdaq.com and MarketBeat coverage, 30 Jul 2026; reconciliation R-036]</span>

The research did one piece of arithmetic to size this against the market, and it carries three caveats. At three modules per engine, 1,200 modules is about 400 engine-equivalents, which is about 17% of a 2,350-visit year; 3,000 modules would be about 1,000 engine-equivalents, about 43%. <span class="cite">[derived in the research, D2 §3.3]</span> These are not shop-visit shares: modules are sold individually, many go into engines that also visit other shops, and the denominator FTAI uses for its "25% market share goal" was not retrieved. <span class="cite">[D5 U5; reconciliation R-042]</span> A third framing, from FTAI's own release of 26 February 2025, is a $22 billion total addressable market for maintenance, repair and exchange in which FTAI held about 5% as of 2024. <span class="cite">[FTAI release, 26 Feb 2025]</span> Dollars, visits and module-equivalents are three different denominators, and Part VII §49 carries all three.

### 25.5 Capacity statements and additions, 2022 to 2026

Before the exhibit, the unit needs a definition, because the independents and FTAI count different things.

<div class="defn"><b>Capacity (shop)</b><p>A shop's capacity is the number of engine inductions a year it can take. Four things set it: floor space and engine stands, which bound how many engines can be open at once; licensed technicians; test-cell hours, since every rebuilt engine must be run before release; and parts flow, because a shop that cannot get turbine blades or repaired cases on time fills up with half-built engines that consume stands and floor without producing output. Additions are usually announced in square feet or as a multiple of previous throughput. FTAI's "modules per year" is a different unit, and Part VII §49 states the conversion FTAI's releases use. <span class="cite">[D2 §2.4, §5; registry C-4]</span></p></div>

Two patterns are visible in the table and both are about units rather than facts. The independents announce money and floor space and, once, a multiple of previous throughput; none of them states inductions a year. FTAI states modules, engines and tests, and once, in old language, shop visits. The only way to compare the two sets is to convert FTAI's modules to engines at the convention its own releases use, and even then an engine-equivalent of modules is not an induction. <span class="cite">[reconciliation R-037; registry C-4]</span> The table is therefore a list of dated announcements, not a capacity model, and the research built no such model. <span class="cite">[D2 §4.8]</span>

### 25.6 The OEM share: not known

No source the research reached gives the share of CFM56 shop visits performed in the manufacturer's network against the independents and the airlines. <span class="cite">[D2 U3]</span> Three facts bound the question without answering it. Safran describes the CFM56 aftermarket's revenue as 85% spare parts and time-and-materials work, so most visits are not performed under the manufacturer's flight-hour contracts (§28.2). <span class="cite">[Safran via Aviation Week, c. 2023/24]</span> Trade press puts about 15% of the CFM56 fleet under long-term agreements against about 50% of the LEAP fleet; the two figures are complements of the same view and are both Aviation Week's reporting of Safran. <span class="cite">[Aviation Week, undated; reconciliation B-4]</span> And Safran attributes part of its LEAP spares growth to "the increasing number of shop visits performed by third-party MROs", which implies the manufacturer tracks third-party share internally without disclosing it. <span class="cite">[Safran, 13 Feb 2026; D2 §3.4]</span> What would resolve the question is shop-visit-by-provider data from Aviation Week or Cirium, or a provider split in the Oliver Wyman fleet forecast. <span class="cite">[D2 U3]</span>

## 26. Demand: shop visits per year, the peak, and the retirement assumption behind it

Part III §23 carries the forecast curve as the manufacturers and forecasters publish it, and this section reads the same figures as a market: how many engines a year the shops of §25 can expect, when the count stops rising, and which single assumption moves it. The two sections cross-reference each other and neither re-boxes the other's terms. <span class="cite">[registry, Part IV rows]</span>

### 26.1 The base year

The reference point is 2019. Safran, as reported by Aviation Week, put annual CFM shop visits in 2019 at "just above 2,000", "almost all of them CFM56-5B and -7B". <span class="cite">[Safran via Aviation Week, "Safran Sees Leap Aftermarket Emerging By Mid-Decade", c. 2023/24]</span> Shop-visit counts for 2020 to 2023 were not found year by year. <span class="cite">[D2 U2]</span> For 2024 Safran expected total CFM shop visits "topping 2,000 again", a figure that includes some LEAP visits. <span class="cite">[Safran via Aviation Week, c. 2023/24]</span> Those are the only pre-2025 points the research holds, and the gap between 2019 and 2024 is carried as an unknown rather than filled.

### 26.2 The two forecasts and the peak

Two other figures in the exhibit do not fit either curve and are carried as unreconciled. The Aviation Week forecast gives the context: about 2,300 CFM56 visits inside about 9,000 engine shop visits of all types in 2025, so the CFM56 is roughly a quarter of all engine shop work by count. <span class="cite">[Aviation Week 2026 Fleet &amp; MRO Forecast, via ePlaneAI]</span>

<div class="box"><div class="h">A plateau is not a peak</div><p>The earlier manufacturer view describes a peak: a top of about 2,500 visits in 2025 or 2026 and a fall after it. The later view describes a plateau: 2,300 to 2,400 visits for three years and a "gradual fade" after 2028. The difference matters to a shop deciding whether to add a stand or a test cell, because capacity added into a peak is idle sooner than capacity added into a plateau, and the research records that a shop or module factory adding capacity now "is adding it into a plateau" on the manufacturer's own forecast. What moved the shape between the two dates was not the fleet, which only shrinks, but the assumption about how fast it shrinks, which §26.3 takes up. Where the fade begins, and how steep it is, depends on that same assumption. <span class="cite">[D2 §5.7; GE Aerospace via Aviation Week, 2026]</span></p></div>

What population each count describes is not stated in the sources. None of them says whether a module exchange performed without a full induction counts as a shop visit, whether visits to the older -3, -5A and -5C variants are included, or whether a quick-turn counts the same as an overhaul. <span class="cite">[D2 §4.1]</span> The reader should hold the counts as the sources' own and not as a single series.

### 26.3 The retirement assumption behind the curve

Part I §2 boxed the retirement rate and gave GE's sequence; Part III §19 is the full treatment with every denominator. Here the point is mechanical. No new -5B or -7B is being built, so the installed base falls by exactly the number of engines retired or converted each year. <span class="cite">[D9 §2.2]</span> Every engine that is not retired keeps flying and keeps accumulating cycles, and eventually it needs the work this Part describes. So the retirement assumption is the one input that sets both the height and the length of the shop-visit curve. <span class="cite">[D9 §2.2; D2 §5.7]</span>

### 26.4 How mature the fleet is

The second input to the curve is how many engines have never been to a shop.

<div class="defn"><b>Fleet maturity</b><p>Fleet maturity is the distribution of an installed base by the number of shop visits each engine has had: none, one, or more. GE reported in June 2019 that 57% of CFM56 engines had not had a first shop visit and 21% had had one; Safran reported in 2024 that about 70% had seen zero or one. A first visit on the latest -5B and -7B variants comes near the life limit and so combines a performance restoration with life-limited-part replacement (Part I §6), which makes it a heavier visit than a mature-run restoration. <span class="cite">[GE Aviation &amp; GECAS Investor Day, 18 Jun 2019; Safran media, 2024; D2 §4.2]</span></p></div>

The three maturity readings in Exhibit 4.4 point the same way and come from sources of different weight. GE's 2019 figure and Safran's 2024 figure are manufacturer materials; the 2026 figure, "over half" with no visit and "another quarter" with one, about 75%, is from a consultancy blog and is labelled so. <span class="cite">[reconciliation R-012; E.1 rule 3]</span> The research did not resolve how 70% in 2024 and about 75% in 2026 can both hold for a fleet that is only getting older, and records the pair as directionally the same with the later figure from the weaker source. <span class="cite">[D2 T7]</span>

Two facts sit beside the maturity figures without the research joining them. Trade sources say first-run -5B and -7B engines of the latest variants fly "almost to LLP limit", so their first visit is a life-limit-driven one (Part I §6). <span class="cite">[FlightGlobal, undated; reconciliation B-7]</span> And Safran attributes part of its 2025 spares growth to "a higher proportion of full work scope shop visits". <span class="cite">[Safran, 13 Feb 2026]</span> Whether the second is a consequence of the first was not stated by either source, and the primer places them side by side and stops.

## 27. Capacity: slots, labor, parts, stands, turnaround

§25.5 defined a shop's capacity as the number of inductions a year it can take, set by floor and stands, technicians, test-cell hours and parts flow. Between 2023 and 2026 each of those four limits was reported as binding somewhere in the CFM56 shop network, and this section gives the figure and date for each. It closes with turnaround time, which Part I §6 boxed and which is the quantity an operator actually feels. Every figure below is from a filing, a consultancy survey or the trade press, and the exhibits say which. <span class="cite">[D2 §2.4, §5]</span>

### 27.1 The four limits, and the unit they share

A shop's output is engines released per year. Each limit caps that output in a different way. Floor space and engine stands cap how many engines can be open at once; a shop that is full of engines waiting for parts is full, whatever its technicians could do. Technicians cap how many engine-hours of work can be done in a month, and the work is licensed, so a vacancy cannot be filled quickly. Test-cell hours cap how many rebuilt engines can be proven and released. Parts flow caps all three, because an engine waiting for a turbine blade or a repaired case occupies a stand, has consumed labour, and cannot go to test. <span class="cite">[D2 §2.4]</span> The industry expresses an addition to any of these as square feet, as §25.5's exhibit showed, because the building is the limit that can be seen and announced. <span class="cite">[D2 §5.5]</span>

### 27.2 Induction slots

<div class="defn"><b>Induction; induction slot</b><p>Induction is the acceptance of an engine into work at a shop, the point from which turnaround time is counted. An induction slot is the date a shop commits to start a given engine. When shops are full, the wait for a slot is distinct from, and additional to, the turnaround, and in 2023 to 2026 that wait became the binding constraint on an operator's engine availability. In other Parts "slot" also means a production slot for alternative parts (Part VI) or a reserved manufacturing slot for turbines (Part IX); this primer says "induction slot" for the shop meaning. <span class="cite">[D2 §2.1, §2.4, §8; registry C-18]</span></p></div>

Bain reported in 2024 that "the wait times just to secure an MRO slot rose by two to three months and, in some cases, six months", and projected that engine maintenance demand would exceed available capacity by more than 17% before the end of the decade if capacity kept its current trajectory. <span class="cite">[Bain &amp; Company, 2024]</span> Trade press reported shops "at full capacity over the past year" in 2025. <span class="cite">[Aviation Week, 2025]</span> Aero Norway's account from the shop floor is that long lead times for induction slots were pushing operators toward quick-turn work, "especially in the case of older-generation engines, such as the CFM56 and V2500". <span class="cite">[Aero Norway in AVM, May 2024]</span> An engine-stand utilisation report carried by the trade press expects engine maintenance demand to peak in the near term in 2026 and to remain constrained through the end of the decade. <span class="cite">[AviationPros, undated]</span>

<div class="box"><div class="h">Why the slot, not the turnaround, became the binding constraint</div><p>An operator planning a removal faces two waits in series. First it waits for a shop to accept the engine; then it waits for the shop to finish. The second wait is the turnaround time of §27.6, which lengthened from about 60 days to 90 to 120 days for a full overhaul. The first wait, which was close to zero when shops had spare stands, grew to two to six months. An aircraft without an engine is grounded or flies on a leased spare for both waits together, so the slot wait added more calendar time to a removal than the longer turnaround did. That is what raised the value of anything that avoids a full induction: a quick-turn visit, a module exchange from inventory, or a green-time engine flown in place of the one that ran out. Each of those substitutes shifts where and how the work is done; none of them changes how many engines reach their limits. <span class="cite">[D2 §5.1; Bain, 2024; D9 §5]</span></p></div>

### 27.3 Labour

Oliver Wyman's 2026 maintenance survey, of more than 150 respondents, found that "two-thirds of MRO operators struggle to find qualified technicians", that labour-rate inflation had "settled in at 5.5–6.0%", and that "more than half of survey respondents expect labor attrition to be worse in the coming year". <span class="cite">[Oliver Wyman, Apr 2026; ePlaneAI, 2026]</span> StandardAero's chief commercial officer and ST Engineering both named labour as the leading constraint on adding capacity. <span class="cite">[Aviation Week, 2025]</span> Oliver Wyman also cites the retirement of skilled workers as a driver of rising maintenance cost, and no headcount or shortfall figure for engine technicians surfaced in the research. <span class="cite">[Oliver Wyman via AviationPros, 2026; D9 U8]</span>

Labour is 20% to 30% of a shop visit on one share breakdown and 15% to 20% on another (§29.1), so a 5.5% to 6.0% rise in labour rates adds roughly 1.1 to 1.8 percentage points to the cost of a visit each year on its own. <span class="cite">[derived in the research, D2 §5.2; Air Cargo Week, 2025/26; Safe Fly Aviation, 2026]</span> Worked example 4.2 puts that beside the parts line. One labour data point from FTAI belongs here for scale: the company reported 985 full-time employees and independent contractors at 31 December 2025, of whom 494 were full-time employees in Canada, about 71% of them covered by collective bargaining agreements; the figure mixes employees and contractors, as the filing's wording does. <span class="cite">[FTAI 10-K FY2025; E.1 rule 24]</span> Part X §85 carries it.

### 27.4 Parts

The parts constraint is the one the sources disagree about, and the disagreement is carried whole.

On one side is the manufacturer's own filing. GE's 10-K for 2025 reports that its operating system raised material input from priority suppliers by more than 40% year on year and that turnaround times for the LEAP, the CFM56 and the GE90 (a widebody engine) improved by more than 10% year on year in the fourth quarter of 2025. <span class="cite">[GE Aerospace, Form 10-K FY2025]</span>

<div class="defn"><b>FLIGHT DECK</b><p>FLIGHT DECK is the name GE Aerospace gives its proprietary operating system for running its factories and shops. Its FY2025 annual report credits it with increasing material input from priority suppliers by more than 40% year on year, enabling a 26% rise in services revenue, and with the turnaround improvement quoted above. The research holds no description of what the system consists of beyond the name and those two claims. <span class="cite">[GE Aerospace, Form 10-K FY2025; D2 §2.4, §3.1]</span></p></div>

On the other side are the surveys and the trade press. Oliver Wyman's April 2026 survey found labour and material shortages remained the primary disruptors; two-thirds of respondents reported that next-generation narrowbody shop-visit costs were running 21% or more above expectation, and a quarter reported 50% or more. <span class="cite">[Oliver Wyman, Apr 2026]</span> Aviation Week reported in 2025 that "parts supply remains a capacity constraint with every OEM, due to the recovery in aircraft utilization and the associated growth in MRO demand coming at the same time that engine OEMs are ramping up production". <span class="cite">[Aviation Week, 2025]</span> AVM reported repair lead times "surpassing more than 200 days in severe cases" on some new-generation programmes, and the trade press describes four-to-six-month waits for repairs at third-party facilities, with buyers advised to order life-limited parts and critical rotables early and to use exchange pools and used material. <span class="cite">[AVM Magazine, 2025; Air Cargo Week and Locatory, Mar 2026]</span> The same trade press says "the supply of used serviceable material remains thin, leaving shops more reliant on newly manufactured core parts", and Aviation Week ran a column headed "A Seller's Market for Used Parts". <span class="cite">[AVM Magazine, 2025; Aviation Week, undated]</span>

The two sides are not necessarily about the same thing. The manufacturer's statement is about its own suppliers and its own shops in one quarter; the survey is about operators' experience across all shops and programmes, and the cost-overrun figure is about new-generation engines. But the research records it as a tension because a reader will meet both: the manufacturer reports easing in late 2025, and the operator survey of April 2026 does not. <span class="cite">[D2 T8; reconciliation R-020]</span> What would narrow it is a dated lead-time series for turbine blades, castings and forgings, which the research sought and did not find. <span class="cite">[D2 U6]</span>

The used-material side of the parts constraint has a cause this Part has already named. Retirements of 1.5% to 2% rather than 3% to 4% mean fewer teardowns, fewer used parts and fewer green-time engines, so shops are pushed back to new parts at the escalated list price, and the manufacturer's pricing power rises. <span class="cite">[D2 §2.4, §5.4]</span> Part V §33 carries the supply side of that and Part VII §52 the agreement FTAI signed with CFM for parts (§28.8).

### 27.5 Engine stands and floor

<div class="defn"><b>Engine stand</b><p>An engine stand is the wheeled fixture that holds a roughly 2.5-tonne engine, or a module, while it is worked on, stored or transported. Every engine in a shop occupies a stand from induction to release, so the stock of stands is a physical limit on work in progress, and a shortage of stands shows up as engines that cannot be inducted even when technicians are free. Utilisation of stands for legacy engines rose by about a third in 2025 over 2024, with CFM56-5A/B stand usage up 60%, according to one trade-press report. Part III §23 glosses the term at first mention. <span class="cite">[D2 §2.1, §8; AviationPros, undated]</span></p></div>

The stand figures are the only hard numbers the research holds on this limit. A report carried by AviationPros says engine-stand utilisation for legacy engines rose by a third in 2025 compared with 2024, and that CFM56-5A/B stand usage rose 60%. <span class="cite">[AviationPros, "Engine Stand Utilization Report", undated]</span> No figure for a stand shortage, a stand lead time or a stand price was found. <span class="cite">[D2 U6]</span> Floor space is reported as the proxy: StandardAero's Winnipeg addition of 70,000 square feet and FTAI's Lisbon site (per call coverage, unverified) are announced in square feet because stands and floor are the limit that an announcement can count. <span class="cite">[D2 §5.5; GlobalAir, Sept 2025]</span>

## 28. The OEM aftermarket model: list prices, escalation, TrueChoice, EngineWise, long-term agreements

The manufacturer earns from a shop visit in two ways. It sells the new parts the visit consumes, at a published price it revises each year, to any shop that performs the work. And it sells services under its own brand: visits in its own shops, parts and repairs to other shops, and multi-year contracts under which it takes over the maintenance of an engine for a price per flight hour. The balance between those two is what distinguishes the CFM56's aftermarket from the LEAP's, and this section takes them in order.

<div class="defn"><b>OEM aftermarket model</b><p>The OEM aftermarket model is the way the engine's manufacturer earns from its installed base after delivery: spare parts sold at an escalating catalogue list price to every shop, plus branded services (shop visits, parts and repair packages, used material, repair management) and long-term agreements priced per flight hour. On the CFM56 about 85% of aftermarket revenue is spare parts and time-and-materials work, against about 40% for the LEAP, so the CFM56 is an open, transactional market in which the manufacturer's main capture is parts rather than contracts, and the LEAP is being set up as a largely contracted one. <span class="cite">[Safran via Aviation Week, c. 2023/24; D2 §2.3, §3.4]</span></p></div>

### 28.1 Spare parts at list price, escalating

Part I §4 boxed the catalogue list price as the manufacturer's published price for every new spare part, repriced at least annually, and escalation as the annual increase in it. The mechanics are simple and the consequence is large. The shop buys the part at list and passes it through at list plus a handling margin, or the airline buys the part directly under a materials agreement; either way the manufacturer receives the list price for every new part a visit consumes. <span class="cite">[D2 §2.2]</span> Used and alternative parts are priced as discounts to the same list, so they inherit its escalation (Part V §34, Part VI §44). <span class="cite">[D3 §2.5]</span>

### 28.2 Time-and-materials and long-term agreements

The second distinction in the manufacturer's model is who pays for a visit and when.

<div class="defn"><b>Time-and-materials (T&amp;M)</b><p>Under a time-and-materials arrangement the operator pays for each shop visit as it occurs: parts at the catalogue list price and labour by the hour, with the workscope agreed visit by visit. The operator carries the cost risk (a heavier workscope costs more) and the volume risk (more removals cost more), and it chooses the shop and, within the shop manual, the source of the parts. It is the pay-per-visit model and the opposite of a long-term agreement. <span class="cite">[D2 §2.3, §8]</span></p></div>

<div class="defn"><b>Long-term service agreement (LTSA); also LTA, rate-per-flight-hour (RPFH) or flight-hour agreement</b><p>A long-term service agreement is a multi-year contract under which the operator pays a fixed amount per engine flight hour and the manufacturer, or a shop, commits to perform or pay for the engine's shop visits. The provider takes the volume and cost risk and in return controls the workscope, the parts sourcing and the choice of shop. Revenue under such a contract is recognised against an estimate of the contract's total profitability, so a change in that estimate flows through current-period profit: GE's 2025 segment profit was reduced by "an unfavorable change in estimated profitability of long-term service agreements, primarily from the estimated impact from tariffs". About 15% of the CFM56 fleet is under such agreements against about 50% of the LEAP fleet, with the LEAP targeted at 60–70% by 2030. The dossiers use three names for one concept; this primer uses LTSA and gives LTA and RPFH as aliases once, here. Part III §20 glosses the term at first mention. <span class="cite">[D2 §2.3; D9 §2.5; D11 §2.3; GE Aerospace, Form 10-K FY2025; registry C-12]</span></p></div>

Safran's description of the CFM56 aftermarket is that 85% of its revenue is spare parts and time-and-materials work, against about 40% for the LEAP, and that the LEAP fleet will reach 60% to 70% coverage by long-term agreements by 2030. <span class="cite">[Safran via Aviation Week, "CFM Aftermarket Shifting To Long-term Agreements", c. 2023/24]</span> The fleet-share figures are complements of the same picture: about 15% of CFM56 engines under long-term agreements against about 50% of the roughly 5,500 LEAPs in service at the time of the report. <span class="cite">[Aviation Week, "Leap Aftermarket Poised For Growth", undated; reconciliation B-4]</span> Both figures are Aviation Week's reporting of Safran's view, so their agreement is weak as corroboration. <span class="cite">[reconciliation B-4]</span>

The distinction matters to every later Part of this primer for one reason. Under a time-and-materials visit the operator, or the shop it chooses, decides whether a part is new from the manufacturer, used, repaired or an approved alternative; under a long-term agreement the manufacturer decides, and it has no reason to choose a competitor's part. <span class="cite">[D2 §2.3]</span> So the used-material market of Part V, the alternative parts of Part VI and the module exchanges of Part VII all address the 85% of the CFM56 aftermarket that is transactional, and would address far less of a fleet that was contracted the way the LEAP is being contracted. The research found no source that quantifies how much CFM56 parts demand sits inside long-term agreements and so outside the open market. <span class="cite">[D2 §3.4]</span>

### 28.4 Material solutions, and the manufacturer as a seller of used parts

<div class="defn"><b>Material solutions</b><p>Material solutions is the generic trade phrase for a shop's or a manufacturer's package of used serviceable material, repaired parts and approved alternative parts assembled to reduce the new-parts bill of a shop visit. The whole commercial logic of a shop that is not the manufacturer sits on this line, since material is 60–70% of a visit. The research could not match the phrase to a current CFM or GE programme name; GE's used-material offering sits inside TrueChoice Material, and a historical Pratt &amp; Whitney "Global Material Solutions" venture on the CFM56-3 was found only as a headline. This primer uses the generic sense. <span class="cite">[D2 §2.2, §8; D4 U10; registry C-19]</span></p></div>

The manufacturer competes on this line too. GE states that "GE and CFM are the largest used serviceable material provider for their products", and that it will "continue to strengthen MRO access to OEM materials to support further CFM56 longevity". <span class="cite">[GE Aerospace investor-relations page, 2026]</span> The name, scale and CFM56 role of GE's used-material arm were not captured, and the research records the gap as a cluster shared with Parts V and VI. <span class="cite">[D3 U15; D4 U10; cluster K-16]</span> What the pricing of used material against new looks like, 60% to 80% of a new part's price on average or 70% to 75% of list when a trader sells to an airline, is Part V §34's subject, and the two figures are carried there as a pair. <span class="cite">[Aviation Week, undated; Aircraft Commerce Issue 120; reconciliation R-022]</span>

### 28.5 Repair management and overhaul consulting

<div class="defn"><b>Repair management</b><p>Repair management is a service the manufacturer sells to a shop that is not its own: it manages the routing of the shop's piece-part repairs through the manufacturer's repair network and data, so the independent shop buys repair capability rather than building it. GE sold such a contract to Aero Norway, a CFM56 independent. A related product is overhaul consulting, under which the manufacturer advises an airline's own shop on its overhauls; EgyptAir signed a TrueChoice overhaul-consulting agreement with GE. The research holds the names of the two contracts and no terms. <span class="cite">[GE Aerospace release, "Aero Norway awards repair management contract to GE Aviation", undated; Amwal Al Ghad, undated; D2 §2.3]</span></p></div>

These two products show the manufacturer earning from visits it does not perform. A repair-management contract puts the manufacturer's repairs into an independent's visit; an overhaul-consulting agreement puts its workscope judgement into an airline's. Both leave the shop independent in name and bring part of the visit's repair slice (§29.2) back to the manufacturer. <span class="cite">[D2 §2.3]</span>

### 28.6 IAE EngineWise and FleetCare

Part I §10 introduced the V2500 and boxed EngineWise as Pratt &amp; Whitney's aftermarket services brand, under which the IAE network performs V2500 shop visits. IAE is the consortium of Pratt &amp; Whitney (part of RTX), Pratt &amp; Whitney Aero Engines International, Japanese Aero Engines Corporation and MTU Aero Engines. <span class="cite">[D2 §2.3; Part I §10]</span> RTX describes EngineWise as "a variety of aftermarket services to maximize engine performance and fleet availability". <span class="cite">[RTX / Pratt &amp; Whitney release, 6 Jun 2024]</span>

The agreement that brings EngineWise into this primer is FTAI's. On 6 June 2024 IAE and FTAI announced a five-year EngineWise agreement covering more than 100 full performance-restoration shop visits on V2500 engines, which RTX called "one of IAE's largest engine maintenance agreements by number of engines with a non-airline customer"; FTAI then owned or was committed to more than 140 V2500 engines and aimed for 200 by 2025. <span class="cite">[RTX / Pratt &amp; Whitney release, 6 Jun 2024]</span> Functionally this is the opposite of the Module Factory: a large engine owner buying manufacturer-network restorations at a negotiated price for a committed volume. Part VII §51 carries it. <span class="cite">[D5 §2.5]</span>

The V2500 market the agreement sits in has two counts for 2025 and the primer carries both. Aviation Week's V2500 data tool projected a spike to about 1,400 V2500 shop visits in 2025, with more than 7,800 overhaul visits and 3,142 life-limited-part-driven visits over the following ten years. <span class="cite">[Aviation Week, "Data Tool: The Future Of IAE V2500 Engine", 2024/25]</span> RTX's chief financial officer, as reported by Aviation Week in 2026, put 2025 V2500 shop visits at "slightly more than 800", with 2026 expected within about 20 of that. <span class="cite">[RTX CFO via Aviation Week, 2026; reconciliation R-009]</span> One is a published forecast and the other a reported outturn, both via the same publication, and the research does not resolve them. IAE separately said it expects no V2500 retirement surge over the next five years. <span class="cite">[IAE via Aviation Week, 2024/25]</span>

"FleetCare" was not found as a current IAE or Pratt &amp; Whitney product name; only EngineWise was. <span class="cite">[D2 U9]</span> The brief for this primer named it, and the primer records that the name did not surface rather than describing a product it did not find.

### 28.8 The CFM–FTAI materials agreement of January 2026

On 22 January 2026 FTAI announced a multi-year materials agreement with CFM International under which FTAI "secures OEM replacement part supply, thrust performance upgrades and component repair", described by both companies as strengthening "the open maintenance, repair, and overhaul (MRO) ecosystem". <span class="cite">[FTAI release, 22 Jan 2026]</span> The agreement places FTAI, a buyer of used material and a partner in an alternative-parts venture, on the manufacturer's TrueChoice Material side of the ledger at the same time; on FTAI's fourth-quarter 2025 call its chief executive named it, with the approved turbine blade and more used material, as a lever for moving segment margins from the mid-30s toward 40%. <span class="cite">[MarketBeat coverage of the FTAI call, 26 Feb 2026]</span> Its duration, volumes, pricing and any effect on FTAI's use of alternative parts were not retrieved, and the release body itself was located and not read. <span class="cite">[D4 U14; D5 U18; cluster K-19]</span> Part VII §52 carries the agreement within the segment.
