## S1. The machine: a turbofan, its modules, the life limits that force it open, and the margin that erodes

Everything in this primer follows from four physical facts about one engine. It is built in separable sections. Inside them sit parts scrapped on a clock. Its hot section loses temperature margin as it wears. And it is only as usable as its paperwork.

<div class="defn"><b>Turbofan: fan, bypass and core</b><p>A turbofan is a jet engine with a large fan at the front. Most of the air the fan moves passes around the hot middle, the bypass stream; a small share goes through the middle, where fuel is burned and turbines are driven. The turbines spin the fan, and the fan does most of the pushing. <span class="cite">[Part I §1]</span></p></div>

<div class="defn"><b>CFM56 (-7B and -5B), the 737NG and A320ceo it powers, and the V2500 (the A320ceo's alternative engine)</b><p>Counts of CFM56s "in service" are about 23,000 (CFM website, undated), about 24,000 (Aviation Business News, Sept 2025) and more than 22,800 (Safran, end-2025); none says whether spares are included. The -7B is the Boeing 737NG's (Next Generation) only engine; the -5B powers about six in ten Airbus A320ceo (current engine option) aircraft, the rest carrying the V2500 from the IAE consortium. The last -7B was delivered by May 2019 and -5B production for airframes ended in 2022, so every CFM56 that flies tomorrow exists today. <span class="cite">[CFM; Aviation Business News; Safran, 13 Feb 2026; GE Aerospace, 2019; ISTAT (the International Society of Transport Aircraft Trading)]</span></p></div>

<div class="defn"><b>Original equipment manufacturer (OEM), and CFM International</b><p>The original equipment manufacturer (OEM) holds the regulator's approval of the engine's design. It is the sole source of new parts at its published catalogue price, controls the repair manual, and runs its own shops and service contracts. For the CFM56 the OEM is CFM International, the joint venture of GE Aerospace and Safran, described everywhere as 50/50: GE builds the core, Safran the fan, booster, low-pressure turbine and gearbox. The same venture makes the LEAP (CFM's new engine) for the A320neo and the 737 MAX (the re-engined 737). <span class="cite">[CFM website; Part I §2; Part IV §25]</span></p></div>

<div class="defn"><b>Module, and the core as the hot module</b><p>A module is a section of the engine built to be removed and replaced as a unit, bolted to its neighbours at flanges, with its own serial number and records. The CFM56 has three gas-path major modules, the fan and booster, the core and the low-pressure turbine (LPT), plus an accessory gearbox. The core is the hot, fast, expensive middle: the high-pressure compressor (HPC), the combustor and the high-pressure turbine (HPT). Sources count three to six modules depending on the level counted; FTAI's releases convert at three per engine ("450 modules (150 engines)", 26 Feb 2025). In FTAI's exchange business "core" also means the unit a customer hands back, called the "returned core" here. <span class="cite">[Aircraft Commerce Issue 58; FTAI release, 26 Feb 2025; Part I §3; reconciliation R-035]</span></p></div>

<div class="defn"><b>Life-limited part (LLP) and the engine flight cycle (EFC)</b><p>An engine flight cycle (EFC) is one take-off and landing; a mature short-haul narrowbody flies roughly 1,000 to 1,500 cycles a year (Aircraft Value News). A life-limited part (LLP) is a disk, spool or shaft whose failure the engine case cannot be designed to contain. It is certified for a fixed number of cycles and must be scrapped at that limit whatever its condition: 30,000 in the fan and booster, 20,000 in the compressor and high-pressure turbine, 25,000 in the low-pressure turbine on the -7B. These are the innermost parts, so reaching a limit forces the engine open. <span class="cite">[Aircraft Commerce Issue 34; records mini-packs; Aircraft Value News]</span></p></div>

<div class="defn"><b>Exhaust-gas temperature (EGT) margin</b><p>Every engine has a certified exhaust-gas temperature (EGT) red line. EGT margin is the gap between that limit and the temperature the engine reaches at full take-off thrust on a hot day. As blade tips and seals wear the engine runs hotter and the margin shrinks; at zero the engine must come off, and restoring the high-pressure turbine's blades, vanes and seals recovers most of it. <span class="cite">[Aircraft Commerce Issue 27; Part I §5]</span></p></div>

<div class="defn"><b>Time on wing</b><p>Time on wing is the interval between an engine's installation and its removal, set by whichever clock runs out first: the life-limited part with the fewest cycles remaining or the temperature margin. A mature CFM56 has a performance restoration every 10,000 to 15,000 cycles and a full overhaul with life-limited-part replacement at 20,000 to 25,000 (Leeham, Mar 2024): roughly seven to fifteen years between restorations at 1,000 to 1,500 cycles a year. <span class="cite">[Leeham News, 8 Mar 2024; Part I §5–§6]</span></p></div>

<div class="worked"><div class="h">Worked example S.1 — The shorter clock governs</div>
<p>Inputs: a CFM56-7B whose high-pressure turbine disk has used 18,500 of its 20,000 cycles (limit from the mini-packs; cycles used assumed); 1,500 cycles a year (top of Aircraft Value News's range); temperature margin for another 5,000 cycles (assumed).</p>
<div class="eqblock">cycles remaining on the disk = 20,000 − 18,500 = 1,500
time to the limit = 1,500 ÷ 1,500 a year = about one year
margin remaining = 5,000 cycles, worth nothing to this owner</div>
<p>The disk sets the removal date at about a year, and 5,000 cycles of margin go unused. On a fresh disk and a worn hot section the margin governs instead, and the disk's life goes into the shop unused. Two inputs are assumed; the point is the rule. <span class="cite">[Part I worked example 1.1]</span></p></div>

Three consequences follow. An engine at a shop visit is really three or four assets with different remaining lives. The quickest return to service is often to replace the one module that has run out. And the usable supply is the documented supply: a module with a gap in its records cannot be installed. The fleet behind all of this only shrinks, since every engine retired, torn down or converted leaves the pool and nothing enters it. <span class="cite">[Part I §3–§4, §9]</span>

## S2. What a shop visit is and costs, and why the parts bill is where the money sits

When either clock runs out, the engine comes off and goes to a shop. The bill has five lines, and the mix among them decides most of what follows.

<div class="defn"><b>Shop visit</b><p>A shop visit is any removal of the engine for work in an engine shop, from a visit to fix one finding to a complete rebuild. The shop disassembles the engine, inspects every piece, replaces or repairs what is out of limits, rebuilds it and runs it in a test cell before release. "Teardown" is reserved here for dismantling an engine to sell its parts. <span class="cite">[Part I §6]</span></p></div>

<div class="defn"><b>Maintenance, repair and overhaul (MRO), and the independent MRO</b><p>MRO is the industry's generic term for maintenance, repair and overhaul work and the companies that do it. An independent MRO is an engine shop not owned by the engine's manufacturer: a stand-alone company such as StandardAero, an airline's maintenance arm, or another engine maker's shop such as MTU (a German engine maker). All work to the manufacturer's manual. <span class="cite">[Part I §6; Part IV §25]</span></p></div>

<div class="defn"><b>Workscope, performance restoration and overhaul</b><p>The workscope specifies which modules are opened, how deep, and which parts are replaced new, repaired or swapped for used material. A performance restoration opens the core to recover temperature margin; the fan and low-pressure turbine are inspected but not fully disassembled. An overhaul disassembles every module and replaces the life-limited parts that would fall due in the next run ("zero-time": a complete new set). A hospital or quick-turn visit fixes one thing and returns the engine; a module exchange is one form of it. <span class="cite">[Part I §6; registry C-7]</span></p></div>

<div class="defn"><b>Turnaround time (TAT) and the induction slot</b><p>Turnaround time (TAT) runs from an engine's induction into a shop to its release: 90 to 120 days for a full CFM56 overhaul in 2025 against about 60 before the pandemic (trade press); "sustaining about 90 days" in GE's shops (May 2026). The induction slot is the date a shop commits to start; when shops are full the wait for one, two to six months (Bain, 2024), is additional to the turnaround, and every day away needs a spare engine or grounds an aircraft. <span class="cite">[Aviation Business News, 2025; GE Aerospace, 27 May 2026; Safe Fly Aviation, 2026 (consultancy blog); Bain, 2024]</span></p></div>

<div class="defn"><b>The shop-visit cost build</b><p>A visit's cost is labour, new OEM parts, repairs, life-limited-part replacement (new or used) and the test-cell run, plus freight, consumables and the shop's margin. Material is 60 to 70% of the total, labour 20 to 30% and repairs 10 to 20% (Air Cargo Week); a consultancy blog adds that life-limited parts are 40 to 60% of parts cost when replaced and high-pressure turbine blades and vanes 15 to 25%. <span class="cite">[Air Cargo Week, 2025/26; Safe Fly Aviation, 2026; Part I §7]</span></p></div>

<div class="defn"><b>Catalogue list price and escalation</b><p>The catalogue list price is the manufacturer's published price for every new spare part, repriced at least annually; shops pass it through at list plus a handling margin, and used and alternative parts are priced as discounts to it. Escalation is the annual increase: around 10% a year on new spares in 2022 and 2023 (Aviation Week); Safran's chief executive described pricing "above inflation by 3 to 4 points"; and the published price of a full -7B life-limited-part set compounded at about 7% a year from 2008 to 2025, derived in the research. <span class="cite">[Aviation Week, late 2023; Part I worked example 1.3; Part IV §28; reconciliation R-018]</span></p></div>

<div class="worked"><div class="h">Worked example S.2 — Stub life, and why the life-limited-part decision outweighs the whole restoration</div>
<p>Inputs: an engine arrives for a restoration at 12,000 cycles (assumed); its core parts have a 20,000-cycle limit (sourced), so 8,000 remain; the next run is planned at 12,000 cycles (assumed, inside Leeham's range). A full new set lists at $5.7M (MyAirTrade, June 2025, scope not visible) or about $4.0M (Aircraft Value News, Nov 2019). The restoration costs $2.15M or $1.4M, the midpoints of the two consultancy ranges.</p>
<div class="eqblock">keep the parts: the engine comes off again after 8,000 cycles, forced by the limit, with its restoration two-thirds used
replace them new now: 8,000 of each part's 20,000 cycles (40%) are scrapped unused
cost per cycle, full life used:   5,700,000 ÷ 20,000 = $285   (at $4.0M: $200)
cost per cycle, scrapped at 12,000: 5,700,000 ÷ 12,000 = $475   (at $4.0M: $333)
restoration alone, per cycle of the next run: 2,150,000 ÷ 12,000 = $179   (at $1.4M: $117)</div>
<p>The shorter remaining lives already inside the engine, the "stub life" (Aircraft Commerce), add a third to a half to the cost of every cycle if the parts are scrapped early, a penalty larger than the whole restoration on either set price. The third option, which the used-parts market supplies, is used life-limited parts with 12,000 to 15,000 cycles remaining; their prices were not retrieved. <span class="cite">[Aircraft Commerce Issue 34; Part I worked examples 1.2 and 1.6; reconciliation R-016, R-017]</span></p></div>

The parts bill is where the money sits for three reasons. Material is 60 to 70% of a visit and is priced off the catalogue, so escalation flows into every visit whoever performs it. Life-limited parts are 40 to 60% of parts cost when replaced and no alternative maker produces one for the CFM56, so that line is single-source. And the high-pressure turbine's blades and vanes, the next largest line, sit where most of the lost margin is recovered (S8). A shop's levers against the manufacturer are therefore all on the material line: used parts, approved alternative parts, repair instead of replacement, and exchange of a module instead of overhaul of the engine. <span class="cite">[Part I §7; Part VI §38]</span>

## S3. Where the money sits across the chain: a first margin map, bases stated, nothing ranked

Before the chain goes further, its links and the profit each reports give the segment sections a frame. This is a map, not an analysis.

<div class="defn"><b>Margin pool</b><p>The margin pool is this primer's term for how the profit earned in keeping an engine type flying is distributed across the links of the chain: the manufacturer, the shops, the repairers, the alternative-parts makers, the used-material traders, the lessors and the servicers of third-party capital. No company reports a "margin pool"; the term names a way of reading the filings together. <span class="cite">[Part I §11; Part XI §86]</span></p></div>

<div class="defn"><b>FTAI's two reportable segments: Aerospace Products and Aviation Leasing</b><p>Aerospace Products "develops and manufactures, repairs/refurbishes, and sells aircraft engines and aftermarket components primarily for the CFM56-7B, CFM56-5B, and V2500" through its Module Factory (S9); FY2025 segment revenue was $1,936.2M. Aviation Leasing "owns and manages aircraft and engines and leases and sells them, directly and through an equity-method investment"; it held 290 assets at 31 December 2025 (S10). FTAI Power appears as a third line in the 2027 guidance; whether it is a reportable segment was not confirmed (S11). <span class="cite">[FTAI 10-K FY2025; FTAI release, 29 Jul 2026; Part VII §45; Part VIII §58]</span></p></div>

<div class="defn"><b>Adjusted EBITDA (consolidated and by segment) and guidance</b><p>Adjusted EBITDA is earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortisation as FTAI adjusts them: items treated as non-recurring or non-cash are removed and the company's pro-rata share of Adjusted EBITDA from its equity-method investments is added back. FTAI reports it consolidated ($597.3M in 2023, $862.1M in 2024, $1,190.9M in 2025) and by segment (Aerospace Products $160.0M, $380.6M, $671.3M; Aviation Leasing $500.1M and $608.9M for 2024 and 2025), and sets guidance in the segment measure. The segments do not sum to the total (Corporate and Other about −$89.2M in 2025). <span class="cite">[FTAI 10-K FY2025; Part X §80, §84]</span></p></div>

Three cautions travel with the table. The margins sit on five bases: an EBITDA margin exceeds an EBIT margin by the depreciation share of revenue, about nine points at FTAI in 2025 ($225.8M on $2,507.4M). A share of cost is not a margin. And a parts pass-through compresses a percentage: a shop billing $2M of the manufacturer's parts at a small margin beside $1M of its own labour shows a thin percentage, a parts maker a wide one, and the dollars may be similar. FTAI's segment sells a module or engine it owns, so its margin is built more like a parts business's than a shop's. <span class="cite">[FTAI 10-K FY2025; Part X §80; Part XI §86.9]</span>

One structural fact belongs on the map. Safran describes about 85% of the CFM56's aftermarket revenue as spare parts and time-and-materials work, against about 40% for the LEAP, and about 15% of the CFM56 fleet is under long-term flight-hour agreements against about 50% of the LEAP fleet. On this engine the manufacturer earns mainly by selling parts at list into visits it does not perform or control, which leaves room for every substitute in S8 and S9; on a contracted engine the manufacturer chooses the parts. <span class="cite">[Safran via Aviation Week, c. 2023/24; Part IV §28]</span>

## S4. Who owns engines and how they pay: leases, reserves, engine values, green time

Almost every dollar an engine earns passes through a lease, and almost every dollar its owner borrows is secured on that lease. The lease is where the life an engine consumes is turned into cash, and where its value is written down part by part.

<div class="defn"><b>Operating lease</b><p>An operating lease rents an aircraft or engine from its owner, the lessor, to an operator, the lessee, for a fixed term and periodic rent; the lessor keeps title and the risk that the asset is worth more or less than expected when it comes back. Nearly all of FTAI's leases are operating leases. At the end of the term the lessor extends, re-leases, sells or parts out. <span class="cite">[FTAI 10-K FY2025; Part II §12]</span></p></div>

<div class="defn"><b>Maintenance reserves</b><p>Maintenance reserves are payments the lessee makes in addition to rent, set per flight hour or cycle and collected per component (each engine's restoration and life-limited parts, the airframe's checks, the landing gear). They pre-fund the eventual shop visit; the lessor reimburses a completed event up to the balance held and keeps any unclaimed balance at lease end. FTAI books reserves it no longer has to return as "maintenance revenue" ($218.5M in 2025). <span class="cite">[AerCap 20-F FY2013; FTAI 10-K FY2025; Part II §13]</span></p></div>

<div class="defn"><b>Return conditions</b><p>Return conditions are the contractual minimum state of a leased engine at redelivery: minimum hours and cycles to the next restoration, minimum cycles remaining on each life-limited part, and records in order. With the reserve regime they decide who pays for the life consumed. They may also exclude parts and repairs that are not the manufacturer's; current practice on that clause was not found. <span class="cite">[Part II §13; Part VI §42]</span></p></div>

<div class="defn"><b>Lease rate factor (LRF)</b><p>The lease rate factor (LRF) is monthly rent divided by the asset's market value; 1.0% a month is a 12% gross annual yield before depreciation, interest and maintenance. No source states a factor for FTAI, for Willis Lease or for the CFM56; the primer's figures are derived: 0.70% a month (mixed bases), 0.87 to 1.1% (FTAI's 2025 book), about 1.0% (Willis Lease), and 0.9% assumed in one fee example. <span class="cite">[Part II §12, §14; reconciliation R-030]</span></p></div>

<div class="defn"><b>Half-life value and maintenance-adjusted value</b><p>Half-life is an appraisal convention, not a physical midpoint: the value of an engine with every module halfway between restorations and every life-limited part at half its life, so that no adjustment has been made for actual maintenance status (ISTAT); IBA (an appraiser) publishes CFM56 values on that basis. Maintenance-adjusted value adds, for each item, the actual remaining fraction less one half, times the cost to restore it. <span class="cite">[ISTAT Jetrader, 2010; IBA, Sept 2025; Part I §8]</span></p></div>

<div class="defn"><b>Green time</b><p>Green time is the usable life left on an engine or module before its next mandatory shop visit, set by whichever runs out first: the shortest-lived part, the temperature margin or a hard inspection. A green-time engine is one the owner will not restore, flown or leased as-is and then torn down. The pool is fed by retirements, so it shrinks exactly when a slow-retiring fleet most wants it. <span class="cite">[Part II §16; registry C-2]</span></p></div>

Two disagreements in the exhibit reach every later Part. On rent, the appraiser's $100,000 a month and the consultancy's $42,000 to $48,000 agree on the rise since 2019, about a third, and disagree on the level; the lower figure sits beside green-time values and may price a part-worn engine. Two -7B spare engines at the appraiser's rent would command $200,000 a month, 88% of the $228,000 a 12-year-old 737-800 earned in July 2025 (IBA); at the consultancy's rent, $84,000 to $96,000, 37 to 42%. The first mixes 2024 and 2025 rents and the second may price part-worn engines, so both are carried, but neither disputes the direction: the engines are separable, their value is dominated by maintenance condition, no new CFM56 is being built, and a 15-to-20-year-old airframe has few other uses. FTAI states the consequence: it "owns and leases jet aircraft which often facilitates the acquisition of engines at attractive prices". <span class="cite">[IBA, Apr 2024 and Jul 2025; Safe Fly Aviation, 2026; FTAI Q2 2026 release; Part II worked example 2.6]</span>

<div class="defn"><b>Special-purpose vehicle (SPV), asset-backed securitization (ABS) and warehouse facility</b><p>A special-purpose vehicle (SPV) is a bankruptcy-remote entity that owns only a defined pool of assets and the debt secured on them. An asset-backed securitization (ABS) sells leased aircraft or engines to such a vehicle, which issues notes in tranches paid in order of seniority; the sponsor usually keeps the equity and services the leases for a fee, and rating agencies size the senior notes by stressing rents, downtime, maintenance and residual values, so an appraiser's note that values have levelled off reaches the debt a pool can carry. A warehouse facility is a revolving bank loan to an SPV that funds assets until there are enough to securitize. FTAI's Strategic Capital Initiative (SCI) vehicles use all three (S10); on 22 May 2026 the first of them priced FTAI MRE 2026-1, $612M of notes on 48 A320ceo and 737NG aircraft on lease to 23 airlines, with Series A expected to be rated Asf by Fitch and A(sf) by KBRA (a rating agency) and Series B in the BBB (triple-B) category, BBB+sf, by Fitch. <span class="cite">[FTAI release, 22 May 2026, via Finviz and AviTrader; Part II §17]</span></p></div>

## S5. Why 2026 looks the way it does: the fleet that only shrinks, retirements that did not come, new engines that did not relieve, airframers that did not deliver

Nothing in S1 to S4 changes between a quiet year and a tight one; what changes is the quantities. Between 2019 and 2026 four things moved them the same way.

<div class="defn"><b>Installed base, retirement rate and the shop-visit forecast</b><p>The installed base is the population of engines fitted to aircraft in the operating fleet; the three CFM56 counts of S1 may include spare and stored engines, and a consultancy blog's about 14,200 "active" and more than 19,000 "flying" are labelled consultancy figures and never the fleet figure. The retirement rate is the share of a fleet withdrawn in a year: GE's planning assumption for 2026 moved from 3–4% of the base, to 2–3%, to 1.5–2% (GE CFO via Aviation Week, 2026); GE reported about 1.5% retired in 2025 and expected about 2% in 2026 (via Leeham, 22 Jan 2026); first-quarter 2026 retirements ran "below 1%" (GE investor-relations page). The shop-visit forecast counts expected inductions a year: 2,300 to 2,400 CFM56 visits a year in 2026 to 2028 with the apex around 2027 to 2028 and a gradual fade (GE via Aviation Week, 2026), against Safran's earlier peak of about 2,500 in 2025 to 2026 (c. 2023/24). <span class="cite">[Part III §18, §19, §23; reconciliation R-001, R-004, R-006]</span></p></div>

Because no new -5B or -7B is built, the base falls by exactly the number retired or converted each year, and an engine that does not retire both accumulates cycles toward the visits of S2 and withholds its parts from the used-material market: the same number sits on both sides of the aftermarket.

The fleet count is contested, and every per-engine figure in the chain divides by it: none of the three manufacturer-level figures states its basis, and the consultancy's 14,200 was read in the research both as the whole active fleet and as the -7B fleet alone. <span class="cite">[reconciliation R-001, R-002]</span> The retirement record is contested twice over. GE's planning number for 2026 fell three times within a year and the realised rate fell further, with the two venues differing by half a point on the 2026 expectation. At aircraft level an appraiser said fewer than 200 aircraft retired in 2024 and a data provider recorded 587, with the scope visible in neither; arithmetic on other sources gives about 410 and about 1,130 a year, and Cirium's own series ran 502, 465, 333 and 325 for 2019 to 2022. <span class="cite">[GE CFO via Aviation Week, 2026; Leeham, 22 Jan 2026; IBA via AviTrader, 24 Sep 2024; Cirium via Informa and via Aerodynamic Advisory, IATA (the airlines' trade body) conference, 2023; reconciliation R-004, R-005]</span>

<div class="worked"><div class="h">Worked example S.5 — The gap between the planned and the realised retirement rate, in engines</div>
<p>Inputs: a base of about 24,000 (Aviation Business News, Sept 2025) or more than 22,800 (Safran, end-2025); rates at the midpoints of GE's ranges, 3.5% for "3–4%" and 1.75% for "1.5–2%" (midpoints are the primer's choice); the 2025 actual of about 1.5% and 2026 expectation of about 2% (GE via Leeham).</p>
<div class="eqblock">on 24,000: 3.5% = 840 engines a year; 1.75% = 420; 1.5% = 360; 2.0% = 480; below 1% = fewer than 240

<p>About 400 engines a year on any manufacturer-level base, roughly 1,200 over three years, are still flying that the manufacturer's first plan had retired: each is a future shop visit and a teardown that did not happen. The three-year figure is an upper bound. <span class="cite">[Part III worked example 3.2]</span></p></div>

Why did so few retire? An aircraft is retired when its next heavy event costs more than the aircraft is worth kept, and when a replacement is available. From 2023 to 2026 the second condition failed: an airline with no new aircraft pays for the visit rather than shrink, and a lessor whose lessee cannot find a replacement extends. Cirium's count that only about a dozen mid-life 737NGs and fewer than 25 A320ceos were available to rent anywhere measures how closed the gate was. <span class="cite">[Cirium Ascend via Bloomberg; Part III §19]</span>

<div class="defn"><b>The context shock: the geared turbofan (GTF) powder-metal issue, LEAP durability and its kit, and the 737 MAX rate cap</b><p>Three failures of replacement kept the old fleet flying. Pratt & Whitney's geared turbofan (GTF) on the A320neo had turbine discs made from contaminated metal powder, disclosed by RTX (Pratt & Whitney's parent) in 2023; 835 GTF-powered A320neos were grounded or stored in late 2025 on Cirium's count against RTX's planning figure of about 350, and aircraft-on-ground (AOG) levels were down more than 20% from the 2025 peak by January 2026. CFM's LEAP came off wing early, at 2,000 to 6,000 cycles, in hot and harsh environments; CFM certified a LEAP-1B durability kit with the Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) on 18 July 2026, with production cutover expected in early 2027. And the FAA capped 737 production at 38 a month from January 2024, raising it to 42 in October 2025 and completing the review to 47 in May 2026, against a 2018 peak of 52; Airbus delivered 793 aircraft in 2025 against a target of 820 and expects A320neo rate 75 only by the end of 2027. <span class="cite">[Cirium via The Flying Engineer, 2026; Leeham, 27 Jan 2026; Visual Approach; CFM release, 18 Jul 2026; TravelPulse, Oct 2025; CAPA (the Centre for Aviation), May 2026; Wikipedia compilation; Argus Media; Air Data News; Part III §20–§21]</span></p></div>

The mechanism from each shock to the CFM56 is indirect and specific. A grounded A320neo needs its flying replaced by an A320ceo on the same pilot rating, which works the -5B and the V2500 harder, not the -7B. An early LEAP removal is a LEAP visit in the manufacturer's network, but an operator whose 737 MAX fleet is less available keeps its 737NGs, which touches the -7B. A cap of 38 a month against 52 holds back on the order of 170 737NG replacements a year at a one-for-one ceiling. Each shock carries its own reversal: the groundings were easing by January 2026, the LEAP-1B kit cuts over in early 2027, and the backlogs are owed rather than cancelled, so when rates rise the deferred retirements arrive with them. <span class="cite">[Part III §20.3, worked example 3.4]</span>

The lessors closed the loop. With 75 to 90% of expiries extended for four to six years, the maintenance events that would have fallen at a lease transition were paid by the incumbent airline inside the extended term, and only 10 to 25 aircraft of every 100 came back to the used market as acquisition candidates or teardown feedstock. The resulting forecast is contested between the two CFM partners, a peak of about 2,500 against a plateau of 2,300 to 2,400 through 2028, and either depends on a retirement rate that moved three times in a year. <span class="cite">[Cirium Ascend via Bloomberg; IBA, Nov 2023; Part III worked example 3.5; reconciliation R-006, R-007]</span>

## S7. The shop-visit market and the OEM's pricing: demand plateau, capacity shortage, list-price escalation, the OEM's own offerings

The market the engine creates has four kinds of shop, the manufacturer's network, the large independents, the airlines' maintenance arms and, since 2022, FTAI's Module Factory, and one price-setter, the manufacturer, which earns from nearly every visit without performing it.

Demand is a plateau whose height and length are contested. Safran's earlier framing had CFM56 visits peaking at about 2,500 a year in 2025 and 2026 and then falling; GE's 2026 framing has 2,300 to 2,400 a year, "probably closer to 2,400", held through 2028 with the apex around 2027 to 2028 and a gradual fade. The later view is lower at its top and later in reaching it; the CFM56 is about a quarter of all engine shop work by count (about 2,300 of about 9,000 visits in 2025). The shape moved with the retirement assumption, so the fade's start date is the least certain number in the chain. <span class="cite">[Safran and GE via Aviation Week; Aviation Week 2026 Fleet & MRO Forecast via ePlaneAI; reconciliation R-006, R-007]</span>

<div class="worked"><div class="h">Worked example S.7 — Reconciling 2,300 visits a year with the installed base</div>
<p>Inputs: 2,300 visits a year (GE via Aviation Week, 2026, low end); bases of about 24,000, more than 22,800, more than 19,000 "flying" and about 14,200 "active" (the last two consultancy, labelled); a mature restoration every 10,000–15,000 cycles at 1,000–1,500 cycles a year (Leeham; Aircraft Value News), roughly 6.7 to 15 years; spares at about 10% of installed engines (MTU).</p>
<div class="eqblock">on 24,000: 2,300 ÷ 24,000 = 9.6% a year → about 10.4 years between visits
on 22,800: 10.1% → 9.9 years;  on 19,000: 12.1% → 8.3 years;  on 14,200: 16.2% → 6.2 years
removing 10% spares from 24,000: 2,300 ÷ 21,600 = 10.6% → 9.4 years</div>
<p>An implied interval of about ten years matches the mature interval only at the long end of its range; about six years on the consultancy base is shorter than any sourced interval. So the 24,000 base is consistent with the sourced interval only if it includes a few thousand spare, stored or green-time engines, or if long first runs are depressing the visit rate: about 70% of the fleet had seen at most one shop visit in 2024 (Safran). Which base is right decides every per-engine figure in the chain, including FTAI's share of it. <span class="cite">[Part III worked example 3.6; reconciliation R-001, R-008]</span></p></div>

Capacity has two readings and both are carried. Trade press and consultancies describe a full system: overhauls at 90 to 120 days, slot waits of two to six months, demand set to exceed engine-maintenance capacity by more than 17% by the end of the decade (Bain, 2024), and two-thirds of operators short of technicians (Oliver Wyman, April 2026). GE's filing and conference material read the other way: turnaround for the LEAP, CFM56 and GE90 (a widebody engine) improved by more than 10% year on year in the fourth quarter of 2025, and CFM56 turnaround was "sustaining about 90 days" in May 2026. The consequence does not depend on which is right: a removal that returns in five to ten months ties up a spare for that long, so the shortage of slots is itself demand for spare engines and for anything that returns the aircraft to service in weeks, which is the exchange of S9. <span class="cite">[Aviation Business News, 2025; Bain, 2024; Oliver Wyman, Apr 2026; GE 10-K FY2025; GE Aerospace, 27 May 2026; reconciliation R-019, R-020]</span>

The manufacturer's model on this engine has four elements. Spare parts are sold to any shop at the published catalogue price, which escalated about 10% a year in 2022 and 2023 and, for -7B parts, 20 to 30% after August 2023 on one shop's account (Magnetic via Aviation Week); the 2024 to 2026 steps were not found. About 85% of CFM56 aftermarket revenue is transactional, bought visit by visit with the operator or its shop choosing the parts, against about 40% for the LEAP; the remainder sits under long-term service agreements (LTSAs), multi-year contracts at a rate per flight hour under which the manufacturer performs or pays for the visits and chooses the shop and the parts, covering about 15% of the CFM56 fleet and targeted at 60 to 70% of the LEAP fleet by 2030 (Safran via Aviation Week). GE sells the same services under TrueChoice, with Flight Hour, Overhaul, Material and Transitions lines, the last covering green-time leases, exchanges and material buy-back; GE says "GE and CFM are the largest used serviceable material provider for their products" and calls the CFM56 "an open network ... with multiple providers who compete". And it signs materials agreements with shops: FTAI's of 22 January 2026 "secures OEM replacement part supply, thrust performance upgrades and component repair", terms undisclosed. <span class="cite">[Aviation Week, late 2023, 2024 and c. 2023/24; GE Aerospace statements, 2026; FTAI release, 22 Jan 2026; Part IV §28; reconciliation R-009, R-018]</span>

Put the elements together and the manufacturer's capture on this engine is plain. Most visits are bought visit by visit; within each, 60 to 70% of the bill is material; most material is bought new at list. So the manufacturer receives the largest slice of nearly every CFM56 visit without performing it, and its escalation lands on that slice whoever runs the shop. The substitutes of S8 and the exchanges of S9 therefore address the 85% of the aftermarket that is transactional, and would address far less of a fleet contracted the way the LEAP is being contracted; the manufacturer is on the substitutes' side too, through TrueChoice and the January 2026 agreement. <span class="cite">[Part IV §28–§29]</span>

An operator whose engine is at its life limit has four options: a full visit with new life-limited parts after the slot wait and the turnaround; a module exchange (S9); a green-time engine leased to fly out the remaining months; or a sale of the engine for its parts and the purchase of another. The last three exist because the engine is modular and a secondary market exists, and low retirements shrink the supply behind all three just when the slot shortage makes the first slow and escalation makes it expensive. <span class="cite">[Part IV §30]</span>

## S8. The substitutes for new parts: used material from teardowns, approved alternative parts (PMA) and designated-representative (DER) repairs

Three things can replace a new part from the manufacturer: a used part from a dismantled engine, a new part made by someone else under a regulator's approval, and a repair that keeps the old part in service. All three are priced against the manufacturer's list, and all three depend on a supply or an approval the manufacturer does not control.

<div class="defn"><b>Used serviceable material (USM)</b><p>Used serviceable material (USM) is any part that has been in service, removed, inspected and if necessary repaired to the manual, and released on an airworthiness tag with its full history; a part with a gap in its paperwork is scrap. Used material trades at 60 to 80% of a new part's price on average and above list when the manufacturer cannot deliver (Aviation Week); a specialist trader sells repaired engine material to an airline at 70 to 75% of list (Aircraft Commerce). Because the base is the catalogue, the used price rises with every revision even if the discount does not move. <span class="cite">[Aviation Week, undated; Aircraft Commerce Issue 120; Part V §31, §34; reconciliation R-022]</span></p></div>

<div class="defn"><b>Teardown, part-out and feedstock</b><p>Part-out is the owner's decision to dismantle a retired aircraft or engine and sell its components; teardown is the disassembly. Feedstock is the trade's word for the aircraft and engines bought to be torn down; FTAI's filings extend it to the unserviceable module a customer returns in an exchange and to engine inventory held for module production. A teardown module is valued like an engine in miniature: parts at used-material discounts, plus the life-limited parts' remaining life (life fraction times list, discounted), less the repair to make it serviceable. Supply is a lagged function of retirements. <span class="cite">[Part V §32–§33, §36; registry C-14]</span></p></div>

A run-out CFM56 bought for $0.8 to $1.2M yields $1.6 to $2.2M of used material (Safe Fly Aviation, 2026, consultancy blog), 25 to 39% of a half-life -7B's $5.7M to $6.4M (IBA, Sept 2025); no owner tears down a $6M engine for $2M of parts, so engines with life are sold or leased whole and only run-out engines reach the teardown market. Gross retirements on the manufacturer-level bases are roughly 340 to 480 engines a year (1.5 to 2% of 22,800 to 24,000), of which an unknown share is torn down: some are sold whole, some leased for green time first, and from 2026 some earmarked as power-generation cores (S11). <span class="cite">[Part V worked examples 5.1 and 5.4; Part II §16]</span>

The 2023 to 2026 shortage follows in the steps the sources describe: the retirement wave did not arrive, would-be feedstock was leased as green time or overhauled, teardown companies competed for a smaller pool, and prices rose, with CFM56 values up "as much as 50%" in two years on one trade-press account, "a lessors' market" (IBA, April 2024), "a seller's market for used parts" (Aviation Week) and AerSale's whole-asset sales down from 20 engines in 2024 to 13 in 2025 in "a hypercompetitive feedstock environment". <span class="cite">[ePlaneAI; IBA via AviTrader, 25 Apr 2024; Aviation Week; AerSale, 5 Mar 2026; Part V §33]</span> FTAI sits on both sides of the trade: it buys engines and whole aircraft for their material, and it sells the used modules and parts it does not build into modules through an exclusive partnership the annual report does not name; AAR (a parts distributor and maintenance company) announced on 27 March 2025 that the two had extended their exclusive Serviceable Engine Products agreement for CFM56 material through 2030, covering more than 450 engines as trade coverage carried it; the agreement's terms were not read. <span class="cite">[FTAI 10-K FY2025; AAR via PRNewswire, 27 Mar 2025, body as carried by ePlaneAI; Part V §33.6, §35]</span>

<div class="defn"><b>Parts Manufacturer Approval (PMA)</b><p>A Parts Manufacturer Approval (PMA) is the FAA's combined design-and-production approval that lets a company other than the type-certificate holder legally make and sell a replacement part, with no access to the manufacturer's data. Approvals have concentrated in consumables and simple parts. A cooled, coated high-pressure turbine blade is the hard case: its life depends on an alloy, a crystal structure and a coating the applicant must reproduce and prove in an engine test programme it funds itself. Chromalloy's approval of a CFM56-5B/7B blade on 30 October 2025, "the only aftermarket alternative to the OEM manufactured new part", is treated in the trade as that milestone. No PMA exists for any CFM56 life-limited part. <span class="cite">[eCFR, 14 CFR (Code of Federal Regulations) Part 21 Subpart K; HEICO 10-K FY2025; Chromalloy release, 30 Oct 2025; Part VI §37–§38, §41; reconciliation R-024]</span></p></div>

<div class="defn"><b>Designated Engineering Representative (DER) repair</b><p>A Designated Engineering Representative (DER) is an engineer appointed by the FAA to approve engineering data on its behalf; a DER repair is a major repair done to data a DER approved rather than to the manufacturer's manual, so the part stays the manufacturer's part and what the manual would scrap is restored. The cadence decides which lever applies: at a first visit, around 20,000 cycles, airlines typically repair the turbine blades; at a second, around 30,000, they cannot be repaired again and a new part is needed, which is where an approved alternative blade competes. <span class="cite">[FAA Order 8110.37F; In Practise, undated; Part VI §40]</span></p></div>

## S9. FTAI's answer: the Module Factory and the Aerospace Products segment

FTAI's module business is the fourth lever of S2 organised as a production line. It rebuilds modules before any customer engine arrives, holds them in inventory, and sells them into engines whose one expired module would otherwise send the whole engine through a 90-to-120-day visit after a two-to-six-month wait.

<div class="defn"><b>Module Factory</b><p>The Module Factory is FTAI's name for its CFM56-7B and -5B modular repair and refurbishment programme, first described in 2022 at a 500,000-square-foot Montréal facility "with a capacity for up to 300 shop visits per year" and bought for $170.0M on 9 September 2024. It is a place, a programme, and a process that builds and holds an inventory of serviceable modules for exchange, fed by returned cores, engines retired from FTAI's leasing fleet and the capital vehicles, and engines and aircraft bought for the purpose. <span class="cite">[FTAI 10-K FY2025; FTAI release, 9 Sep 2024; Part VII §46]</span></p></div>

<div class="defn"><b>Module exchange</b><p>A module exchange separates a customer's engine at the flanges, removes the module that triggered the removal, installs a serviceable module from inventory, tests the engine and returns it. The customer pays an exchange fee and gives up the unserviceable module, the returned core, which becomes FTAI's feedstock. The industry prices an exchange as the fee plus the difference in life-limited-part life between the modules swapped. Under US revenue rules the returned core is noncash consideration at fair value, so FTAI books revenue equal to the fee plus the core's value and carries the core in inventory until it is sold again. No provider's exchange fee, FTAI's included, was found in any source. <span class="cite">[Part VII §46–§47; reconciliation cluster K-01]</span></p></div>

<div class="defn"><b>Maintenance, Repair and Exchange (MRE), and MRE Contract revenue</b><p>MRE is FTAI's name for its offer, a contrast with MRO: instead of restoring a customer's engine while the customer waits, FTAI swaps in a serviceable module or engine and takes the unserviceable unit as feedstock. It is sold as engine, module and parts sales on the "Aerospace products revenue" line ($455.0M, $1,079.8M and $1,600.5M for 2023 to 2025), not split further in any retrieved filing. MRE Contract revenue is a second line, new in 2025, for sales to the vehicles of the 2025 Partnership that FTAI manages and part-owns: $335.8M for 2025 and $221.2M, $182.8M and $404.0M for the first quarter, second quarter and half of 2026. Two filings define it differently: the Q3 2025 10-Q describes "stand-ready obligations to provide replacement CFM56-5B and CFM56-7B engines to customers as they become unserviceable", and the Q2 2026 10-Q "the transaction price for sales of CFM56-5B, CFM56-7B and V2500 engines and related modules to the SPVs of the first SCI partnership"; one is an obligation and the other a sale, and both are carried. <span class="cite">[FTAI 10-K FY2025; FTAI 10-Qs Q3 2025 and Q2 2026; Part VII §45, §48; reconciliation R-044]</span></p></div>

## S10. FTAI's capital: Aviation Leasing, the Strategic Capital vehicles, the money that flows between them, and the 2026 securitization

The other half of FTAI owns things. Aviation Leasing held 290 assets at 31 December 2025, 47 aircraft and 243 engines, with utilization of about 77% on the company's equity-weighted definition. Its 2025 lines were lease income of $235.2M, maintenance revenue of $218.5M and asset sales of $106.9M against segment Adjusted EBITDA of $608.9M, so rent cannot account for the earnings; maintenance revenue, asset-sale margins, a $46.4M gain on sales to the first capital vehicle and the equity-method share must. <span class="cite">[FTAI 10-K FY2025; Part VIII §58]</span> The aircraft is bought for its two engines (S4), so the book is both rent and a feedstock pipeline for S9; in 2025 the company began selling it into vehicles funded by others while keeping three roles in them.

<div class="defn"><b>Strategic Capital Initiative (SCI): the 2025 Partnership, the 2026 SPV and the Seed Assets</b><p>The Strategic Capital Initiative (SCI), launched 30 December 2024, raises third-party capital into closed-end vehicles that buy on-lease mid-life 737NG and A320ceo aircraft; FTAI manages each as Servicer, co-invests as a limited partner, and supplies every engine shop visit for the fleet through exchanges with its MRE business. The 2025 Partnership closed on 27 October 2025 at a $2.0B equity hard cap with a $2.5B asset-level debt commitment, and by 17 August 2026 had "approximately $6.0 billion of total capital across over 300 aircraft", fully committed and in "harvest phase". The Seed Assets are the 46 on-lease aircraft FTAI sold from its own book to start it, "at an estimated net purchase price of $549 million". The 2026 SPV closed a $2.0B warehouse facility with an accordion to $3.0B on 14 August 2026; its equity and FTAI's stake were undisclosed. <span class="cite">[FTAI releases, 30 Dec 2024, 26 Feb 2025, 27 Oct 2025, 17 Aug 2026; FTAI 10-K FY2025; Part VIII §60–§62; reconciliation R-055]</span></p></div>

<div class="defn"><b>Servicer and servicing fee</b><p>The Servicer manages a vehicle's assets for a fee: leasing and remarketing the aircraft, collecting rent and reserves, overseeing condition and selling. FTAI "as the Servicer, provides aircraft management services to the 2025 Partnership, and receives customary, market-based compensation": $10.2M for 2025, $6.99M for the second quarter of 2026 and $12.85M for the first half. The fee's rate, its base and the income-statement line it sits on are not disclosed. <span class="cite">[FTAI 10-K FY2025; FTAI 10-Q Q2 2026; Part VIII §61, §63]</span></p></div>

<div class="defn"><b>Equity method and profit elimination</b><p>Under the equity method an investor with significant influence but not control carries its stake at cost plus its share of earnings, less distributions and eliminated profit, on one line, with none of the investee's revenue, assets or debt consolidated. FTAI's stake in the 2025 Partnership is 19% in the FY2025 10-K and the 2026 10-Qs and 20% in the Q3 2025 10-Q. Profit elimination removes from current earnings the profit on sales to such an investee and recognises it later: "the profit from the MRE Contract revenue is eliminated through equity method earnings and will be recognized over time as the 2025 Partnership generates income". Eliminations were $10.0M in the first quarter of 2026 and $6.6M in the second, $16.6M for the half; whether that is 19% of the profit or all of it is not stated. <span class="cite">[FTAI 10-Qs Q3 2025, Q1 and Q2 2026; FTAI 10-K FY2025; Part VIII §63–§64; reconciliation R-047, R-048]</span></p></div>

<div class="defn"><b>Asset-light model and harvest phase</b><p>"Asset-light" is management's term for owning fewer leased assets directly and earning servicing fees, maintenance work and equity income from vehicles that own them; the July 2026 cut to Leasing guidance was explained as "reflecting a continued shift to an asset-light business model". Harvest phase is the period after a closed-end vehicle has finished buying, in which it collects rent, pays distributions and sells assets until it winds up; the 2025 Partnership's term and exit route were not located. <span class="cite">[FTAI releases, 29 Jul 2026 and 17 Aug 2026; FTAI 10-Q Q2 2026; Part VIII §60–§61; reconciliation R-058]</span></p></div>

## S11. FTAI Power: a second life for cores, the J&F order, the market it sells into

The newest business removes CFM56 engines from aviation for good. It sits on the same side of the ledger as retirement and teardown, and competes with both for the same run-out cores.

<div class="defn"><b>FTAI Power: Mod-1, heat rate, behind-the-meter bridging power, and J&F Power Systems</b><p>FTAI Power, launched 30 December 2025, converts CFM56 engines into aeroderivative generator sets: the fan is removed and replaced by a small compressor, a power turbine and generator are fitted, and the fuel system is changed for gas. Mod-1 is its 25 megawatt (MW) trailer-mounted unit, described in call coverage as 35 to 40% efficient at a heat rate (fuel energy per kilowatt-hour) of about 9,000 and as giving "near-fully depreciated" engines a 10-to-20-year second life; the release-level statements are capacity for "over 100 units annually", first delivery by the fourth quarter of 2026 and 100 units in 2027. Behind-the-meter (BTM) generation is power made on the customer's side of the utility meter; bridging power is temporary on-site generation until a grid connection arrives. J&F Power Systems, the venture with Jereh Group (a Chinese maker of trailer-mounted gas-turbine packages) that packages and sells Mod-1, is "FTAI's joint venture" to FTAI and "its subsidiary" in Jereh's exchange disclosure as reported; the ownership split and consolidating party are undisclosed. On 22 July 2026 J&F received a five-year master supply agreement and an initial $1.465 billion purchase order from "a leading international cloud service provider" (FTAI's release) or "a U.S. hyperscaler" (call summaries), for delivery in batches through November 2027; the unit count was not disclosed. <span class="cite">[FTAI releases, 30 Dec 2025 and 22 Jul 2026; FTAI 10-Q Q2 2026; TheValueist and Aviation Week, call coverage, 26 Feb 2026; FilingReader, 22 Jul 2026; Part IX §68–§71; reconciliation R-061]</span></p></div>

The market is defined by a shortage the sources date. The International Energy Agency expects global data-center electricity use to more than double to about 945 TWh by 2030. GE Vernova's gas-turbine backlog plus slot reservations rose from 50 GW in 2025 to 116 GW at the second quarter of 2026, with slots sold for 2031 delivery, and Siemens Energy quotes lead times of three years and more. A buyer that needs power in 2026 or 2027 cannot get a new frame turbine; that is the opening for a 25 MW unit built from a used airline engine, as for piston plants and fuel cells. The 2027 guidance puts FTAI Power's Adjusted EBITDA at $450M in the release text; one call summary gives "$450 million to $750 million", and both are carried. <span class="cite">[IEA, Apr 2025; Power Engineering, Jul 2026; Oilprice, 2026; FTAI release, 29 Jul 2026; GuruFocus, 30 Jul 2026; Part IX §71; reconciliation R-062]</span>

## S12. The company, its numbers and what remains unknown

<div class="defn"><b>Internalization, and the company in one paragraph</b><p>FTAI Aviation Ltd. is a Cayman Islands company listed on Nasdaq. It began as Fortress Transportation and Infrastructure Investors LLC, listed on the New York Stock Exchange on 15 May 2015 and externally managed by a Fortress Investment Group subsidiary; it spun off its infrastructure businesses on 1 August 2022 and became a Cayman parent on 10 November 2022. Internalization is the termination of an external management agreement, the hiring of the manager's people, and a payment for the fee stream given up; FTAI's closed on 28 May 2024 for a stated $300.0M, $150.0M in cash paid on 17 June 2024 and 1,866,949 shares valued at $150.0M, so expense lines before and after mid-2024 are not like for like. <span class="cite">[FTAI 10-Q Q3 2024; FTAI 8-K, 28 May 2024; FTAI Infrastructure 10-K FY2024; Part X §75–§77]</span></p></div>

The 2025 consolidated statement shows the chain's money in one place. Revenues were $2,507.4M, of which Aerospace products $1,600.5M, MRE Contract $335.8M, lease income $235.2M, maintenance revenue $218.5M and asset sales $106.9M; cost of sales was $1,349.7M, depreciation and amortisation $225.8M and interest expense $247.8M; consolidated Adjusted EBITDA was $1,190.9M. The company reported net income in 2024 and 2025 and consumed cash in operations in both years (+$129.0M in 2023, −$188.0M in 2024, −$310.7M in 2025), because buying assets to sell is an operating outflow while buying assets to lease is an investing outflow: a factory holding modules ahead of demand shows its purchases as operating cash out until they are sold. Read backwards from this table, the chain is one sentence long. A company that owns aircraft bought for their engines, that rebuilds the modules of an engine no longer in production into exchanges for a fleet that did not retire on schedule, that sells those exchanges in part to vehicles it manages and part-owns, and that has begun turning run-out cores into generators for a power market short of turbines, depends on three quantities: how many CFM56 engines there are, how fast they leave, and what a new part from their only manufacturer costs. Each is contested, and the ten disagreements below are where the contest sits.
