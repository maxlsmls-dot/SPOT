<div class="part-divider"><div class="pn">The Spine</div><h1>The chain in one pass</h1><p>This is the whole chain, start to finish, in one reading: the engine, what it costs to keep flying, who pays, why the fleet that powers the world's older narrowbodies is still flying in 2026, how that fact became a shop-visit plateau and a parts shortage, who sells the substitutes for new parts, and how FTAI Aviation built a module business, a capital-management business and a power business on top of it. ### How to read the Spine

The Spine follows the flow of the industry, not the order of the Parts. It starts with a machine and ends with a company's balance sheet. It defines, in a short box at first use, only the terms a reader cannot do without; every other term is defined in the Part that owns it. It carries only the numbers another link depends on, and it presents each of them the way the research fixed them: with a source, a date, and, where two sources disagree, both figures and no average. Ten places in the chain carry a disagreement that bears directly on the economics; the Spine presents each where the chain reaches it and lists them at the end. Nothing here is ranked and nothing is recommended.

## S1. The machine: a turbofan, its modules, the life limits that force it open, and the margin that erodes

Everything in this primer is a consequence of four physical facts about one engine. The engine is built in separable sections. Inside those sections sit parts that are scrapped on a clock. The hot section loses temperature margin as it wears. And the engine is only as usable as its paperwork. This section states the four facts and the arithmetic that connects them.

<div class="defn"><b>Turbofan: fan, bypass and core</b><p>A turbofan is a jet engine with a large fan on the front. The fan pushes most of the air around the hot middle of the engine, not through it; the air that goes around is the bypass stream. Only a small share of the air goes through the middle, where fuel is burned and turbines are driven. The turbines spin the fan, and the fan does most of the pushing. The CFM56 is a high-bypass turbofan with two rotating assemblies, one inside the other, connected only by the air flowing between them. <span class="cite">[Part I §1]</span></p></div>

<div class="defn"><b>CFM56 (-7B and -5B), the 737NG and A320ceo it powers, and the V2500 (the A320ceo's alternative engine)</b><p>The CFM56 is a family of turbofans of which more than 35,000 have been delivered. Published counts of engines "in service" are about 23,000 (CFM website, undated), about 24,000 as of September 2025 (Aviation Business News) and more than 22,800 at end-2025 (Safran), and none states whether spare and stored engines are included. Two variants matter here. The -7B is the only engine ever fitted to the Boeing 737NG (Next Generation), the 737-600 to -900ER built from about 1997 to 2019. The -5B powers about six in ten Airbus A320ceo (current engine option) aircraft, built from about 1988 to 2020; the other four in ten carry the V2500, the alternative engine from the IAE consortium. The last -7B for a commercial 737NG was delivered by May 2019 and installed-engine production of the -5B ended in 2022. No new CFM56 is built for an airframe, so every CFM56 that flies tomorrow exists today. <span class="cite">[CFM website, undated; Aviation Business News, Sept 2025; Safran, 13 Feb 2026; GE Aerospace, 2019; ISTAT (the International Society of Transport Aircraft Trading), undated]</span></p></div>

<div class="defn"><b>Original equipment manufacturer (OEM), and CFM International</b><p>The original equipment manufacturer (OEM) is the company that holds the regulator's approval of the engine's design and built it. It is the sole source of new parts at its published catalogue price, it controls the manual that says how the engine may be repaired, and it runs its own repair shops and service contracts. For the CFM56 the OEM is CFM International, the joint venture of GE Aerospace and Safran Aircraft Engines, described everywhere as 50/50 and carried here as that widely published figure; GE builds the core and Safran the fan, booster, low-pressure turbine and gearbox. The same venture makes the LEAP (CFM's new-generation engine), which replaces the CFM56 on the A320neo and the 737 MAX (the re-engined 737). <span class="cite">[CFM website, undated; Part I §2; Part IV §25]</span></p></div>

The engine comes apart at a few large bolted joints, and the sections between the joints are the units in which this whole market trades.

<div class="defn"><b>Module, and the core as the hot module</b><p>A module is a section of the engine designed to be removed and replaced as a unit. It is bolted to its neighbours at flanges and carries its own part number, serial number and maintenance record, so it can move from one engine to another when its build standard, records and part numbers allow. The CFM56 has three gas-path major modules, the fan and booster, the core and the low-pressure turbine (LPT), plus an accessory gearbox outside the gas path. The core, also called the gas generator, is the hot, fast, expensive middle: the high-pressure compressor (HPC), the combustor and the high-pressure turbine (HPT). Sources count the modules as three, four, "four to five" or six, depending on the level counted and whether the gearbox is included; FTAI's own releases convert at three modules per engine ("450 modules (150 engines)", 26 Feb 2025). In FTAI's exchange business "core" has a second meaning, the unserviceable unit a customer hands back; the Spine says "returned core" for that sense. <span class="cite">[Aircraft Commerce Issue 58, 2008; FTAI release, 26 Feb 2025; Part I §3; reconciliation R-035]</span></p></div>

<div class="defn"><b>Life-limited part (LLP) and the engine flight cycle (EFC)</b><p>An engine flight cycle (EFC) is one take-off and landing; a mature short-haul narrowbody flies roughly 1,000 to 1,500 cycles a year (Aircraft Value News). A life-limited part (LLP) is a disk, spool or shaft whose failure the engine case cannot be designed to contain. It is certified for a fixed number of cycles and must be scrapped at that limit whatever its condition. On the -7B the limits are 30,000 cycles in the fan and booster, 20,000 in the compressor and high-pressure turbine, and 25,000 in the low-pressure turbine. The parts are the innermost parts of the engine, so reaching a limit forces the engine open. <span class="cite">[Aircraft Commerce Issue 34, 2004; AJW and StandardAero mini-packs; Aircraft Value News, undated]</span></p></div>

The life limits are the first of two clocks. The second is a thermometer.

<div class="defn"><b>Exhaust-gas temperature (EGT) margin</b><p>Every engine has a certified exhaust-gas temperature (EGT) red line it may not exceed. EGT margin is the gap between that limit and the temperature the engine actually reaches at full take-off thrust on a hot day. As blade tips and seals wear, the engine burns more fuel to make the same thrust and runs hotter, so the margin shrinks; it erodes fastest in the first 1,000 to 2,000 cycles after a shop visit. At zero margin the engine can no longer make its rated thrust within its limit and must come off the wing. Opening the core and restoring the high-pressure turbine's blades, vanes and seals recovers most of it. <span class="cite">[Aircraft Commerce Issue 27, 2003; Part I §5]</span></p></div>

<div class="defn"><b>Time on wing</b><p>Time on wing is the number of cycles or hours between an engine's installation and its removal. It is set by whichever of the two clocks runs out first: the life-limited part with the fewest cycles remaining, or the exhaust-gas-temperature margin, with a mandatory inspection as a rarer third. A mature CFM56 has a performance restoration every 10,000 to 15,000 cycles and a full overhaul with life-limited-part replacement at 20,000 to 25,000 cycles (Leeham, Mar 2024); at 1,000 to 1,500 cycles a year that is roughly seven to fifteen years between restorations. <span class="cite">[Leeham News, 8 Mar 2024; Aircraft Value News, undated; Part I §5–§6]</span></p></div>

<div class="worked"><div class="h">Worked example S.1 — The shorter clock governs</div>
<p>Inputs: a CFM56-7B whose high-pressure turbine disk has consumed 18,500 cycles against its 20,000-cycle limit (limit sourced from the records mini-packs; cycles consumed assumed). The aircraft flies 1,500 cycles a year (sourced as the top of Aircraft Value News's range). The engine's temperature margin would allow another 5,000 cycles (assumed).</p>
<div class="eqblock">cycles remaining on the disk = 20,000 − 18,500 = 1,500
time to the limit = 1,500 ÷ 1,500 a year = about one year
margin remaining = 5,000 cycles, worth nothing to this owner</div>
<p>So the disk sets the removal date at about a year, and the engine comes off with 5,000 cycles of unused margin. The same arithmetic runs the other way on a fresh disk and a worn hot section: the margin governs and the disk's remaining life is carried into the shop unused. Limits of the example: two inputs are assumed; the point is the rule, not the figures. <span class="cite">[Part I worked example 1.1]</span></p></div>

Three consequences follow, and the rest of the chain is built on them. First, an engine at a shop visit is really three or four assets with three or four different remaining lives, because CFM set the limits at 30,000, 20,000 and 25,000 cycles and the margin clock runs on its own schedule. A fan and a core from the same engine are never at the same point in their lives. Second, the quickest way to return an engine to service is often to replace the one module that has run out rather than to restore all of them, because the engine was designed to split at the module flanges. Third, the usable supply of engines and modules is the documented supply, not the physical one. A module with a gap in its records cannot be installed, so it does not count as supply however good its metal. The fleet behind all of this only shrinks: every engine retired, torn down or converted leaves the pool, and nothing enters it. <span class="cite">[Part I §3–§4, §9]</span>

## S2. What a shop visit is and costs, and why the parts bill is where the money sits

When either clock runs out, or something breaks, the engine comes off and goes to a shop. The bill it generates there has five lines, and the mix among them decides most of what follows.

<div class="defn"><b>Shop visit</b><p>A shop visit is any removal of the engine from the aircraft for work in an engine shop, from a visit to fix one finding to a complete rebuild. The shop disassembles the engine on stands, cleans and inspects every piece, replaces or repairs what is out of limits, rebuilds it and runs it in a test cell before release. "Teardown" is reserved in this primer for dismantling an engine to sell its parts, not for shop work. <span class="cite">[Part I §6]</span></p></div>

<div class="defn"><b>Maintenance, repair and overhaul (MRO), and the independent MRO</b><p>MRO is the industry's generic term for maintenance, repair and overhaul work and for the companies that do it. An independent MRO is an engine shop not owned by the engine's manufacturer: a stand-alone company such as StandardAero, an airline's maintenance arm that sells to others, or another engine maker's shop that is independent on this engine, such as MTU (a German engine maker) through MTU Maintenance. Every shop holds a regulatory repair-station approval and works to the manufacturer's shop manual. <span class="cite">[Part I §6; Part IV §25]</span></p></div>

<div class="defn"><b>Workscope, performance restoration and overhaul</b><p>The workscope is the specification of what a visit will do: which modules are opened, how deep, and which parts are replaced new, repaired, or swapped for used material. A performance restoration opens the core and restores the compressor, combustor and high-pressure turbine to recover temperature margin; the fan and low-pressure turbine may be inspected but are not fully disassembled. An overhaul disassembles every module to piece parts and replaces the life-limited parts that would fall due within the next run; "zero-time" is one source's term for an overhaul with a complete new set. Replacing life-limited parts is a separate decision that a life-limit-driven removal combines with the restoration. A hospital or quick-turn visit fixes one thing and returns the engine; a module exchange is one of the things it does. <span class="cite">[Part I §6; registry C-7]</span></p></div>

<div class="defn"><b>Turnaround time (TAT) and the induction slot</b><p>Turnaround time (TAT) is the calendar time from an engine's induction into a shop to its release. A full CFM56 overhaul took about 60 days before the pandemic and 90 to 120 days in 2025 (trade press); GE's shops report "sustaining about 90 days" (May 2026); a consultancy blog gives 75 to 90 days for a restoration and 120 to 150 for a heavy visit. The induction slot is the date a shop commits to start an engine, and when shops are full the wait for a slot is additional to the turnaround: two to three months and up to six (Bain, 2024). Every day away needs a spare engine or grounds an aircraft. <span class="cite">[Aviation Business News, 2025; GE Aerospace, 27 May 2026; Safe Fly Aviation, 2026 (consultancy blog); Bain, 2024; Part I §6; Part IV §27]</span></p></div>

<div class="defn"><b>The shop-visit cost build</b><p>The cost of a shop visit is the sum of labour (hours times the shop's rate), new OEM parts that are scrapped and replaced (blades, vanes, seals, bearings), repairs of parts that can be restored rather than replaced, life-limited-part replacement (new or used), and the test-cell run, plus freight, consumables and the shop's margin. Material is 60 to 70% of the total, labour 20 to 30% and repairs 10 to 20% (Air Cargo Week); a consultancy blog adds that life-limited parts are 40 to 60% of parts cost when replaced and high-pressure turbine blades and vanes 15 to 25%. <span class="cite">[Air Cargo Week, 2025/26; Safe Fly Aviation, 2026 (consultancy blog); Part I §7]</span></p></div>

<div class="defn"><b>Catalogue list price and escalation</b><p>The catalogue list price is the manufacturer's published price for every new spare part, repriced at least annually. A shop passes it through at list plus a handling margin, and used and alternative parts are priced as discounts to it, so they inherit its movements. Escalation is the manufacturer's annual increase. Trade press put new spare-parts prices up around 10% a year in 2022 and 2023, with a double-digit step on 1 November 2022 and a high-single-digit step in August 2023; Safran's chief executive described a rule of pricing "above inflation by 3 to 4 points"; and the published prices of a full -7B life-limited-part set compounded at about 7% a year from 2008 to 2025, a figure derived in the research. The two series cover different parts and years and are carried together. <span class="cite">[Aviation Week, late 2023; Part I worked example 1.3; Part IV §28; reconciliation R-018]</span></p></div>

Two things stand out in the exhibit, and the primer carries both without resolving them. Every cost figure comes from trade press or one consultancy blog; no manufacturer's or shop's price list was read. And the two consultancy sets disagree with each other on the same scope. <span class="cite">[reconciliation R-013]</span> A further check comes from the set prices. A restoration at $1.4M plus a full new set at $4M is $5.4M, above every quoted "with LLPs" range, so the quoted totals must assume partial replacement, used parts or older price levels. <span class="cite">[Part I §7.1]</span>

The life-limited-part line is where the arithmetic bites, because the parts are bought from one source at a price that only rises, and because an engine rarely reaches a shop with all of its parts at zero.

<div class="worked"><div class="h">Worked example S.2 — Stub life, and why the life-limited-part decision outweighs the whole restoration</div>
<p>Inputs: an engine arrives for a restoration at 12,000 cycles since new (assumed). Its core life-limited parts have a 20,000-cycle limit (sourced: mini-packs), so 8,000 cycles remain, and the owner plans a next run of 12,000 cycles (assumed, inside Leeham's 10,000–15,000 range). A full new set lists at $5.7M (sourced: MyAirTrade, June 2025, scope not visible) or about $4.0M (sourced: Aircraft Value News, Nov 2019); the two are never mixed in one line. The restoration itself costs $2.15M (midpoint of Safe Fly's $1.8–2.5M) or $1.4M (midpoint of its $1.2–1.6M).</p>
<div class="eqblock">keep the parts: the engine comes off again after 8,000 cycles, forced by the limit, with its restored performance two-thirds used
replace them new now: 8,000 of each part's 20,000 cycles (40%) are scrapped unused
cost per cycle, full life used:   5,700,000 ÷ 20,000 = $285   (at $4.0M: $200)
cost per cycle, scrapped at 12,000: 5,700,000 ÷ 12,000 = $475   (at $4.0M: $333)
restoration alone, per cycle of the next run: 2,150,000 ÷ 12,000 = $179   (at $1.4M: $117)</div>
<p>So the shorter remaining lives of the parts already inside the engine, the "stub life" (Aircraft Commerce), add a third to a half to the cost of every cycle if the parts are scrapped early, and that penalty is larger than the entire restoration workscope on either set price. The third option, which the whole used-parts market exists to supply, is to install used life-limited parts with 12,000 to 15,000 cycles remaining, matched to the planned run; dealers advertise such packages on cycles remaining, and their prices were not retrieved. Limits of the example: the cycles consumed and the planned run are assumed; the set price has two readings (R-016, R-017); the package price is unknown. <span class="cite">[Aircraft Commerce Issue 34, 2004; Part I worked examples 1.2 and 1.6]</span></p></div>

The parts bill is where the money sits for three reasons that the reader can now assemble. Material is 60 to 70% of a visit, and most material is priced off the manufacturer's catalogue, so the catalogue's annual escalation flows into every visit whoever performs it. Life-limited parts are 40 to 60% of parts cost when replaced, and no alternative maker produces one for the CFM56, so that line is a single-source purchase at the escalating list price. And the high-pressure turbine's blades and vanes, the next largest line at 15 to 25% of parts cost, sit where most of the engine's lost margin is recovered, which is why an approved alternative to that blade matters as much as it does (S8). <span class="cite">[Air Cargo Week, 2025/26; Safe Fly Aviation, 2026; Part I §7; Part VI §38]</span> The levers a shop other than the manufacturer has are therefore all on the material line: used parts instead of new, approved alternative parts instead of the manufacturer's, repair instead of replacement, and exchange of a module instead of overhaul of the engine. Sections S8 and S9 take those four levers in turn.

## S3. Where the money sits across the chain: a first margin map, bases stated, nothing ranked

Before the chain is followed further, it helps to see its links and the profit each one reports, so that the segment sections have a frame. This is a map, not an analysis.

<div class="defn"><b>Margin pool</b><p>The margin pool is this primer's term for how the profit earned in keeping an engine type flying is distributed across the links of the chain: the manufacturer's new parts and services, the shops' labour and test, the component repairers, the makers of approved alternative parts, the traders in used material and teardown, the lessors of engines and aircraft, and the servicers of third-party capital. No company reports a "margin pool"; the term names a way of reading the filings together. Its inputs are the slice-by-slice capture of a shop visit and each company's reported margin on its own basis. <span class="cite">[Part I §11; Part XI §86]</span></p></div>

<div class="defn"><b>FTAI's two reportable segments: Aerospace Products and Aviation Leasing</b><p>FTAI Aviation reports two segments. Aerospace Products "develops and manufactures, repairs/refurbishes, and sells aircraft engines and aftermarket components primarily for the CFM56-7B, CFM56-5B, and V2500" through its Module Factory (S9); its FY2025 segment revenue was $1,936.2M. Aviation Leasing "owns and manages aircraft and engines and leases and sells them, directly and through an equity-method investment"; it held 290 assets at 31 December 2025 (S10). A third line, FTAI Power, appears in the 2027 guidance; whether it is a reportable segment in the 2026 quarterly reports was not confirmed (S11). <span class="cite">[FTAI 10-K FY2025; FTAI release, 29 Jul 2026; Part VII §45; Part VIII §58]</span></p></div>

<div class="defn"><b>Adjusted EBITDA (consolidated and by segment) and guidance</b><p>Adjusted EBITDA is earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortisation (EBITDA), as FTAI adjusts them: items the company treats as non-recurring or non-cash are removed, and the company's pro-rata share of Adjusted EBITDA from its unconsolidated equity-method investments is added back. FTAI reports it consolidated ($597.3M in 2023, $862.1M in 2024, $1,190.9M in 2025) and by segment (Aerospace Products $160.0M, $380.6M, $671.3M; Aviation Leasing $500.1M and $608.9M for 2024 and 2025), and it sets its guidance in the segment measure. The segment figures do not sum to the consolidated one; the difference is Corporate and Other, about −$89.2M in 2025 by subtraction. The exact FY2025 definition and reconciliation were not retrieved. <span class="cite">[FTAI 10-K FY2025 segment table; Part X §80, §84]</span></p></div>

Three cautions travel with the table, and the reader who holds them can read every later figure correctly. The margins sit on five different bases. An EBITDA margin exceeds an EBIT margin by the depreciation share of revenue, which at FTAI was $225.8M on $2,507.4M in 2025, about nine points, so a lessor's EBITDA margin is high by construction and says little until depreciation and interest are deducted. <span class="cite">[FTAI 10-K FY2025; Part X §80; Part XI §86]</span> A share of cost is not a margin: the first row says where the bill goes, not what anyone earns on it. And a parts pass-through compresses a percentage: a shop that bills $2M of the manufacturer's parts at a small handling margin beside $1M of its own labour shows a thin percentage on a large revenue line, while a parts maker that sells its own product shows a wide one, and the two may earn similar dollars. FTAI's segment sells a refurbished module or engine it owns, so its margin is closer in construction to a parts business's than to a shop's, and the research warns against reading it beside StandardAero's as the same activity measured twice. <span class="cite">[Part XI §86.9]</span>

One structural fact about the manufacturer's capture belongs on this map. Safran describes about 85% of the CFM56's aftermarket revenue as spare parts and time-and-materials work, against about 40% for the LEAP, and about 15% of the CFM56 fleet is under long-term flight-hour agreements against about 50% of the LEAP fleet. <span class="cite">[Safran via Aviation Week, c. 2023/24; Aviation Week, undated; Part IV §28]</span> On this engine the manufacturer earns mainly by selling parts at list into visits it does not perform and does not control. That open structure is what leaves room for every substitute described in S8 and S9; on a contracted engine the manufacturer chooses the parts, and it has no reason to choose a competitor's.

## S4. Who owns engines and how they pay: leases, reserves, engine values, green time

Almost every dollar an engine earns passes through a contract, usually an operating lease, and almost every dollar its owner borrows is secured on that contract. The lease is where the cost of the life an engine consumes is turned into cash, and where an engine's value is written down part by part.

<div class="defn"><b>Operating lease</b><p>An operating lease is a contract under which the owner, the lessor, rents an aircraft or engine to an operator, the lessee, for a fixed term in exchange for periodic rent, while the lessor keeps title and keeps the risk that the asset is worth more or less than expected when it comes back. Nearly all of FTAI's leases are operating leases. At the end of the term the lessor extends, moves the asset to a new lessee, sells it, or parts it out. <span class="cite">[FTAI 10-K FY2025; Part II §12]</span></p></div>

<div class="defn"><b>Maintenance reserves</b><p>Maintenance reserves are payments the lessee makes to the lessor in addition to rent, set per flight hour or per cycle and collected per component: each engine's performance restoration, each engine's life-limited parts, the airframe's heavy checks, the landing gear. They pre-fund the eventual shop visit, so that the lessor holds cash for the life consumed if the lessee defaults or hands the asset back. The lessor reimburses a completed event up to the lesser of the balance held and the qualifying cost; an unclaimed balance stays with the lessor at lease end. Strong-credit lessees may instead pay end-of-lease compensation in one lump for the difference in condition between delivery and return. On a mid-life narrowbody the reserve stream can approach half of basic rent, and the lessor books reserves as revenue when it is no longer obliged to return them, which is FTAI's "maintenance revenue" line ($218.5M in 2025). <span class="cite">[AerCap 20-F FY2013; FTAI 10-K FY2025; Part II §13]</span></p></div>

<div class="defn"><b>Return conditions</b><p>Return conditions are the contractual minimum state of a leased engine at redelivery: minimum hours and cycles to the next restoration, minimum cycles remaining on each life-limited part, a borescope inspection and a test-cell run, and records in order. Together with the reserve or compensation regime they decide who pays for the maintenance life consumed during the lease. They may also exclude parts and repairs that are not the manufacturer's, which is where a lessor's view of alternative parts is written down; current practice on that clause for 2024 to 2026 was not found. <span class="cite">[Part II §13; Part VI §42]</span></p></div>

<div class="defn"><b>Lease rate factor (LRF)</b><p>The lease rate factor (LRF) is the monthly rent divided by the asset's current market value, as a percentage; 1.0% a month is a 12% gross annual yield before depreciation, interest and maintenance. No source the research reached states a factor for FTAI, for Willis Lease or for the CFM56 as a type. Every figure in the primer is derived and carries its basis: 0.70% a month (a consultancy green-time rent on an appraiser's half-life value, mixed bases), 0.87 to 1.1% (FTAI's own 2025 book, on cost and on net book value), about 1.0% (Willis Lease's on-lease book, derived from its filing), and 0.9% assumed in one fee example. <span class="cite">[Part II §12, §14; reconciliation R-030]</span></p></div>

<div class="defn"><b>Half-life value and maintenance-adjusted value</b><p>Half-life is an appraisal convention, not a physical midpoint: the value of an engine with every module halfway between restorations and every life-limited part at half its certified life, so that no adjustment has been made for actual maintenance status (ISTAT). Appraisers such as IBA publish values on that basis. Maintenance-adjusted value is the half-life value plus, for each item, the actual remaining fraction less one half, multiplied by the cost to restore that item, summed module by module. On this primer's inputs the swing between a fresh and a run-out -7B of the same age is about $2.8M on one set price and about $1.9M on the other. Because the modules carry clocks that deliberately do not align, the adjustment is naturally computed module by module. <span class="cite">[ISTAT Jetrader, 2010; IBA, Sept 2025; Part I §8]</span></p></div>

<div class="defn"><b>Green time</b><p>Green time is the remaining usable life on an engine or module before its next mandatory shop visit, set by whichever runs out first: the life-limited part with the fewest cycles remaining, the exhaust-gas-temperature margin, or a hard inspection. A green-time engine is one the owner does not intend to restore, flown or leased as-is to use up that life and then torn down; a green-time lease rents that life for months to a few years at a rent that is the price of the time consumed. The pool of green time is fed by retirements, so it shrinks exactly when a slow-retiring fleet most wants it. <span class="cite">[Part II §16; registry C-2]</span></p></div>

The floor under an engine's value is what it fetches taken apart, which S8 works. Between a fresh engine at its half-life value plus an adjustment and a run-out engine worth its parts lies the market in time: a part-worn engine leased for its remaining months, or torn down now. That choice, and the pool of engines it draws on, is where a slow-retiring fleet shows up first.

<div class="defn"><b>Special-purpose vehicle (SPV), asset-backed securitization (ABS) and warehouse facility</b><p>A special-purpose vehicle (SPV) is a bankruptcy-remote entity that exists only to own a defined pool of assets and the debt secured on them, so a lender looks only to those assets and their leases, not to the sponsor. An asset-backed securitization (ABS) is a sale of a portfolio of leased aircraft or engines to such a vehicle, which issues notes to investors in tranches paid in order of seniority; the sponsor usually keeps the equity and services the leases for a fee, and rating agencies size the senior notes by stressing rents, downtime, maintenance and residual values. A warehouse facility is a revolving bank loan to an SPV that funds assets one at a time against an advance rate until there are enough to securitize. FTAI's capital vehicles, under its Strategic Capital Initiative (SCI), are built from all three (S10); on 22 May 2026 the first of them priced FTAI MRE 2026-1, $612M of notes on 48 A320ceo and 737NG aircraft on lease to 23 airlines, with Series A expected to be rated Asf by Fitch and A(sf) by KBRA (a rating agency) and Series B BBB+sf by Fitch. <span class="cite">[FTAI release, 22 May 2026, via Finviz and AviTrader; Part II §17]</span></p></div>

The reason the rating agencies matter beyond the lessor's own income statement is now visible. An appraiser's note that engine values have levelled off, or that narrowbody rents fell 11 to 13%, feeds straight into the stress tests that decide how much senior debt a pool of leased engines can carry. <span class="cite">[Part II §17]</span>

## S5. Why 2026 looks the way it does: the fleet that only shrinks, retirements that did not come, new engines that did not relieve, airframers that did not deliver

Nothing in S1 to S4 changes between a quiet year and a tight one. What changes is the quantities: how many engines need a shop visit, how many are torn down, how many spares are needed and for how long. Between 2019 and 2026 four things moved those quantities in the same direction.

<div class="defn"><b>Installed base, retirement rate and the shop-visit forecast</b><p>The installed base is the population of engines fitted to aircraft in the operating fleet; published CFM56 counts of about 23,000 (CFM, undated), about 24,000 (Aviation Business News, Sept 2025) and more than 22,800 (Safran, end-2025) may include spare and stored engines, and a consultancy blog's about 14,200 "active" and more than 19,000 "flying" are labelled consultancy figures and never the fleet figure. The retirement rate is the share of a fleet withdrawn in a year; GE's planning assumption for CFM56 retirements in 2026 moved from 3–4% of the base, to 2–3%, to 1.5–2% (GE's chief financial officer via Aviation Week, 2026), GE reported about 1.5% retired in 2025 and expected about 2% in 2026 (via Leeham, 22 Jan 2026), and first-quarter 2026 retirements ran "below 1%" (GE investor-relations page). The shop-visit forecast counts expected inductions a year: 2,300 to 2,400 CFM56 visits a year in 2026 to 2028 with the apex around 2027 to 2028 and a gradual fade (GE via Aviation Week, 2026), against an earlier Safran framing of a peak of about 2,500 in 2025 to 2026 (c. 2023/24), from just above 2,000 in 2019. <span class="cite">[Part III §18, §19, §23; reconciliation R-001, R-004, R-006]</span></p></div>

Because no new -5B or -7B is built, the base falls by exactly the number of engines retired or converted each year. Every engine that does not retire keeps flying, keeps accumulating cycles, and reaches the visits of S2. Every engine that does not retire is also one that is not torn down, so its parts do not reach the used-material market. The same number sits on both sides of the aftermarket at once.

Why did so few engines retire? An aircraft is retired when its next heavy event, a shop visit or a life-limited-part replacement, costs more than it is worth kept, and when a replacement is available. Between 2023 and 2026 the second condition failed. An airline at the maintenance cliff with no new aircraft to fly pays for the shop visit anyway, because the alternative is to shrink; a lessor whose lessee cannot find a replacement extends the lease rather than take the aircraft back into a market with no buyer-operators. Cirium's count that only about a dozen mid-life 737NGs and fewer than 25 A320ceos were available to rent anywhere in the world is the measure of how closed the gate was. <span class="cite">[Cirium Ascend via Bloomberg; Part III §19]</span> Two things closed it.

CFM's LEAP, on the A320neo and sole on the 737 MAX, came off wing early at 2,000 to 6,000 cycles in hot and harsh environments; CFM certified a LEAP-1B durability kit with the Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) and the European Union Aviation Safety Agency (EASA) on 18 July 2026, with full production cutover expected in early 2027. And the FAA capped 737 production at 38 a month after the January 2024 door-plug event, raising it to 42 in October 2025 and completing the review to 47 in May 2026, against a 2018 peak of 52; Airbus delivered 793 aircraft in 2025 against an original target of 820 and now expects A320neo rate 75 only by the end of 2027. <span class="cite">[Cirium via The Flying Engineer, 2026; Leeham, 27 Jan 2026; CFM release, 18 Jul 2026; TravelPulse, Oct 2025; CAPA, May 2026; Argus Media; Air Data News; Part III §20–§21]</span></p></div>

The mechanism from each shock to the CFM56 fleet is indirect and specific. A grounded Pratt & Whitney-powered A320neo needs its flying replaced, and the practical replacement is an A320ceo on the same pilot rating; that keeps the -5B and the V2500 flying harder, not the -7B. An early LEAP removal generates a LEAP shop visit in the manufacturer's network, not a CFM56 visit, but an operator whose 737 MAX fleet is less available than planned keeps its 737NGs; that touches the -7B. A production cap of 38 a month against a 2018 rate of 52 holds back on the order of 170 737NG replacements a year at a one-for-one ceiling, or about 77 at Cirium's replacement ratio of 46.1%. <span class="cite">[Part III §20.3, worked example 3.4]</span> Each shock also carries its own reversal: the GTF groundings were already easing in January 2026, the LEAP-1B kit cuts over in early 2027, and the backlogs are owed rather than cancelled, so when the rates rise the deferred retirements arrive with them.

The lessors' response closed the loop. With 75 to 90% of scheduled expiries extended for four to six years, the maintenance events that would have fallen at a lease transition fell inside the extended term and were paid by the incumbent airline, and only 10 to 25 aircraft of every 100 came back to the used market as acquisition candidates or teardown feedstock. <span class="cite">[Cirium Ascend via Bloomberg; IBA, Nov 2023; Part III worked example 3.5]</span> The shape of the forecast that results is contested between the two CFM partners at two dates, a peak of about 2,500 in 2025 to 2026 against a plateau of 2,300 to 2,400 through 2028, and the long end of either curve depends on the retirement rate that moved three times in a year. <span class="cite">[reconciliation R-006, R-007]</span>

## S7. The shop-visit market and the OEM's pricing: demand plateau, capacity shortage, list-price escalation, the OEM's own offerings

The market that S1's engine creates has four kinds of shop and one price-setter. The shops are the manufacturer's own network, the large independents, the airlines' maintenance arms and, since 2022, FTAI's Module Factory. The price-setter is the manufacturer, which earns from nearly every visit without performing it.

The demand side is a plateau whose height and length are contested. Safran's earlier framing, from about 2023 or 2024, had CFM56 visits peaking at about 2,500 a year in 2025 and 2026 and then falling. GE's 2026 framing has about 2,300 for each of 2026 and 2027, widened to 2,300 to 2,400 and "probably closer to 2,400", held through 2028 with the apex around 2027 to 2028 and a gradual fade after. The later view is both lower at its top and later in reaching it. <span class="cite">[Safran and GE via Aviation Week; reconciliation R-006, R-007]</span> The CFM56 is roughly a quarter of all engine shop work by count: about 2,300 visits inside about 9,000 of all types in 2025. <span class="cite">[Aviation Week 2026 Fleet & MRO Forecast, via ePlaneAI]</span> What moved the shape between the two dates was not the fleet, which only shrinks, but the retirement assumption behind it, which is why a shop or a module factory adding capacity now is adding it into a plateau on the manufacturer's own forecast, and why the date the fade begins is the least certain number in the chain.

The capacity side has two readings and the primer carries both. Trade press and consultancies describe a system that is full: a CFM56 overhaul at 90 to 120 days against about 60 before the pandemic, waits for an induction slot of two to six months, engine-maintenance demand set to exceed capacity by more than 17% by the end of the decade if capacity grows on its current path (Bain, 2024), two-thirds of maintenance operators unable to find enough technicians and labour-rate inflation of 5.5 to 6.0% (Oliver Wyman, April 2026), and used-material supply that "remains thin". GE's own filing and conference material read the other way: turnaround for the LEAP, CFM56 and GE90 (a widebody engine) improved by more than 10% year on year in the fourth quarter of 2025, material input from priority suppliers rose more than 40%, and CFM56 turnaround was "sustaining about 90 days" in May 2026. <span class="cite">[Aviation Business News, 2025; Bain, 2024; Oliver Wyman, Apr 2026; GE 10-K FY2025; GE Aerospace, 27 May 2026; reconciliation R-019, R-020]</span> The consequence for the chain does not depend on which reading is right. A removal that returns in five to ten months ties up a spare for that long, so the shortage of slots is itself demand for spare engines and spare-engine leases, and for anything that returns the aircraft to service in weeks rather than months, which is the exchange of S9.

The manufacturer's model is the frame everything else sits in.

Put the first three rows together and the manufacturer's capture on this engine is plain. Most visits are chosen and paid for visit by visit; within each, 60 to 70% of the bill is material; most material is bought new at list. So the manufacturer receives the largest slice of nearly every CFM56 visit without performing it and without a contract that obliges the operator to come back, and its annual escalation lands on that slice whoever runs the shop. Under a time-and-materials visit the operator or its shop decides whether a part is new, used, repaired or an approved alternative; under a long-term agreement the manufacturer decides. The substitutes of S8 and the exchanges of S9 therefore address the 85% of the CFM56 aftermarket that is transactional, and would address far less of a fleet contracted the way the LEAP is being contracted. <span class="cite">[Part IV §28–§29]</span> The manufacturer is also present on the other side of the substitutes' line: it sells used material and exchanges through TrueChoice, and it supplies FTAI's own shops with parts and repairs under the January 2026 agreement whose terms were not disclosed.

An operator whose engine is at its life limit therefore has four options, and the whole market is visible in them: a full shop visit with new life-limited parts, in which case the price is the manufacturer's price and the wait is the slot plus the turnaround; a module exchange, which S9 sells; a green-time engine leased to fly out the remaining months; or a sale of the engine for its parts and the purchase of another. The second, third and fourth exist only because the engine is modular and because a secondary market in modules, time and used material exists, and low retirements shrink the supply behind all three at exactly the moment the slot shortage makes the first slow and escalation makes it expensive. <span class="cite">[Part IV §30]</span>

## S8. The substitutes for new parts: used material from teardowns, and PMA and DER

Three things can replace a new part from the manufacturer in a shop visit: a used part from a dismantled engine, a new part made by someone else under a regulator's approval, and a repair that keeps the old part in service. All three are priced against the manufacturer's list price, and all three depend on a supply or an approval the manufacturer does not control.

<div class="defn"><b>Used serviceable material (USM)</b><p>Used serviceable material (USM) is any part that has been in service, has been removed, has been inspected and if necessary repaired to the manufacturer's manual, and has been released on an airworthiness tag with its full history, so that it is legally fit to be installed again. It is made by the manufacturer, so it sits outside the debate over alternative parts, but it competes for the same line in the parts bill. A part with a gap in its paperwork is scrap whatever its condition. Used material trades at 60 to 80% of a new part's price on average, up to 85% early in a platform's life, and above list when the manufacturer cannot deliver (Aviation Week); a specialist trader sells repaired engine material to an airline at 70 to 75% of list (Aircraft Commerce). Because the base is the catalogue, the used price rises with every catalogue revision even if the discount does not move. <span class="cite">[Aviation Week, undated; Aircraft Commerce Issue 120; Part V §31, §34; reconciliation R-022]</span></p></div>

<div class="defn"><b>Teardown, part-out and feedstock</b><p>Part-out is the owner's decision to dismantle a retired aircraft or engine and sell its components individually; teardown is the physical disassembly, about three months for an airframe. Feedstock is the trade's word for the aircraft and engines bought, or taken on consignment, to be torn down; FTAI's filings extend it to the unserviceable module a customer returns in an exchange and to engine inventory held for module production. A serviceable module from a teardown is valued like an engine in miniature: its parts at used-material discounts to list, plus the remaining life of the life-limited parts inside it (life fraction times list price, discounted), less the repair needed to make it serviceable. Its supply is a lagged function of retirements. <span class="cite">[Part V §32–§33, §36; registry C-14]</span></p></div>

<div class="worked"><div class="h">Worked example S.8 — Why only run-out engines become feedstock, and how many there are</div>

<p>No owner tears down a $6M engine for $2M of parts, so engines with life are sold or leased whole and only engines at or near run-out reach the teardown market. The 2023 to 2026 shortage follows in six steps that the sources describe. Airlines kept CFM56-powered aircraft flying because new deliveries slipped and new engines spent time off wing. The expected post-2022 retirement wave did not arrive. The engines that would have been feedstock were leased as green-time engines or overhauled instead. Teardown companies competed for a smaller pool. And the price of everything in the pool rose: CFM56 values up "as much as 50%" in two years on one trade-press account, -7B rents about a third above 2019 on both the appraiser's and the consultancy's figures, "a lessors' market" (IBA, April 2024) and "a seller's market for used parts" (Aviation Week), with AerSale's whole-asset sales falling from 20 engines and one aircraft in 2024 to 13 engines in 2025 in what its management called "a hypercompetitive feedstock environment". <span class="cite">[ePlaneAI; IBA via AviTrader, 25 Apr 2024; Aviation Week; AerSale, 5 Mar 2026; Part V §33]</span> The manufacturer is also the largest used-material trader by its own statement, buying engines back under TrueChoice Transitions and selling their material through TrueChoice Material, so an independent teardown company competes with the OEM for feedstock and for customers at once. <span class="cite">[GE Aerospace, 2026; Part V §33.6]</span> FTAI sits on both sides of this trade: it buys engines and whole aircraft for their material, and it sells the used modules and parts it does not build into modules through an exclusive partnership. The FY2025 annual report does not name the partner; AAR (a parts distributor and maintenance company) announced on 27 March 2025 that the two had extended their exclusive Serviceable Engine Products agreement for CFM56 material through 2030, covering a pool of more than 450 engines as trade coverage of the release carried it, and the agreement's terms were not read. <span class="cite">[FTAI 10-K FY2025; AAR / PR Newswire, 27 Mar 2025, body as carried by ePlaneAI; Part V §35]</span>

<div class="defn"><b>Parts Manufacturer Approval (PMA)</b><p>A Parts Manufacturer Approval (PMA) is the FAA's combined design-and-production approval that lets a company other than the type-certificate holder legally make and sell a replacement part for a certificated engine. The applicant must show by its own tests and computations that the part meets the airworthiness standards, or that it is identical to the original, with no access to the manufacturer's data. Approvals have historically concentrated in consumables and simple parts; HEICO's Flight Support Group adds "approximately 300 to 500" or "approximately 400 to 550" a year, two readings of one filing, and earned $750.4M of operating income on $3,117.3M of sales in its year to 31 October 2025. A cooled, coated high-pressure turbine blade is the hard case, because its life depends on an alloy, a crystal structure and a coating the applicant must reproduce and prove in an engine-level test programme it funds itself. Chromalloy's FAA approval of a CFM56-5B/7B high-pressure turbine blade on 30 October 2025, "the only aftermarket alternative to the OEM manufactured new part", is treated in the trade as that milestone. No PMA exists for any CFM56 life-limited part. <span class="cite">[eCFR, 14 CFR (Code of Federal Regulations) Part 21 Subpart K; HEICO 10-K FY2025; Chromalloy release, 30 Oct 2025; Part VI §37–§38, §41; reconciliation R-024]</span></p></div>

<div class="defn"><b>Designated Engineering Representative (DER) repair</b><p>A Designated Engineering Representative (DER) is an engineer appointed by the FAA to approve engineering data on its behalf. A DER repair is a major repair whose data a DER approved rather than being taken from the manufacturer's shop manual; the part stays the manufacturer's part, and the repair saves money by restoring what the manual would scrap, so that no new part of any origin is bought, or by restoring it more cheaply than the manual's repair. The cadence decides which lever applies: at a first shop visit, around 20,000 cycles, airlines typically repair the high-pressure turbine blades; at a second, around 30,000, the hot-section parts typically cannot be repaired again and a new part is needed, which is where an approved alternative blade competes with the manufacturer's. <span class="cite">[FAA Order 8110.37F; In Practise, undated; Part VI §40]</span></p></div>

## S9. FTAI's answer: the Module Factory and the Aerospace Products segment

FTAI's module business is the fourth lever of S2 organised as a production line. It does the disassembly, inspection, repair and rebuilding before any customer engine arrives, holds the finished modules in inventory, and sells them into engines whose one expired module would otherwise send the whole engine through a 90-to-120-day visit after a two-to-six-month wait.

<div class="defn"><b>Module Factory</b><p>The Module Factory is FTAI's name for its CFM56-7B and -5B modular repair and refurbishment programme, in the FY2025 annual report's words "a dedicated commercial maintenance program designed to focus on modular and parts repair and refurbishment"; the language first used in 2022 and still in the filing places it at a 500,000-square-foot Montréal facility "with a capacity for up to 300 shop visits per year". It is a place (Montréal, bought outright for $170.0M on 9 September 2024), a programme, and a process: a production line that builds and holds an inventory of serviceable modules for exchange. Material enters it from customer cores returned in exchanges, from engines retired from FTAI's own leasing fleet, from the capital vehicles' aircraft, and from engines and aircraft bought for the purpose, such as the ten off-lease 737-700s acquired from WestJet in September 2026. <span class="cite">[FTAI 10-K FY2025; FTAI releases, 9 Sep 2024 and 28 Sep 2026; Part VII §46]</span></p></div>

<div class="defn"><b>Module exchange</b><p>A module exchange separates a customer's engine at the major-module flanges, removes the module that triggered the removal, installs a serviceable module from inventory of compatible build standard and documented life-limited-part status, tests the engine and returns it. The customer pays an exchange fee and gives up the unserviceable module, the returned core, which becomes FTAI's feedstock for a later serviceable module. The industry prices an exchange as the fee plus the difference in life-limited-part life between the module handed over and the module received, adjusted for the condition of the other hardware. Under US revenue rules the returned core is noncash consideration measured at fair value, so FTAI books revenue equal to the fee plus the core's fair value, receives the fee in cash and a module in kind, and carries the module in inventory until it is sold again. No provider's exchange fee, FTAI's included, was found in any source. StandardAero lists module changes among its quick-turn services and GE sells exchanges under TrueChoice Transitions. <span class="cite">[Part VII §46–§47; reconciliation cluster K-01]</span></p></div>

<div class="defn"><b>Maintenance, Repair and Exchange (MRE), and MRE Contract revenue</b><p>MRE is FTAI's name for its offer, chosen as a contrast with the industry's MRO: instead of restoring a customer's engine while the customer waits, FTAI swaps in a serviceable module or engine and takes the unserviceable unit as feedstock. It is sold as engine, module and parts sales to airlines, lessors and other shops on the "Aerospace products revenue" line ($455.0M in 2023, $1,079.8M in 2024, $1,600.5M in 2025), which no retrieved filing splits into engines, modules, parts and repairs. MRE Contract revenue is a second line, new in 2025, for sales to the special-purpose vehicles of the 2025 Partnership that FTAI manages and part-owns: $335.8M for 2025, $221.2M in the first quarter of 2026, $182.8M in the second and $404.0M for the half, against $170.2M a year earlier. Two filings define it differently: the Q3 2025 10-Q describes "stand-ready obligations to provide replacement CFM56-5B and CFM56-7B engines to customers as they become unserviceable", and the Q2 2026 10-Q describes "the transaction price for sales of CFM56-5B, CFM56-7B and V2500 engines and related modules to the SPVs of the first SCI partnership"; one is an obligation and the other a sale, and both are carried. <span class="cite">[FTAI 10-K FY2025; FTAI 10-Qs Q3 2025 and Q2 2026; Part VII §45, §48; reconciliation R-044]</span></p></div>

## S10. FTAI's capital: Aviation Leasing, the Strategic Capital vehicles, the money that flows between them, and the 2026 securitization

The other half of FTAI owns things. Aviation Leasing held 290 assets at 31 December 2025, 47 aircraft and 243 engines, including 8 aircraft and 17 engines in Russia; 37 aircraft and 143 engines were on lease, and utilization was about 77% on the company's definition, which weights each asset by its equity value and excludes airframes. Its 2025 lines were lease income of $235.2M, maintenance revenue of $218.5M and asset sales revenue of $106.9M, and its segment Adjusted EBITDA was $608.9M, so rent cannot account for the earnings figure on its own; maintenance revenue, asset-sale margins, a $46.4M gain on sales to the first capital vehicle and the equity-method share must together account for most of it, and the segment note that would show the composition was not retrieved. <span class="cite">[FTAI 10-K FY2025; Part VIII §58]</span> The aircraft is bought for the two engines under its wings (S4), so the leasing book is both a source of rent and a feedstock pipeline for S9.

In 2025 the company began selling that book into vehicles funded by other people's money while keeping three roles in them.

<div class="defn"><b>Strategic Capital Initiative (SCI): the 2025 Partnership, the 2026 SPV and the Seed Assets</b><p>The Strategic Capital Initiative (SCI), launched 30 December 2024, is FTAI's asset-management arm. It raises third-party capital into closed-end vehicles that buy on-lease mid-life 737NG and A320ceo aircraft; FTAI manages each vehicle as Servicer, co-invests as a limited partner, and supplies every engine shop visit for the fleet through module and engine exchanges with its MRE business. The 2025 Partnership, the first vehicle, closed on 27 October 2025 at a $2.0B equity hard cap against a $1.5B target, carried a $2.5B asset-level debt commitment arranged in February 2025, and by 17 August 2026 had "approximately $6.0 billion of total capital across over 300 aircraft", was fully committed, had paid a first distribution on 30 June 2026 and was in "harvest phase"; a release of 22 May 2026 said it then owned 292 aircraft. The Seed Assets are the 46 on-lease aircraft FTAI sold from its own book to start it, "at an estimated net purchase price of $549 million"; the sales cut leasing equipment at cost from $2,963.5M to $2,057.6M during 2025. The 2026 SPV, the second vehicle, closed a $2.0B warehouse facility from 13 lenders with an accordion to $3.0B on 14 August 2026; its equity size and FTAI's stake were undisclosed. The release's "$5.5 billion of warehouse financing" matches the disclosed $2.5B plus $2.0B only if the accordion is counted. <span class="cite">[FTAI releases, 30 Dec 2024, 26 Feb 2025, 27 Oct 2025, 22 May 2026, 17 Aug 2026; FTAI 10-K FY2025; Part VIII §60–§62; reconciliation R-055]</span></p></div>

<div class="defn"><b>Servicer and servicing fee</b><p>The Servicer is the party contracted to manage a vehicle's assets it does not wholly own: leasing and remarketing the aircraft, collecting rent and reserves, overseeing their technical condition and selling them, for a fee. FTAI "as the Servicer, provides aircraft management services to the 2025 Partnership, and receives customary, market-based compensation": $10.2M for 2025, $6.99M for the second quarter of 2026 and $12.85M for the first half. The fee's rate, its base (committed equity, invested cost or rents) and the income-statement line it sits on are not disclosed. <span class="cite">[FTAI 10-K FY2025; FTAI 10-Q Q2 2026; Part VIII §61, §63]</span></p></div>

<div class="defn"><b>Equity method and profit elimination</b><p>Under the equity method an investor with significant influence but not control carries its stake at cost plus its share of the investee's earnings, less distributions and eliminated profit, on one balance-sheet line and one income line, with none of the investee's revenue, assets or debt consolidated. FTAI's limited-partner interest in the 2025 Partnership is described as 19% in the FY2025 10-K and the 2026 10-Qs and as 20% in the Q3 2025 10-Q; it is accounted for this way and carried at $281.7M (31 Dec 2025), $279.4M (31 Mar 2026) and $365.5M (30 Jun 2026). Profit elimination removes from current earnings the profit on sales to such an investee and recognises it later: "the profit from the MRE Contract revenue is eliminated through equity method earnings and will be recognized over time as the 2025 Partnership generates income". Eliminations were $10.0M in the first quarter of 2026 and $6.6M in the second, $16.6M for the half. Whether the eliminated amount is 19% of the profit or all of it is not stated. <span class="cite">[FTAI 10-Qs Q3 2025, Q1 and Q2 2026; FTAI 10-K FY2025; Part VIII §63–§64; reconciliation R-047, R-048]</span></p></div>

<div class="defn"><b>Asset-light model and harvest phase</b><p>"Asset-light" is management's term for owning fewer leased assets directly and earning instead servicing fees, maintenance work and equity income from vehicles that own them; the July 2026 cut to Aviation Leasing guidance was explained as "reflecting a continued shift to an asset-light business model". ## S11. FTAI Power: a second life for cores, the J&F order, the market it sells into

The newest business removes CFM56 engines from aviation for good. It sits on the same side of the ledger as retirement and teardown, and it competes with both for the same run-out cores.

<div class="defn"><b>FTAI Power: Mod-1, heat rate, behind-the-meter bridging power, and J&F Power Systems</b><p>FTAI Power, launched 30 December 2025, converts CFM56 engines into aeroderivative generator sets: the fan is removed and replaced by a small compressor, a power turbine and generator are fitted, and the fuel system is changed for gas. Mod-1 is its 25 megawatt (MW) trailer-mounted unit, described in call coverage as 35 to 40% efficient at a heat rate of about 9,000, deployable in about two weeks, and giving the engine a 10-to-20-year second life on "near-fully depreciated" input engines; the release-level statements are capacity for "over 100 units annually", first delivery by the fourth quarter of 2026 and 100 units in 2027. Heat rate is the fuel energy needed to make one kilowatt-hour, so 9,000 is 37.9% efficient and a 50%-efficient piston engine is about 6,824. Behind-the-meter (BTM) generation is power made on the customer's side of the utility meter, and bridging power is temporary on-site generation used until a grid connection arrives, "five to seven years" on one supplier's description of its customers. J&F Power Systems is the venture with Jereh Group, a Chinese maker of trailer-mounted oilfield gas-turbine packages, through which Mod-1 is packaged and sold: FTAI calls it "FTAI's joint venture"; Jereh's exchange disclosure, as reported, calls it "its subsidiary"; the ownership split and the consolidating party are undisclosed. On 22 July 2026 J&F received a five-year master supply agreement and an initial $1.465 billion purchase order from "a leading international cloud service provider" (FTAI's release) or "a U.S. hyperscaler" (call summaries), for delivery in batches through November 2027 on milestone payments with a performance adjustment capped at 10%; the unit count was not disclosed. <span class="cite">[FTAI releases, 30 Dec 2025 and 22 Jul 2026; FTAI 10-Q Q2 2026; TheValueist and Aviation Week, call coverage, 26 Feb 2026; FilingReader, 22 Jul 2026; Part IX §68–§71; reconciliation R-061]</span></p></div>

The market it sells into is defined by a shortage the sources date precisely. The International Energy Agency expects global data-center electricity use to more than double to about 945 TWh by 2030. GE Vernova's gas-turbine backlog plus slot reservations rose from 50 GW in 2025 to 116 GW at the second quarter of 2026, with slots being sold for 2031 delivery; Siemens Energy quotes lead times of three years and more; Wood Mackenzie counted 110 GW of orders at end-2025 against 60 to 70 GW a year of manufacturing capacity. <span class="cite">[IEA, Apr 2025; Power Engineering, Jul 2026; Oilprice, 2026; Part IX §71]</span> A buyer that needs power in 2026 or 2027 cannot get a new frame turbine, and that is the opening for a 25 MW unit built from a used airline engine, for piston plants and for fuel cells. The 2027 guidance puts FTAI Power's Adjusted EBITDA at $450M, confirmed in the release text; one call summary gives "$450 million to $750 million", and both are carried. <span class="cite">[FTAI release, 29 Jul 2026; GuruFocus, 30 Jul 2026; reconciliation R-062]</span>

## S12. The company, its numbers and what remains unknown

<div class="defn"><b>Internalization, and the company in one paragraph</b><p>FTAI Aviation Ltd. is a Cayman Islands company listed on Nasdaq. The chain, read backwards from this table, is one sentence long. A company that owns engines and aircraft bought for their engines, that rebuilds the modules of an engine no longer in production into exchanges for a fleet that did not retire on schedule, that sells those exchanges in part to vehicles it manages and part-owns, and that has begun turning the run-out cores into generators for a power market short of turbines, is a company whose every line depends on the same three quantities: how many CFM56 engines there are, how fast they leave, and what a new part from their only manufacturer costs.
