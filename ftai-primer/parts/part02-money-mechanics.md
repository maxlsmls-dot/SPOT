<div class="part-divider"><div class="pn">Part II</div><h1>First principles: how money moves around an engine</h1><p>This Part explains how an aircraft or an engine turns into cash flows: the lease that rents it out, the reserves that pay for the life a lessee uses up, the settlement at the end of the lease, the rent an engine earns on its own, the trade in engines with a little life left, and the debt that funds the owner. ### Where this Part sits in the build

Part I described the CFM56 (the engine family Part I defined) as a machine: how it comes apart, which parts are scrapped on a clock, what a shop visit costs, and what the engine is worth at any point in its life. This Part takes that object and follows the money around it. Almost every dollar an engine earns for its owner passes through a contract, usually an operating lease, and almost every dollar its owner borrows is secured on that contract. So the lease is the unit of analysis here, the way the module was the unit in Part I. Part III, which follows, supplies the context: the fleet, the retirements that did not happen, the new engines that arrived late, and a translation table that turns each fact into a requirement on the aftermarket. Nothing in this Part depends on anything written after it. Parts VIII and XI, which cover FTAI's leasing segment, its third-party capital vehicles and its peers, depend on every definition here.

## 12. The operating lease, term by term

An aircraft or engine earns money for its owner in three ways. It earns rent while it is leased. It earns payments for the maintenance life a lessee consumes while flying it. And it earns a price when it is sold, either whole or in parts. The first two flows are written into a lease, and the third is shaped by the condition the lease requires the asset to come back in. This section takes the lease apart term by term. Section 13 works the maintenance terms through a full example, section 14 applies the whole to an engine on its own, and sections 15 to 17 follow the asset to the end of its life and to the lenders who fund it.

The two parties have plain names. The lessor is the owner who rents the asset out. The lessee is the operator, almost always an airline, who rents it. The research found no reason to make either word more technical than that. <span class="cite">[definition assembled in the research, D6 §2.1]</span>

<div class="defn"><b>Operating lease</b><p>An operating lease is a contract under which the owner (the lessor) rents an aircraft or engine to an operator (the lessee) for a fixed term in exchange for periodic rent, while the lessor keeps title to the asset and keeps the risk that it is worth more or less than expected when it comes back. Nearly all of FTAI's leases, and nearly all of Willis Lease Finance's, are operating leases. The alternative is the finance lease, defined next. <span class="cite">[FTAI 10-K FY2025; Willis Lease Finance Q3 2025 release; definition assembled in the research, D6 §2.1]</span></p></div>

<div class="defn"><b>Finance lease (sales-type lease)</b><p>A finance lease transfers substantially all the economic risks and rewards of owning the asset to the lessee, so that in substance it is a loan secured on the asset rather than a rental. Willis Lease Finance Corporation (WLFC) reports a small book of "investments in sales-type leases", $16.3M of its $2,888.5M lease portfolio at 30 September 2025, which are finance leases by another name; FTAI reports none. <span class="cite">[WLFC Q3 2025 release; definition assembled in the research, D6 §2.1]</span></p></div>

The distinction matters for everything that follows. Under an operating lease the lessor still owns the engine when it comes back, so the lessor cares what condition it comes back in, which is why the maintenance terms in §13 exist. Under a finance lease the lessee effectively owns it and the lessor is a lender. This Part is about operating leases unless it says otherwise.

### 12.1 Term, rent and the lease rate factor

The term is the fixed period of the lease. For a mid-life narrowbody it typically runs several years; FTAI reported a weighted-average remaining term of 44 months on its aircraft on lease and 38 months on its engines on lease at 31 December 2025. <span class="cite">[FTAI 10-K FY2025]</span> Those are remaining terms, not the terms the leases were signed at; the research did not retrieve new-lease terms at signing or the mix of short against long engine leases in FTAI's book (D6 U15). Engine leases divide into short-term and long-term, which Part I §6.4 defined; §14 carries them.

<div class="defn"><b>Lease rate (rent)</b><p>The lease rate is the periodic rent, quoted per month. The sourced points in this primer are about $228,000 a month for a 12-year-old Boeing 737-800 and about $220,000 for a comparable Airbus A320-200 as of July 2025, and about $100,000 a month for a CFM56-7B spare engine in 2024, all from IBA (an appraiser), against $42,000 to $48,000 a month for a CFM56-7B in 2026 from a consultancy blog that may be describing a green-time engine. The two engine figures are carried as a pair throughout this primer and never averaged. <span class="cite">[IBA, Jul 2025, via India Seatrade News, 6 Sep 2026; IBA, Apr 2024, via AviTrader; Safe Fly Aviation, 2026 (consultancy blog); reconciliation R-028]</span></p></div>

Rent on its own says little about whether a lease is a good one for the owner, because a $228,000 rent on a $15M aircraft is a very different thing from the same rent on a $30M aircraft. The industry therefore quotes rent as a percentage of what the asset is worth.

<div class="defn"><b>Lease rate factor (LRF)</b><p>The lease rate factor is the monthly rent divided by the asset's current market value, expressed as a percentage. An LRF of 1.0% means annual rent is 12% of value. It is the leasing industry's shorthand for gross yield, before depreciation, interest and maintenance. It rises with asset age, because older assets carry more residual and maintenance risk and lose value faster in percentage terms, and it rises with interest rates. No source the research reached states a lease rate factor for FTAI, for Willis Lease or for the CFM56 as a type; every figure in this primer is derived, and §14 shows each with its basis. <span class="cite">[definition assembled in the research, D6 §2.1, D11 §2.2; reconciliation R-030]</span></p></div>

<div class="worked"><div class="h">Worked example 2.1 — Reading a lease rate factor</div>
<p>Inputs: rent of $228,000 a month for a 12-year-old 737-800 (sourced: IBA, July 2025). The aircraft's half-life market value was not retrieved by the research (D6 U9), so the value is the unknown in this example and the lease rate factor is the variable.</p>
<div class="eqblock">LRF = rent ÷ value
at LRF 0.8% a month: value = 228,000 ÷ 0.008 = $28.5M
at LRF 1.0% a month: value = 228,000 ÷ 0.010 = $22.8M

<p>So the same rent is a 9.6% gross yield on a $28.5M aircraft and a 14.4% gross yield on a $19.0M one. The identity cuts both ways: a lessor who knows the value quotes the lease rate factor, and a buyer who knows the rent and the factor other lessors accept can back out what the aircraft is worth to them. Limits of the example: the three factors are chosen to show the arithmetic and are not market data; the aircraft value was not sourced; and the factor for a specific lease depends on the lessee's credit, the term and the return conditions below. <span class="cite">[IBA, Jul 2025; D6 U9]</span></p></div>

### 12.2 The security deposit

<div class="defn"><b>Security deposit</b><p>A security deposit is cash, or a bank letter of credit, that the lessee posts at signing. The lessor holds it against default and returns it at redelivery if the lessee has performed. It is a credit instrument, not a maintenance instrument: it protects the lessor against unpaid rent, not against life consumed from the engines. FTAI's balance sheet carries "security deposits" as a liability; the amounts by year were not returned by the research (D6 U7). <span class="cite">[FTAI 10-K FY2025; definition assembled in the research, D6 §2.1]</span></p></div>

The deposit is the first of three pots of the lessee's money that a lessor may hold. The other two, maintenance reserves and end-of-lease compensation, are maintenance instruments and are the subject of §13. They are defined here because the lease contains them.

### 12.3 Maintenance reserves and end-of-lease compensation

Every hour an engine flies uses up some of the life that was in it when the lease began: the exhaust-gas temperature margin that a performance restoration will have to recover, and the cycles on each life-limited part (Part I §4 and §5). A lease must say who pays for that. There are two regimes.

<div class="defn"><b>Maintenance reserves (supplemental rent; maintenance rents)</b><p>Maintenance reserves are periodic payments the lessee makes to the lessor in addition to basic rent, "calculated with reference to the utilization of airframes, engines and other major life-limited components during the lease". They are set per flight hour or per flight cycle and collected per component: the airframe's heavy checks, the landing gear, the auxiliary power unit, each engine's performance restoration and each engine's life-limited parts. They pre-fund the eventual shop visit so that if the lessee defaults or hands the asset back, the lessor already holds cash for the life consumed. The lessor reimburses the lessee for a completed maintenance event up to the lesser of the reserve balance held for that event and the qualifying cost; any balance not claimed against a qualifying event stays with the lessor at lease end. AerCap calls them maintenance rents; Willis Lease books them as maintenance reserve revenue when they are no longer refundable. <span class="cite">[AerCap 20-F FY2013; Spirit Airlines 10-K FY2013; WLFC Q3 2025 release]</span></p></div>

<div class="defn"><b>End-of-lease (EOL) compensation</b><p>End-of-lease compensation is the alternative regime, used for lessees whose credit is strong enough that the lessor does not demand monthly reserves. The lessee must "re-deliver the aircraft in a similar maintenance condition (normal wear and tear excepted) as when accepted under the lease, with reference to major life-limited components", and "to the extent that such components are redelivered in a different condition than at acceptance, there is an end-of-lease compensation adjustment for the difference at redelivery". The money can flow either way: the lessee pays if the asset comes back with less life than it had at delivery, and the lessor pays or credits if it comes back with more. Many leases combine the two regimes, reserves for some components and an end-of-lease adjustment for others. <span class="cite">[AerCap 20-F FY2013]</span></p></div>

The difference between the regimes is timing and credit, not amount. Under reserves the lessor collects monthly and holds the cash. Under end-of-lease compensation the lessor collects one lump at the end and bears the lessee's credit in the meantime. Either way the lessee pays for the life it consumes, measured against the condition at delivery, and §13 works both through the same lease.

### 12.4 Return conditions and redelivery

Part I §9 defined return conditions, also called redelivery conditions: the contractual minimum state of the asset at redelivery. For an engine that is typically a minimum number of flight hours and cycles remaining to the next performance restoration, a minimum number of cycles remaining on the life-limited parts, a borescope inspection and a test-cell run. For an airframe it is time remaining to the next heavy check, fresh paint or a paint credit, and records in order. <span class="cite">[definition assembled in the research, D6 §2.1]</span> Redelivery is the act of handing the asset back at the end of the term, and the return conditions are what the lessor checks at that moment.

Return conditions do a specific job in the money flow. Together with the reserve or end-of-lease regime they decide who bears the cost of the maintenance life consumed during the lease. If the engine comes back below the required condition, the lessee must either restore it before redelivery or pay. Return conditions may also exclude parts and repairs that are not the manufacturer's; Part VI §42 carries that clause, and the research recorded that current lessor practice on it, for 2024 to 2026, was not found (D4 U1). <span class="cite">[D4 §2.9; reconciliation C.4 item 2]</span>

### 12.5 Extensions

A lease does not have to end at its term. The incumbent airline can renew it, and in the years this primer covers most did.

<div class="defn"><b>Lease extension</b><p>A lease extension is the renewal of an expiring operating lease with the incumbent airline instead of taking the aircraft back. In 2023 to 2024, 75–90% of scheduled lease expiries on mid-life narrowbodies were being extended (Cirium Ascend, via Bloomberg); extension terms stretched to four to six years, and extension rents in some cases ran more than 40% above the age-specific rates agreed just before (IBA, November 2023). Part III §22 carries the full treatment. <span class="cite">[Cirium Ascend via Bloomberg and AJOT (American Journal of Transportation), date not visible, context 2023–24; IBA Weekly, 24 Nov 2023; Key.Aero, "Lease trends"]</span></p></div>

An extension matters here because it changes the return-condition arithmetic. The redelivery that would have triggered a settlement is postponed, the engines fly further toward their next shop visit, and the reserves or compensation keep accruing against a later date. IBA recorded cases in which aircraft due back at year twelve were not expected to return until 2032, at eighteen years of age. <span class="cite">[IBA Weekly, 24 Nov 2023]</span> Section 15 carries what that did to rents.

### 12.6 Transferring a lease: lease-attached trading and novation

A leased aircraft is often sold while the lease is running. The buyer wants the lease as much as the aircraft, because the lease is the cash flow.

<div class="defn"><b>Lease-attached trading</b><p>Lease-attached trading is buying an aircraft from another lessor with the existing lease transferred to the buyer, so that the buyer acquires a rent stream and a return-condition obligation rather than an empty aircraft. The lease is what makes the asset financeable: lenders and securitization investors underwrite contracted rent from a known airline. Part VIII §60 and §67 cover FTAI's own transactions of this kind. <span class="cite">[definition assembled in the research, D6 §2.4; D7 §2.4]</span></p></div>

The legal step by which the lease moves is a novation: the old lessor, the new lessor and the airline agree that the new lessor takes the old lessor's place in the contract, with the airline's consent. No source in the research describes novation specifically; the word is used here in its ordinary legal sense to name the step the dossier describes as the lease being "transferred", and nothing in this primer turns on it. What does turn on it is that an on-lease aircraft changes hands with its deposit, its reserve balances and its return conditions attached, so the buyer inherits the whole money flow described in this section.

### 12.7 Residual value

The last term is not in the contract but sits behind every number in it.

<div class="defn"><b>Residual value and residual-value risk</b><p>Residual value is the lessor's forecast of what the asset will be worth, or fetch in a part-out, at the end of its depreciation life. Residual-value risk is the risk that the realised figure differs. Under an operating lease that risk stays with the lessor, which is what distinguishes it from a finance lease. FTAI's depreciation policy for leasing equipment, meaning the useful lives and residual assumptions it uses, was not returned by the research (D6 U8). <span class="cite">[definition assembled in the research, D6 §2.1]</span></p></div>

The parties who put numbers on residual value, and on current value, are the appraisers Part I §8 defined: IBA, mba Aviation, Ascend by Cirium and AVITAS (a US appraisal firm) publish base values and market values at half-life, and the market trades and the rating agencies test on their figures. Part I §8 also defined base value (the appraiser's opinion of the asset's underlying economic value in a balanced market) and market value (the most likely trading price in the actual current market, above base value when supply is tight and below it when soft). This Part uses those definitions without repeating them. What it adds is the observation that the research reached market values for the CFM56-7B (Exhibit 2.3) and no base values for any engine, and no appraised values of any kind for a 12-to-15-year-old 737-800 or A320ceo (D1 U9; D6 U9). <span class="cite">[IBA, Sept 2025; reconciliation cluster K-02]</span>

## 13. Maintenance reserves, return conditions and the lease-end settlement (worked example)

The maintenance terms are where most of the money in a mid-life lease actually sits. The reason is arithmetic. A performance restoration on a CFM56 costs between $1.2M and $2.5M on the two consultancy ranges Part I carried, and more with life-limited parts; an aircraft carries two such engines; and a six-year lease consumes a large fraction of a restoration interval on each. So the payments for life consumed can approach half of the basic rent, and the settlement at the end can be a multi-million-dollar negotiation. This section explains the mechanism, then works it through the one example the research built.

### 13.1 How a reserve is set and spent

A reserve is collected by component because each component has its own clock. The airframe has heavy checks on a calendar and flight-hour schedule. The landing gear has an overhaul interval. The auxiliary power unit has its own hours. Each engine has two clocks of its own: the performance-restoration clock, measured in hours or cycles since the last restoration, and the life-limited-part clock, measured in cycles against each part's certified limit (Part I §4 and §5). <span class="cite">[mba Aviation, "Maintenance Matters in Aircraft ABS Deals", undated; ABS is the asset-backed securitization §17 defines; Maheshwari & Co., undated]</span>

<div class="defn"><b>Auxiliary power unit (APU)</b><p>The auxiliary power unit is the small gas turbine in the aircraft's tail that supplies electrical power and compressed air on the ground and in emergencies. It is one of the components a lease reserves for separately, and one of the higher-value items when an airframe is parted out. Part V §32 carries the part-out treatment. <span class="cite">[D3 §2.2; D6 §2.1]</span></p></div>

The rate for each component is set so that the balance accumulated by the next event roughly covers it. For an engine restoration the rate is per flight hour; for life-limited parts it is per flight cycle, because the parts are certified in cycles (Part I §4). No source the research reached gives the rates a lessor actually charges on a CFM56 lease; the example below uses rates labelled as assumed, and the research recorded the gap (D6 U7; reconciliation C.2 item 6). <span class="cite">[D6 §2.2]</span>

Spending the reserve follows one rule, quoted from a lessee's own filing: the lessor reimburses "the lesser of (1) the amount of the maintenance reserve held by the lessor associated with the specific maintenance event or (2) the qualifying costs related to the specific maintenance event". <span class="cite">[Spirit Airlines 10-K FY2013]</span> Two consequences follow. If the event costs more than the balance, the lessee funds the difference. If the event costs less, the lessor keeps the rest. And because the reserve covers only life consumed since the lease began, not life consumed by earlier operators, a reserve rarely covers a whole shop visit unless the engine was delivered fresh from one. <span class="cite">[D6 §2.2]</span>

For the lessor, reserve receipts are not income when they arrive. They are a liability, carried as maintenance deposits, until the event is reimbursed or the lease ends with the balance unclaimed, at which point the unclaimed balance becomes revenue. <span class="cite">[WLFC Q3 2025 release; D11 §2.2]</span> The size of that line at an engine lessor shows how much of the money is maintenance rather than rent. Willis Lease reported maintenance reserve revenue of $76.1M in the third quarter of 2025 alone, up 52.8% on a year earlier, against lease rent of $76.6M in the same quarter; for the full year 2025 its combined "core lease rent and maintenance reserve revenues" were $523.6M, of which lease rent was $291.6M, leaving $232.0M of maintenance reserve revenue, a figure derived in the research by subtraction. <span class="cite">[WLFC Q3 2025 release; WLFC 10-K FY2025; derived in D11 §2.2]</span> At AerCap, whose fleet is young, "maintenance rents and other receipts" were $690M in 2025 against basic lease rents of $6,679M, about 9%. <span class="cite">[AerCap Q4 2025 release]</span> The gap between 9% and 44% is the gap between a young fleet far from its shop visits and a mid-life engine book close to them. <span class="cite">[D11 §2.2]</span>

### 13.3 The worked example

The research built one example of a full lease with reserves and a lease-end settlement. Its only sourced input is the rent. Every other input is an assumption chosen to show the arithmetic, and the shop-visit cost it assumed, $4.5M, sits above every sourced range Part I carried, so the example below shows the sourced alternatives beside it. <span class="cite">[D6 §2.2; reconciliation R-013, E.1 rule 4]</span>

<div class="worked"><div class="h">Worked example 2.2 — A 12-year-old 737-800 on a six-year operating lease with maintenance reserves, and the settlement at the end</div>
<p>Inputs. Rent $228,000 a month (sourced: IBA, July 2025). Term 72 months (assumed). Utilization 250 flight hours (FH) and 140 flight cycles (FC) a month per engine (assumed; that is 1,680 cycles a year, a little above the 1,000–1,500 cycles a year Part I uses for a mature narrowbody from Aircraft Value News, and kept as the research set it). Performance-restoration reserve $150 per flight hour per engine and life-limited-part reserve $100 per flight cycle per engine (assumed; no sourced rates, D6 U7). One performance restoration on engine 1 in month 48 (assumed timing). Its cost: $4.5M as the research assumed, shown beside the two consultancy ranges Part I carries for a restoration, $1.8–2.5M (midpoint $2.15M) and $1.2–1.6M (midpoint $1.4M), all from Safe Fly Aviation, a consultancy blog, 2026. At delivery both engines had flown 8,000 hours since their last restoration, and the return condition caps redelivery at 12,000 hours since restoration (both assumed).</p>
<p>Step 1, basic rent over the term.</p>
<div class="eqblock">228,000 × 72 = $16,416,000</div>
<p>Step 2, reserves accrued per engine over 72 months, then for two engines.</p>
<div class="eqblock">restoration reserve: 150 × 250 × 72 = $2,700,000 per engine
LLP reserve: 100 × 140 × 72 = $1,008,000 per engine
two engines: 5,400,000 + 2,016,000 = $7,416,000
reserves as a share of rent: 7,416,000 ÷ 16,416,000 = 45%</div>
<p>So on these assumptions the maintenance cash flow is not a side-show; it is almost half the size of rent.</p>
<p>Step 3, the month-48 shop visit on engine 1. The restoration balance held for that engine at month 48 is 150 × 250 × 48 = $1,800,000. The lessor reimburses the lesser of the balance and the qualifying cost.</p>
<div class="eqblock">at the research's assumed cost of $4,500,000: lessor reimburses 1,800,000; lessee funds 2,700,000
at the $2,150,000 midpoint of the $1.8–2.5M range: lessor reimburses 1,800,000; lessee funds 350,000
at the $1,400,000 midpoint of the $1.2–1.6M range: lessor reimburses 1,400,000; lessor keeps 400,000</div>
<p>The three lines show the "lesser of" rule doing its work. At either of the higher costs the lessee pays the excess, because the reserve covers only the 48 months of life consumed under this lease, not the 8,000 hours consumed before delivery. At the lowest cost the reserve more than covers the visit and the lessor keeps the surplus. After month 48, engine 1 accrues restoration reserves again from zero.</p>
<p>Step 4, lease end on engine 2, which had no shop visit. It was delivered at 8,000 hours since restoration and flew 250 × 72 = 18,000 hours, so it would be at 26,000 hours since restoration. The return condition caps redelivery at 12,000. The lessee must therefore either perform a restoration before redelivery, claiming up to its $2,700,000 balance against the cost, or negotiate a cash settlement. Under a pure reserve regime, if the lessee redelivers out of condition the lessor keeps the $2,700,000 restoration balance and the $1,008,000 LLP balance and pursues the shortfall. Under an end-of-lease compensation regime with no reserves paid, the lessee would owe compensation for 18,000 hours of restoration life consumed at an agreed rate per hour, plus compensation for 140 × 72 = 10,080 cycles of life-limited-part life at an agreed rate per cycle, in one lump at redelivery instead of monthly.</p>
<p>Step 5, what the lessor saw in cash over the lease, at the research's assumed visit cost.</p>
<div class="eqblock">rent received: $16,416,000
reserves received: $7,416,000
reimbursed: −$1,800,000
net reserve retention: 7,416,000 − 1,800,000 = $5,616,000</div>
<p>That $5.6M is not profit. It is the lessor's compensation for the maintenance life that walked out of the engines during the lease, to be spent, or forgone, when the lessor or the next lessee opens them. At the $1.4M visit cost the retention would be $6.0M, because less was reimbursed, and the lessee would have paid for more life than it used on engine 1.</p>
<p>Limits of the example: only the rent is sourced; the reserve rates, utilization, visit timing and return condition are assumed; the $4.5M visit cost sits above every sourced range and the two consultancy ranges disagree with each other; the engine's life-limited-part reserve is never spent here because no life-limited part is replaced, which is the common case within a single six-year lease on parts with 20,000-to-30,000-cycle limits. Which set of life-limited-part prices a lessor uses to size that reserve, $4M or $5.7M for a full -7B set, is the tension Part I §4 carried (R-016). <span class="cite">[D6 §2.2; IBA, Jul 2025; Safe Fly Aviation, 2026; Spirit Airlines 10-K FY2013; reconciliation R-013, R-016]</span></p></div>

The example also shows why the maintenance condition at delivery is negotiated as hard as the rent. The lessee's exposure on engine 2 arose because the engine was delivered with 8,000 hours already consumed and the return condition was tighter than the delivery condition. A lessee who takes an engine fresh from a restoration and returns it at the same condition owes nothing; one who takes it part-worn and must return it fresh is buying the previous operator's consumption. In practice the delivery and return conditions are written to match, and the reserve or compensation covers the difference.

### 13.4 Where a lessor's return comes from

A lease generates rent and reserves. The lessor's return is what is left after the asset's loss of value and the cost of the money that funded it. The research set out the decomposition in four lines.

<div class="box"><div class="h">The four lines of a lessor's return</div><p>Over the holding period a lessor earns (a) rental yield, meaning rent received, which is the lease rate factor times value, plus any net maintenance-reserve retention; minus (b) depreciation, the fall in the asset's value from purchase to disposal, or the book charge toward a residual; minus (c) financing cost, the interest on the debt that funds the asset and the cost of the equity; plus or minus (d) end-of-life proceeds, the sale price or part-out proceeds against the carrying residual, plus any end-of-lease compensation collected. An older asset has a higher lease rate factor and most of its depreciation behind it, but more maintenance exposure and a shorter remaining lease life, so line (d) matters more for it. In FTAI's structure line (d) is routed into the Aerospace Products segment: the 10 off-lease 737-700s bought from WestJet "will support FTAI's Aerospace Products business by expanding the Company's supply of CFM56-7B engines and modules" (Part VIII §67). <span class="cite">[definition assembled in the research, D6 §2.1; FTAI release, 28 Sep 2026]</span></p></div>

<div class="worked"><div class="h">Worked example 2.3 — Decomposing a lessor's return into yield, depreciation, financing cost and residual</div>
<p>Inputs, every one assumed for illustration and none of them market data: the aircraft is bought at value V; the lease rate factor is 1.0% a month, so rent is 12% of V a year; straight-line depreciation is 3.5% of V a year toward a residual; debt funds 70% of V at 6.5%, so interest is 0.70 × 6.5% = 4.55% of V a year; equity funds the other 30%. For a sense of scale only, the $228,000 rent of worked example 2.2 at a 1.0% factor implies V of $22.8M, which is not an appraised value (D6 U9).</p>
<div class="eqblock">rent yield: 12.00% of V
less depreciation: −3.50% of V
less interest: −4.55% of V
net cash return on total capital: 12.00 − 3.50 − 4.55 = 3.95% of V
on the 30% equity slice: 3.95 ÷ 0.30 = 13.2% of equity</div>
<p>Now change one input. At a lease rate factor of 0.8% a month, rent is 9.6% of V, and the net return falls to 9.6 − 3.5 − 4.55 = 1.55% of V, or 5.2% on equity. At 1.2% a month it rises to 14.4 − 8.05 = 6.35% of V, or 21.2% on equity. The leverage multiplies both directions: a 0.2-point move in the monthly factor moves the equity return by about eight points on these inputs.</p>
<p>Two further flows sit outside those lines. Net maintenance-reserve retention adds cash, $5.6M over six years in worked example 2.2, which on V of $22.8M is about 4.1% of V a year, but it is matched by the restoration cost the lessor will face before re-leasing; it is timing, not margin, unless the asset is parted out instead. End-of-life proceeds against the carrying residual add or subtract, and for an aircraft whose value sits mostly in its engines (§15) that line is largely the engines' value. Downtime between leases, remarketing costs and end-of-lease shortfalls subtract. Limits of the example: every percentage is assumed; FTAI's own depreciation policy and cost of debt by asset were not retrieved (D6 U8); Part VIII §58 works FTAI's implied gross yield from its filed balances and Part XI §88 sets it beside Willis Lease and Air Lease. <span class="cite">[D6 §2.2 step 6; D6 §2.7]</span></p></div>

The structure of that return explains the behaviour of the specialists this primer describes. A new-aircraft lessor earns a low factor on a slowly depreciating asset with long leases and little maintenance exposure. A mid-life engine lessor earns a higher factor on an asset whose value is close to the sum of its modules, with reserves nearly as large as rent and a part-out decision at every lease end. Section 14 puts filed numbers on both, and Part XI §88 sets FTAI's beside them.

## 14. Engine leasing: spare ratios, short-term leases, lease-rate factors

Everything in §12 and §13 applies to an engine leased on its own, with two differences. An engine is a smaller, more mobile asset than an aircraft, so its leases can be as short as a few months. And an engine's value is dominated by its maintenance condition, so the lease-end decision is sharper: restore and re-lease, lease out the remaining life, or take it apart. This section explains why airlines lease engines at all, how the leases are priced, and what the one listed pure-play engine lessor's numbers look like.

### 14.1 Why airlines lease spare engines

An airline needs more engines than it has engine positions, because engines come off the wing for scheduled shop visits and for unscheduled removals. Part I §6.4 defined the spare engine and the spare ratio, spares as a percentage of installed engines, and quoted the statement of the German engine maker MTU Aero Engines (MTU) that airlines "used to have about 15% of spare engines of their installed engines, but this figure is now down to 10% and is expected to further decrease to about 7–8% for newer engine types". <span class="cite">[MTU AEROREPORT, undated]</span> A consultancy argues the 10% rule "is no longer sufficient because engines are coming off wing more often and taking longer to repair". <span class="cite">[Acumen Aviation, undated (consultancy blog)]</span>

The economic reason to lease rather than own is that a spare earns nothing while it waits. Airlines "are less inclined to keep as many owned spare engines as they used to, mainly because of the significant investment involved in assets that generate very little return". <span class="cite">[MTU AEROREPORT, undated]</span> Leasing converts a lumpy multi-million-dollar purchase into a monthly cost, and in a lessor's pool one spare can serve several operators in turn. The sharpest form of the need has its own name.

<div class="defn"><b>Aircraft on ground (AOG)</b><p>An aircraft on ground is one that cannot fly for want of a serviceable part or engine. Short-term engine leases exist to cover AOG events and planned shop visits; a lessor that can deliver an engine within days is selling the airline the difference between a grounded aircraft and a flying one. <span class="cite">[MTU AEROREPORT, undated; FlightGlobal, "Freedom to be flexible", undated]</span></p></div>

### 14.2 Short-term and long-term leases, and shop-visit cover

Part I §6.4 defined the two durations. A short-term engine lease runs "a period of a few months to a maximum of three years" and is "ideal if a replacement engine is required for the duration of a shop visit"; a long-term lease runs for years and provides a permanent spare. <span class="cite">[MTU AEROREPORT, undated; FlightGlobal, undated]</span> The short-term lease is the one that ties engine leasing to the shop-visit market Part IV describes. A CFM56 restoration takes an engine away for 75 to 120 days on the figures Part I §6.3 carried, and when shops are full the wait for a place in the queue adds two to three months, up to six. <span class="cite">[Safe Fly Aviation, 2026 (consultancy blog); Aviation Business News, 2025; GE Aerospace, 27 May 2026; Bain, 2024; reconciliation R-019]</span>

<div class="defn"><b>Induction slot</b><p>An induction slot is the scheduled date on which an engine shop commits to start work on an engine; induction is the acceptance of the engine into work. When shops are full the wait for a slot is additional to the turnaround time and, in 2024, became the binding constraint, adding two to three months and up to six. Part IV §27 carries the full treatment. <span class="cite">[Bain, 2024; D2 §2.1, §2.4]</span></p></div>

When slots are delayed, "operators often turn to short-term or green-time engine leasing". <span class="cite">[MTU AEROREPORT, undated]</span> A green-time engine, which Part I §8.2 defined and §16 of this Part carries, is one with limited life left before its next shop visit, leased out to burn that life rather than be restored; its rent is lower. The lessor's product is therefore time: the months between removal and return, priced per month. FTAI's offer sits beside it as a different answer to the same problem.

<div class="defn"><b>Maintenance, Repair and Exchange (MRE)</b><p>Maintenance, Repair and Exchange is FTAI's name for its offer, a deliberate contrast with the industry's maintenance, repair and overhaul (MRO). Instead of restoring a customer's engine part by part while the customer waits, or leasing the customer a spare for the duration, FTAI removes the module or engine that triggered the visit, installs a serviceable one from its inventory, tests and returns the engine, and takes the unserviceable unit as feedstock. It is a variant of shop-visit cover in which the exchange replaces the spare. Part VII §46 carries the full treatment. <span class="cite">[FTAI 10-K FY2025; D5 §2.2; D6 §2.3]</span></p></div>

<div class="worked"><div class="h">Worked example 2.4 — How many engines a fleet has in the shop, and what the spare ratio must cover</div>
<p>Inputs: a fleet of 100 Boeing 737-800s, each with two CFM56-7B engines, so 200 installed engines (assumed fleet; two engines per aircraft sourced in Part I). Spare ratios of 15%, 10% and 7–8% (sourced: MTU). A mature performance-restoration interval of 10,000–15,000 cycles (sourced: Leeham News, 8 Mar 2024) at 1,000–1,500 cycles a year (sourced: Aircraft Value News), which is between 6.7 and 15 years between restorations; an average of ten years (assumed, close to the roughly ten years implied by 2,300 visits a year on about 24,000 engines, derived in the research) with eight years (Aviation Week's rule of thumb) and six years (the assumption one dossier used and asked to have replaced) as alternatives. Turnaround of 90 days (sourced: GE Aerospace, May 2026) or 120 days (sourced: trade press, 2025), with a slot wait of 60–90 days (sourced: Bain, 2024).</p>
<div class="eqblock">spares at 15%: 0.15 × 200 = 30;  at 10%: 20;  at 7–8%: 14–16
scheduled removals a year at a 10-year interval: 200 ÷ 10 = 20  (8 years: 25;  6 years: 33)
engines away at any time = removals × days away ÷ 365
10-year interval, 90 days: 20 × 90 ÷ 365 = 4.9
10-year interval, 120 days: 20 × 120 ÷ 365 = 6.6
10-year interval, 120 days plus a 90-day slot wait: 20 × 210 ÷ 365 = 11.5
8-year interval, 90 to 210 days: 6.2 to 14.4
6-year interval, 90 to 210 days: 8.1 to 19.0</div>
<p>So on the ten-year interval, scheduled restorations alone keep between five and twelve of the fleet's 200 engines away at any moment, and the rest of a 20-engine spare pool (10%) is what covers unscheduled removals and grounded aircraft. Lengthen the time away from 90 to 210 days and the scheduled requirement alone more than doubles. Shorten the interval to the six years one dossier assumed and the requirement at 210 days nearly consumes the whole 10% pool before any unscheduled event. That is the arithmetic behind the consultancy's claim that 10% "is no longer sufficient", and behind the demand for short-term leases when slots are delayed. Limits of the example: the interval, the fleet and the averaging are assumed; unscheduled removal rates were not sourced; an engine exchange from inventory, which aims at a quick-turn of about 45 days, would cut the days-away figures by half or more and is the mechanism Part VII §55 works through. <span class="cite">[MTU AEROREPORT, undated; Leeham News, 8 Mar 2024; Aircraft Value News, undated; GE Aerospace, 27 May 2026; Aviation Business News, 2025; Bain, 2024; Aviation Week, "Retirement Uptick", date not captured; reconciliation R-008, R-019; derived in D9 T1]</span></p></div>

## 15. Mid-life narrowbody economics: why the engines carry the value

A mid-life narrowbody is the aircraft at the centre of this primer: the Boeing 737NG and the Airbus A320ceo, powered by the CFM56-7B and CFM56-5B that Part I described, in the second half of their service lives. Part I §4 boxed the term with both of the research's framings, and they are consistent: roughly 10 to 18 years old (one dossier), or roughly the middle of a 25-to-30-year economic life (another), when an increasing share of the aircraft's value sits in its engines and those engines are approaching or past a shop visit. <span class="cite">[D6 §2.4; D7 §2.4; registry C-13]</span> This section explains what has happened to those aircraft's values and rents since 2023, why they are bought with leases attached, why the engines carry the value, and what happens to the airframe at the end.

### 15.2 Why mid-life aircraft are bought with a lease attached

A buyer of a 12-year-old aircraft almost always buys it with a lease already on it. There are two routes. In the first, the operating airline sells aircraft it owns to a lessor and rents them straight back, a sale-leaseback, which §17.7 defines; FTAI's September 2026 WestJet transaction, in which a vehicle bought 17 Boeing 737-700s "on lease to WestJet in a sale-leaseback", is the example in the research and belongs to Part VIII §67. <span class="cite">[FTAI release, 28 Sep 2026]</span> In the second, lease-attached trading (§12.6), the buyer takes the aircraft from another lessor with its lease transferred. <span class="cite">[D6 §2.4]</span>

The lease is what makes the asset financeable. Lenders and securitization investors underwrite contracted rent from a known airline for a known term, and the buyer's return over that term is the decomposition of worked example 2.3. <span class="cite">[D6 §2.4]</span> At the end of the term the owner has three choices: re-lease the aircraft, convert it to a freighter, or part it out. The value of that choice is mostly the engines, and the next subsection shows why with the one sourced illustration the research built.

<div class="box"><div class="h">Why the engines carry the value</div><p>Four facts from Part I combine. Their value is dominated by maintenance condition, so a pair fresh from restoration with full life-limited parts is worth several million dollars more than the same pair run-out. No new CFM56 is being built for an airframe, so every serviceable -7B and -5B that will ever exist already exists, while the airframes they power are being replaced. And the airframe at 15 to 20 years has few other uses, which is why the 21-year-old 737-800 rents for "less than $160,000" against $228,000 at 12 years while a -7B rents for the same whether it hangs on a 12-year-old wing or a 21-year-old one. The consequence FTAI states in its own words is that it "owns and leases jet aircraft which often facilitates the acquisition of engines at attractive prices", and the consequence for its third-party vehicles is that the vehicles buy the aircraft and FTAI does the engine work (Part VIII §61). <span class="cite">[Part I §3, §8; IBA, Jul 2025; FTAI Q2 2026 release, 29 Jul 2026; D6 §2.4]</span></p></div>

The floor under an engine's value is what it fetches taken apart. A consultancy blog puts the price of a run-out CFM56 bought as teardown feedstock at $0.8–1.2M and the used material it yields at $1.6–2.2M, and the same blog's "green-time value" of $2.8–3.4M for a -7B is a floor for engines with little life left; a full-life engine is worth substantially more. <span class="cite">[Safe Fly Aviation, 2026 (consultancy blog)]</span> So even at the end of an airframe's life, a pair of run-out -7Bs fetches $1.6–2.4M as feedstock and yields $3.2–4.4M of used material on those figures, before counting any life they still have. Section 16 takes up the trade in engines between those two states.

### 15.4 Freighter conversion as an end-of-life path

A passenger 737-800 at 18 to 25 years has one more use before part-out.

<div class="defn"><b>Freighter conversion, or passenger-to-freighter (P2F)</b><p>Freighter conversion is the structural modification of a used passenger airframe into a cargo aircraft: a main-deck cargo door, a strengthened floor, a rigid barrier between the cockpit and the hold, and a cargo loading system. It extends the airframe's revenue life by 10 to 15 years at low cycles a year. Narrowbodies are converted at 21–22 years old on average; more than 260 Boeing 737-800 freighters were in service in mid-2025 with a pipeline exceeding 300. The conversion cost and the availability of conversion slots were not retrieved (D6 U10). <span class="cite">[Cargo Facts, "Passenger-to-freighter conversions are taking place on increasingly older aircraft"; Acumen Aviation, 18 Dec 2025; Cargo Facts, "In-service 737NG freighter fleet surpasses 100"; D6 §2.4]</span></p></div>

<div class="defn"><b>Supplemental Type Certificate (STC)</b><p>A Supplemental Type Certificate is a regulator's approval of a change to a certified aircraft design. A freighter conversion is performed under an STC owned by the conversion house, which is why conversion capacity belongs to a few STC holders. In Part VI §37 the same instrument is one of the approval bases for alternative parts. <span class="cite">[D6 §2.4; D4 §2.2]</span></p></div>

Conversion changes what the engines are asked to do. A freighter flies few cycles a year, so cargo operators want engines "customized for cargo": lower-cycle, lower-cost workscopes that burn remaining life rather than restore it. On 7 July 2026 FTAI and Aeronautical Engineers Inc. (AEI), a conversion house with "625+ aircraft modified using AEI's STCs", announced a collaboration on a 737-800 freighter solution built on such engines; Part VIII §67 carries it. <span class="cite">[FTAI and AEI release, 7 Jul 2026]</span> Two signals about the conversion market sit side by side in the research and are carried as a pair. The type-specialist data has more than 260 737-800 freighters in service and a pipeline above 300, and an earlier appraiser note had freighter values rising while passenger narrowbody rents receded. The same appraiser warned in September 2026 of an "oversupply of narrowbody conversions". <span class="cite">[Acumen Aviation, Dec 2025; Cargo Facts; IBA via Aeroin, 2025; IBA via India Seatrade News, 6 Sep 2026; reconciliation R-034]</span> The primer records both and takes no view on which describes the market. What the conversion path does for this primer's subject is mechanical: it keeps -7B engines on wing past passenger retirement age, in a low-cycle duty that suits green-time modules, and it removes those airframes and engines from the teardown pool for a decade or more. Part III §24 carries that as a row of the translation table.

## 16. Green time and the trade in part-worn engines

Between a fresh engine worth its half-life value plus an adjustment and a run-out engine worth its parts lies a market in time. This section explains what green time is, how it is leased and traded, and how its value differs from the appraiser's half-life figure.

### 16.1 Green time, the three clocks, and the engines that have it

Part I §8.2 boxed green time: the remaining usable life on an engine or module before its next mandatory shop visit, set by whichever runs out first. The research describes three clocks. The first is the life-limited part with the fewest cycles remaining, because the engine must come off the wing before any such part reaches its limit (Part I §4). The second is exhaust-gas temperature (EGT) margin, the gap between the temperature the engine runs at and its red line, which erodes as the engine wears and which a performance restoration recovers (Part I §5). The third is any hard-time inspection or airworthiness directive that requires the engine to be opened. <span class="cite">[D3 §2.3; D2 §2.3; registry C-2]</span> Green time is the quantity; a green-time engine, or a green-time lease, adds an intention. A green-time engine is "one that still has usable time but whose owner does not intend to pay for the next shop visit, either because the engine is near the end of its life or because the owner's fleet is leaving", sold or leased as-is to run down the remaining time and then torn down. <span class="cite">[D3 §2.3]</span>

<div class="defn"><b>Used serviceable material (USM)</b><p>Used serviceable material is a part taken from a retired or dismantled aircraft or engine, inspected and if necessary repaired to the manufacturer's manual, and released on an airworthiness tag with its full history so that it can be installed again. A part with a gap in its paperwork is scrap whatever its condition. Used material is made by the original manufacturer, so it sits outside the alternative-parts debate, but it competes for the same slot in a shop visit's parts bill; its supply is a lagged function of retirements. Part V §31 carries the full treatment. <span class="cite">[D3 §2.1; D2 §8]</span></p></div>

<div class="defn"><b>Run-out (exhausted) engine</b><p>A run-out or exhausted engine is one with no usable time left before a shop visit: one of its clocks has reached zero. It is the only kind of engine that becomes teardown feedstock while engines with life are leased or sold whole, because a half-life -7B at $5.7–6.4M (IBA, September 2025) is worth several times the $1.6–2.2M of used material a teardown yields (consultancy blog, 2026). Part V §33 carries the full treatment. <span class="cite">[D3 §2.2, §2.3; IBA, Sept 2025; Safe Fly Aviation, 2026]</span></p></div>

### 16.2 The green-time lease

A green-time lease is a short fixed-term operating lease, from months to a few years, at a monthly rent, with the engine returned at or near run-out rather than restored. Because the lessee will not restore the engine, the rent is effectively the price of the time consumed, and the return conditions of §12.4 are inverted: the lessor expects the engine back with less life, not more. <span class="cite">[D3 §2.3]</span> The sequence has a sourced example. The engine trader APOC Aviation (APOC) leased a CFM56-5A to Condor for twelve months on a green-time lease, with the engine "expected to return to APOC's portfolio for teardown and part-out next year after the lease concludes". <span class="cite">[AviTrader, 28 Jul 2025; Aviation Business News, 2025]</span>

The rents the research reached for green-time engines are the consultancy blog's, labelled as such: CFM56-7B $42,000–48,000 a month, "about 34% above 2019" and up 15–20% "in recent months"; CFM56-5B $38,000–44,000. <span class="cite">[Safe Fly Aviation, 2026 (consultancy blog)]</span> Whether those are green-time rents is the research's inference from the page's layout; the appraiser's $100,000 spare-engine rent is the other side of the pair (§14.3, R-028). The appraiser's September 2025 release describes the -7B market as having "a distinct lack of availability", with market values and lease rates above long-term trend, which is the condition in which green-time engines are in demand: when shops are full, "operators often turn to short-term or green-time engine leasing". <span class="cite">[IBA, Sept 2025; MTU AEROREPORT, undated]</span> The engine maker sells the same thing: green-time leases are one element of GE's TrueChoice Transitions menu, which Part IV §28 carries. <span class="cite">[D2 §2.3]</span>

### 16.3 The owner's choice, and the trade in part-worn engines

An engine lessor faces the same decision at every lease end, and a mid-life aircraft owner faces it for each engine when the airframe retires: restore the engine and lease it again at a full-life rent, lease out its remaining green time, or part it out now. <span class="cite">[D6 §2.3]</span> The trade in part-worn engines is the market in which those three options are priced against each other, and it is where the module as a unit of trade (Part I §8; Part V §36) comes from: a core with 80% of its life-limited-part life is worth far more than one with 20%, because the next owner's shop visit will or will not have to open it. <span class="cite">[D3 §2.7]</span>

### 16.4 How green-time value differs from half-life value

The appraiser's half-life market value and the consultancy blog's green-time value are not two estimates of one quantity. They are two quantities. Part I §8 gave the arithmetic that connects them: an engine's maintenance-adjusted value is its half-life value plus, for each clock, the actual remaining fraction less one half, times the cost to restore that clock. An engine with full-life life-limited parts is worth the half-life value plus half the full-life set; a run-out engine is worth the half-life value less half the set less half a restoration, which for an old engine "is approximately the teardown value". <span class="cite">[D3 §2.4; Part I §8.1]</span> A green-time engine sits between those points, and its value falls toward the run-out figure as its clocks run down.

## 17. Financing: securitizations, warehouse facilities, sale-leasebacks (worked tranching example)

A lessor's return in worked example 2.3 had a financing line of 4.55% of value, and the equity return of 13% depended on borrowing 70% of the price at 6.5%. Where that debt comes from, how it is layered, and what it costs the equity when rents fall is the subject of this section. The instruments are general to the industry, and the sourced template is the engine-lessor's: Willis Lease's rated securitizations. FTAI's own vehicles use the same instruments, and the facts specific to them belong to Part VIII; this section defines the instruments and points there.

### 17.1 The vehicle that holds the assets

Almost every form of asset-level debt in this industry starts with a company that exists only to own a defined set of aircraft or engines.

<div class="defn"><b>Special-purpose vehicle (SPV)</b><p>A special-purpose vehicle is a bankruptcy-remote legal entity that exists only to own a defined pool of assets and the debt secured on them, insulated from the sponsor's other liabilities. Bankruptcy-remote means that if the sponsor fails, the SPV's assets are not caught up in the sponsor's insolvency, so a lender to the SPV looks only to those assets and their leases. It is the issuer in a securitization, the borrower under a warehouse facility, and the asset-owning layer beneath a partnership such as FTAI's Strategic Capital vehicles, which FTAI's filings describe as acquiring aircraft through "special purpose entities (SPVs) of the 2025 Partnership". Aircraft lessors are usually organised this way, with each aircraft or sub-pool in its own entity so that lenders and lessees deal with a clean owner. Part VIII §61 and §62 carry FTAI's vehicles. <span class="cite">[FTAI 10-Q Q1 2026; definition assembled in the research, D6 §2.5, D7 §2.1]</span></p></div>

The SPV is the reason a lessor can borrow against a pool of leased engines at a cost that reflects the engines and the airlines renting them rather than the lessor's own credit. Everything in this section is a way of lending to such a vehicle.

### 17.2 The securitization and its parts

<div class="defn"><b>Asset-backed securitization (ABS)</b><p>An asset-backed securitization is a transaction in which a lessor sells a portfolio of leased aircraft or engines to a bankruptcy-remote SPV, which issues notes to investors secured on the assets and on their lease cash flows. The notes are divided into tranches paid in order of seniority. The lessor usually keeps the equity, the residual claim after the notes, and acts as servicer of the leases for a fee. Rating agencies, KBRA (Kroll Bond Rating Agency) in the two deals this section uses, assign ratings to the notes after testing the structure against stressed assumptions for lease-rate declines, downtime, maintenance costs and residual values. <span class="cite">[KBRA, 3 Jun 2025 and 10 Dec 2025; definition assembled in the research, D6 §2.5]</span></p></div>

<div class="defn"><b>Tranche: senior, mezzanine and equity</b><p>A tranche is one layer of the claims on a securitization, paid in order of seniority. The senior notes (Class or Series A) are paid first, carry the lowest coupon and the highest rating. The mezzanine notes (Class B, and sometimes C) are paid after the A notes and carry higher coupons and lower ratings. The equity, sometimes issued as E-certificates, is paid last and absorbs the first losses. Because every dollar of shortfall hits the equity first and every dollar of surplus reaches it last, tranching concentrates both the upside and the downside in the layer the lessor keeps. <span class="cite">[definition assembled in the research, D6 §2.5; KBRA, 10 Dec 2025]</span></p></div>

<div class="defn"><b>Loan-to-value (LTV)</b><p>Loan-to-value is the note balance divided by the appraised value of the assets in the pool. The senior notes might be sized to 60–70% of appraised value and the senior and mezzanine notes together to 75–80%, leaving equity of 20–25%; those ranges are illustrative, because the two deals the research retrieved did not return their loan-to-value ratios (D6 U6). It is distinct from loan-to-cost, which divides debt by the total capital of a vehicle and which Part VIII §61 uses for FTAI's 2025 vehicle. <span class="cite">[definition assembled in the research, D6 §2.5; D7 §2.3]</span></p></div>

<div class="defn"><b>Payment waterfall (ABS)</b><p>The payment waterfall is the ordered allocation of the pool's collections on each payment date: fees and expenses first, then interest on the A notes, then interest on the B notes, then scheduled principal on the A and B notes, then any C notes, with any surplus to the equity. Part VIII §63 uses the same word for a partnership's distribution order, which allocates cash among partners rather than among note classes; the primer says "payment waterfall" for the first and "distribution waterfall" for the second. <span class="cite">[definition assembled in the research, D6 §2.5; registry C-17]</span></p></div>

<div class="defn"><b>Anticipated repayment date (ARD) and legal final maturity</b><p>The anticipated repayment date is the date after which a securitization sweeps all excess cash to pay down principal faster and steps up the coupon on the notes, so that the deal is designed to be refinanced or called at that date. The legal final maturity is the much later last date by which the notes must be repaid. In Willis Lease's ninth engine securitization the two dates are December 2031 and December 2050, a gap of about nineteen years: the structure expects to be refinanced at the first date and treats the second as a backstop. <span class="cite">[KBRA, 10 Dec 2025; Asset Securitization Report, Dec 2025]</span></p></div>

The rating agencies are the gate. The notes carry ratings because an agency, KBRA in the two deals below, has modelled the pool's rents, the lessees' credit, the time engines spend off lease between lessees, the maintenance they will need and the value they will have at the end, under stresses, and found the senior notes able to pay through those stresses. <span class="cite">[KBRA, 3 Jun 2025; KBRA, 10 Dec 2025]</span> That is why the lease-rate and value movements of §14 and §15 matter beyond the lessor's own income statement: an appraiser's report that engine values have "levelled off" or that narrowbody rents fell 11–13% feeds straight into the stress tests that decide how much senior debt a pool can carry. <span class="cite">[IBA, H1 2026; IBA, Jul 2025; D6 §5]</span>

It is arithmetic on a real deal's size, not a description of the deal.

<div class="worked"><div class="h">Worked example 2.8 — Tranching a $392.9M engine securitization on WEST IX's size, and what a fall in rents does to each layer</div>
<p>Inputs. Total notes $392.9M on 49 assets (sourced: KBRA and Asset Securitization Report, December 2025). Anticipated repayment date December 2031, legal final December 2050 (sourced: KBRA). Servicer and administrative-agent fees 13.5% of net rents (sourced rate: WLFC 10-K FY2019 and FY2020; applied here to gross rent as an approximation, since the definition of "net rents" was not retrieved). Everything below is assumed for illustration: the senior and subordinated notes together are 75% of appraised value; the Class A notes alone are 65%; the A coupon is 5.5% and the B coupon 7.5%; the pool earns a lease rate factor of 1.0% a month; other expenses (insurance, remarketing, downtime) are $3.0M a year; scheduled principal is 4% of the notes a year.</p>
<div class="eqblock">implied appraised value: 392.9 ÷ 0.75 = $523.9M  (per asset: 523.9 ÷ 49 = $10.7M)
Class A: 0.65 × 523.9 = $340.5M  (65% LTV)
Class B: 392.9 − 340.5 = $52.4M  (A+B: 75% LTV)
equity: 523.9 − 392.9 = $131.0M  (25%)
annual interest: A 0.055 × 340.5 = 18.7;  B 0.075 × 52.4 = 3.9;  total $22.6M
annual rent at 1.0% a month: 0.01 × 523.9 × 12 = $62.9M
servicer and agent fees: 0.135 × 62.9 = $8.5M</div>
<p>The waterfall in a normal year:</p>
<div class="eqblock">rent 62.9 − fees 8.5 − other expenses 3.0 = 51.4 available for the notes
− A interest 18.7 − B interest 3.9 = 28.8 after interest
− scheduled principal 15.7 = 13.1 to the equity
equity cash yield: 13.1 ÷ 131.0 = 10.0%
coverage of interest plus scheduled principal: 51.4 ÷ (22.6 + 15.7) = 1.34×</div>
<p>Now stress the rent as a rating agency would. If lease rates fall 15%, rent is $53.4M and the fees fall with it to $7.2M; the notes' interest and scheduled principal do not move.</p>
<div class="eqblock">at −15%: 53.4 − 7.2 − 3.0 − 22.6 − 15.7 = $4.9M to the equity  (3.7%);  coverage 43.2 ÷ 38.3 = 1.13×

<p>A 15% fall in rent cuts the equity's cash by nearly two-thirds, from $13.1M to $4.9M, while the A and B notes are paid in full. A 25% fall leaves nothing for the equity and a small shortfall against scheduled principal, which is the point at which a real structure's reserve accounts and cash-trap triggers would operate; they are not modelled here. That asymmetry is the tranching doing its job: the equity concentrates the downside so that the senior notes can carry an A rating, and it concentrates the upside, which is why the lessor keeps it. After the anticipated repayment date in December 2031 the surplus that reached the equity in a normal year would instead be swept to principal and the coupons would step up, so the deal is built to be refinanced then. ### 17.5 The warehouse facility

A securitization needs a pool large and seasoned enough to rate. The instrument that assembles the pool is a bank line.

<div class="defn"><b>Warehouse facility (warehouse line)</b><p>A warehouse facility is a revolving bank loan to an SPV, secured on whatever assets the SPV buys during an availability period, with an advance rate and a borrowing base, and with a term after which the balance must be repaid or refinanced, commonly by issuing a securitization. Assets are accumulated in it until there are enough to securitize, which is why it is called a warehouse. A lessor uses it to fund acquisitions one aircraft at a time without going to the bond market for each. FTAI's second Strategic Capital vehicle closed a $2.0B warehouse facility from 13 lenders on 14 August 2026; Part VIII §62 carries the vehicle. <span class="cite">[FTAI release, 17 Aug 2026; definition assembled in the research, D6 §2.5, D7 §2.2]</span></p></div>

<div class="defn"><b>Advance rate and borrowing base</b><p>The advance rate is the percentage of each asset's value that the banks will lend against it under a warehouse facility. The borrowing base is the total credit available at any moment, recomputed as assets are added, sold or revalued: the sum over the assets of value times advance rate. If values fall, the borrowing base falls, and the borrower must repay or add collateral. <span class="cite">[definition assembled in the research, D6 §2.5]</span></p></div>

<div class="defn"><b>Accordion</b><p>An accordion is a pre-agreed option to enlarge a credit facility if lenders agree to fund more. The 2026 vehicle's $2.0B warehouse carries a $1.0B accordion, so up to $3.0B. FTAI's statement that its Strategic Capital vehicles "have now raised $5.5 billion of warehouse financing in less than two years" matches the disclosed commitments of $2.5B (February 2025) and $2.0B (August 2026), which sum to $4.5B, only if the undrawn accordion is counted; the release does not say how the figure is built. Part VIII §62 carries the full treatment. <span class="cite">[FTAI release, 17 Aug 2026; FTAI release, 26 Feb 2025; reconciliation R-055]</span></p></div>

<div class="worked"><div class="h">Worked example 2.9 — What a warehouse line buys: advance rates, the accordion, and a vehicle's loan-to-cost</div>

### 17.6 How a lessor uses them together, and what that has to do with FTAI

The sequence a lessor follows is the same whether the lessor is Willis Lease or a vehicle managed by FTAI. Buy assets on the warehouse. Season the leases, meaning let them run long enough to show the collections a rating agency can model. Securitize the pool into a rated deal, which repays the warehouse and releases its capacity for the next pool. Keep the equity and the servicing fee. <span class="cite">[D6 §2.5]</span> Willis has done this ten times; the WEST VIII proceeds were partly used to refinance WEST IV, which is the same sequence one generation on. <span class="cite">[KBRA, 3 Jun 2025]</span>

<div class="defn"><b>Servicer and servicing fee</b><p>The servicer is the party contracted to manage the assets of a vehicle it does not wholly own: leasing and remarketing them, collecting rent and reserves, overseeing their maintenance condition, and selling them, for a fee. Willis is the servicer of the WEST deals at 11.5% of net rents plus 2.0% as administrative agent and 3.0% of sale proceeds; FTAI is the Servicer of its Strategic Capital vehicles, "receiving customary, market-based compensation", which amounted to $10.2M in 2025 and $12.85M in the first half of 2026; the rate and base are undisclosed. Part VIII §61 and §63 carry the full treatment. <span class="cite">[WLFC 10-K FY2019 and FY2020; FTAI 10-K FY2025; FTAI 10-Q Q2 2026; D7 §2.5]</span></p></div>

FTAI's third-party capital programme is built from the instruments this section has defined, with a partnership layered on top. Three terms are needed to read it, and Part VIII owns all three.

<div class="defn"><b>Strategic Capital Initiative (SCI)</b><p>The Strategic Capital Initiative is FTAI's asset-management arm, launched on 30 December 2024, which raises third-party capital into closed-end vehicles that buy on-lease, mid-life 737NG and A320ceo aircraft; FTAI manages the vehicles as Servicer, co-invests as a limited partner, and supplies the engine work on their fleets through its Maintenance, Repair and Exchange business. It is not a reportable segment. Part VIII §61 carries the full treatment. <span class="cite">[FTAI release, 30 Dec 2024; FTAI 10-K FY2025; D7 §2.1]</span></p></div>

<div class="defn"><b>The 2025 Partnership and the 2026 SPV</b><p>The 2025 Partnership is the first Strategic Capital vehicle: a closed-end partnership over asset-owning SPVs that closed at a $2.0B equity hard cap in October 2025 (against a $1.5B target), carries a $2.5B asset-level debt commitment made in February 2025, and had committed about $6.0B of total capital across more than 300 aircraft by August 2026, when FTAI described it as in its harvest phase; FTAI holds 19% (the FY2025 10-K and 2026 10-Qs) or 20% (the Q3 2025 10-Q) as a limited partner. The 2026 SPV is the second vehicle, which closed the $2.0B warehouse facility above on 14 August 2026 and made its first disclosed purchase in the WestJet transaction of 28 September 2026; its equity size and FTAI's stake were undisclosed at the research date. Both followed the warehouse-then-securitize shape described above. Part VIII §62 carries the full treatment. <span class="cite">[FTAI releases, 26 Feb 2025, 27 Oct 2025, 17 Aug 2026 and 28 Sep 2026; FTAI 10-K FY2025; FTAI 10-Q Q3 2025; reconciliation R-048]</span></p></div>

<div class="defn"><b>Seed Assets</b><p>Seed Assets is FTAI's term for the on-lease aircraft it sold from its own book to start the 2025 Partnership: 46 aircraft at "an estimated net purchase price of $549 million" at launch. The sales are why FTAI's leasing equipment at cost fell from $2,963.5M to $2,057.6M during 2025 and why its lease income roughly halved year on year in the first half of 2026. Part VIII §60 carries the full treatment. <span class="cite">[FTAI release, 30 Dec 2024; FTAI 10-K FY2025; FTAI 10-Q Q1 2026]</span></p></div>

The vehicles carry two layers of debt in the sense this section has defined them: asset-level debt borrowed by the SPVs and secured on the aircraft and their leases, with no recourse to FTAI, and a warehouse facility within that layer for the second vehicle. <span class="cite">[FTAI release, 26 Feb 2025; FTAI release, 17 Aug 2026; D7 §2.2]</span> The lessor's incentive to sell assets into such vehicles is to recycle equity: it books a gain on sale, keeps a servicing stream and, in FTAI's case, keeps the engine maintenance work on the fleet it sold. <span class="cite">[D6 §2.5; D7 §2.5]</span> The equity it keeps is a 19% or 20% limited-partner stake rather than the whole E-certificate layer a securitization sponsor usually holds, which is one of the differences Part VIII §65 sets beside other platforms. The first vehicle has since used the securitization market itself. On 22 May 2026 FTAI priced FTAI MRE 2026-1, which it called the Strategic Capital vehicle's inaugural asset-backed securitization: $612M of notes backed by 48 A320ceo and 737NG aircraft on lease to 23 airlines, in two classes, Series A expected to be rated Asf by Fitch and A(sf) by KBRA and Series B expected BBB+sf by Fitch, with closing expected on 4 June 2026 and both classes described as significantly oversubscribed. The same release put the vehicle's fleet at 292 aircraft. So the deal is a take-out within the vehicle's asset-level layer, of the kind §17.6 describes, not a borrowing by FTAI itself. Its coupons and loan-to-value were not in the research (D7 U12, now partly resolved). <span class="cite">[FTAI release, "FTAI Aviation Prices Inaugural Asset-Backed Securitization", 22 May 2026, via Finviz and AviTrader 26 May 2026]</span>

### 17.7 Sale-leasebacks

The third instrument is not a loan at all from the lessor's side, but it is the airline's way of financing an aircraft and the lessor's way of acquiring one.

<div class="defn"><b>Sale-leaseback</b><p>A sale-leaseback is a transaction in which an airline sells aircraft it owns to a lessor and leases them straight back, converting owned assets into rent obligations and releasing cash to the airline. For the lessor it is the purest form of lease-attached acquisition: the aircraft arrives with a lease to a known operator already on it, which is what lenders and securitization investors underwrite. FTAI's WestJet transaction of 28 September 2026, in which the 2026 SPV acquired 17 Boeing 737-700s "on lease to WestJet in a sale-leaseback" while FTAI itself acquired 10 off-lease aircraft for their engines and modules, is the example in the research; the price and lease terms were not disclosed. Part VIII §67 carries it. <span class="cite">[FTAI release, 28 Sep 2026; definition assembled in the research, D6 §2.4]</span></p></div>

The sale-leaseback closes the loop this Part opened. The airline that sells its aircraft and rents it back is now a lessee under an operating lease (§12), paying rent and either reserves or end-of-lease compensation (§13), against return conditions that decide who pays for the engine life it consumes. The lessor that bought it funds the purchase on a warehouse line and later a securitization (§17), whose ratings rest on the appraisers' values (§12.7) and whose equity bears the swings in rent (§14, §15). And at the end of the lease the lessor faces the choice of §16: restore and re-lease, lease the green time, or take the engines out and sell them as modules, which is where Part VII begins.
