## 31. What makes a used part serviceable

A used part is not a lesser kind of part. It is the manufacturer's own part, taken from an engine or aircraft that has been in service, and put through a process that ends with a signed release. The process is what this section describes. At the end of it the part is "serviceable", a word with a legal meaning, and it can be bolted into another engine. Without that process the same piece of metal is scrap.

<div class="defn"><b>Used serviceable material (USM)</b><p>Used serviceable material is any aircraft or engine part that has been in service, has been removed, and has been inspected (and repaired if needed) to the manufacturer's manual and released on an airworthiness tag with its full history, so that it is legally fit to be installed again. It is OEM-made, so it sits outside the debate over alternative-manufacturer parts (Part VI), but it competes for the same line in a shop visit's parts bill. A part with a gap in its paperwork is scrap, whatever its physical condition. Its supply is a lagged function of retirements (§33). <span class="cite">[AJW Group via AviTrader, Aug and Oct 2025; D3 §2.1, the USM dossier behind this Part (D3)]</span></p></div>

The alternatives to a used part, when a shop needs to replace one, are three. A new OEM part is bought from the manufacturer at its published catalogue list price (CLP), the price Part I §4 glossed and Part IV §28 owns. An alternative-manufacturer part is one made by a third party under a Parts Manufacturer Approval (PMA) from the US Federal Aviation Administration (FAA); Part VI owns it. A repair is the third alternative, either one the manufacturer's manual describes or one approved separately by an FAA Designated Engineering Representative (DER), which Part VI §40 owns. This Part is about the first substitute only, the used part. <span class="cite">[D3 §2.1; D4 §2.1, the alternative-parts dossier (D4)]</span>

### 31.1 How a part leaves service, and the three places it can go

A part leaves service in one of three ways. It is removed at a shop visit, when the engine is open and inspection finds it out of limits or finds a better part to install. It is removed from an aircraft at retirement. Or the whole aircraft or engine is torn down, which the trade calls part-out. Part I §8 boxed part-out and teardown: part-out is the owner's decision to dismantle and sell the components; teardown is the physical disassembly. §32 carries the economics. <span class="cite">[D3 §2.1; Part I §8]</span>

From there each part goes one of three ways, and the three outcomes are the vocabulary of every teardown report.

<div class="defn"><b>Serviceable as removed</b><p>A removed part is serviceable as removed when it has been inspected to the applicable manual and found within limits. It is tagged and sold without repair. It is the cheapest outcome for the trader, because there is no repair bill, and the fastest, because there is no repair-shop queue. <span class="cite">[D3 §2.1]</span></p></div>

The second outcome is the repairable part. It is outside the manual's limits but can be brought back inside them. For an airframe component the limits and the permitted repairs are in the Component Maintenance Manual (CMM), the manufacturer's document for that component, which Part I §9 boxed. For an engine part they are in the Engine Shop Manual (ESM), also boxed in Part I §9. A repair the manual does not cover may be done under a separately approved DER scheme (Part VI §40). The part goes to a repair shop, is repaired, tested, tagged and sold. The repair cost is the main cost of goods for a trader in used material. <span class="cite">[D3 §2.1]</span>

<div class="defn"><b>Beyond economic repair (BER)</b><p>A removed part is beyond economic repair when it is outside limits and not worth repairing: the repair would cost more than the part would fetch, or no approved repair exists. It is scrapped. <span class="cite">[D3 §2.1]</span></p></div>

<div class="defn"><b>Life-expired</b><p>A life-limited part is life-expired when it has reached the cycle limit the manufacturer certified for it. It has no used-material value, however good its condition, and it must be mutilated so that it cannot re-enter the supply chain. Part I §4 carries the limits: 20,000 cycles for the -7B's compressor and turbine disks, 25,000 for the low-pressure turbine, 30,000 for the fan and booster. <span class="cite">[D3 §2.1; Part I §4]</span></p></div>

The exhibit explains a fact that recurs throughout this Part. Most used material is not serviceable as removed. It passes through a repair shop and is re-certified before it can be sold, so the capacity and price of repair shops (Part IV §27) set both the cost and the pace of the used-parts trade. Rising repair costs are named alongside falling supply as a driver of CFM56 and V2500 part prices (§34). <span class="cite">[Aviation Week, "Magnetic expects CFM56 market challenges", 2024; D3 §5]</span>

### 31.2 The release tag

A part becomes serviceable in the legal sense when an approved organisation signs it off. Part I §9 boxed the two forms and the release tag itself, so this is a reminder. In the FAA system the form is FAA Form 8130-3, the Authorized Release Certificate, also called the Airworthiness Approval Tag. For a repaired or inspected used part it is issued by a repair station certificated under Part 145 of the US regulations; for a new part it is issued by the holder of a production approval. In the European system the equivalent is Form 1 of the European Union Aviation Safety Agency (EASA), issued by an EASA Part-145 maintenance organisation or a Part-21 production organisation. Both certify that the part complies with the applicable regulations and is safe for use. <span class="cite">[Rotabull, 8130-3 instructions, undated; AJW Group via AviTrader, 2025]</span>

Many repair stations hold both approvals and issue a dual release, one tag valid in both systems. That matters commercially. A part tagged for one system only cannot be installed on an aircraft registered in the other without re-certification, so cross-border trade in used parts runs through dual-release shops. <span class="cite">[D3 §2.1, §5]</span>

The tag is necessary and not sufficient. Two further things decide whether a buyer will accept the part: its documented history (§31.3) and the identity of the organisation that signed the tag. The trade press is blunt about the second: the 8130-3 and Form 1 documents "can be easily generated", so buyers vet the supplier as well as the paperwork. Delta's published supplier matrix for aftermarket parts is one example of an airline codifying which supplier categories and which tag types it accepts. <span class="cite">[AJW Group via AviTrader, 2025; Delta TechOps supplier matrix, Mar 2024]</span>

### 31.3 Traceability: why a paperwork gap scraps a part

No regulator allows "untraceable" used parts. Everything must be documented, certified and compliant. <span class="cite">[AJW Group via AviTrader, 2025]</span> The documentation package a buyer requires differs by part class, and the class that matters most for engines is the one with the strictest rule.

<div class="defn"><b>Hard-time (HT) part</b><p>A hard-time part is one with a fixed overhaul or replacement interval, as distinct from an on-condition part that is replaced when inspection finds it worn. It needs complete operating-history records and evidence of compliance with every applicable Airworthiness Directive and Service Bulletin. <span class="cite">[AJW Group via AviTrader, 2025; D3 §2.1]</span></p></div>

Part I §3 boxed the two instructions just named. An Airworthiness Directive (AD) is a regulator's order making a fix or inspection mandatory; a Service Bulletin (SB) is the manufacturer's instruction to modify or inspect. Compliance records for both must travel with hard-time parts and with life-limited parts, and an AD inspection can itself be the clock that ends an engine's remaining usable time (Part II §16). <span class="cite">[Part I §3; D3 §2.1]</span>

For life-limited parts the standard is the one Part I §9 boxed as back-to-birth (BTB) traceability: an unbroken record of every cycle the part has accumulated since new, on which engine serial numbers and under which operators. The arithmetic is why the rule is absolute. Remaining life is the manufacturer's limit minus the documented cycles consumed. An undocumented period cannot be assumed to contain zero cycles. So a gap means the part cannot be shown to have any life, and a part that cannot be shown to have life has no value. <span class="cite">[D3 §2.1; Part I §9]</span>

<div class="box"><div class="h">Why a paperwork gap scraps a part</div><p>Consider a high-pressure turbine disk with a 20,000-cycle limit whose records show 11,000 cycles on three engines, then an eighteen-month period with no record, then 2,000 more cycles. The physical disk may have flown 13,000 cycles and have 7,000 left. But nobody can prove the eighteen months contained no flying. A buyer's engineer cannot write a remaining life into the receiving engine's records, so the part cannot be installed; the shop manual's remaining-life calculation has no input. The part is physically present and commercially absent, exactly as Part I §3 said of a module with a gap in its records. The usable supply of used life-limited parts is the documented supply, not the physical one. <span class="cite">[D3 §2.1; Part I §3, §9]</span></p></div>

Other documents are standard in a used-part package whatever the class: the removal tag from the last operator, the teardown report, a certificate of conformance from the seller, and a declaration that the part has never been through an accident.

<div class="defn"><b>Non-incident statement</b><p>A non-incident statement is the seller's declaration that a part was not installed on an aircraft involved in an accident, a fire or an immersion. It is a standard element of a used-material documentation package, beside the removal tag, the teardown report and the certificate of conformance. <span class="cite">[D3 §2.1]</span></p></div>

What makes a part unsaleable follows from the above. Missing documents mean the part will not be used. An unclear maintenance history or gaps in the paperwork are grounds for rejection. Mismatched part or serial numbers, unusual formatting on a tag, or an unfamiliar issuing organisation all trigger quarantine. <span class="cite">[AJW Group via AviTrader, 2025]</span>

<div class="defn"><b>Quarantine</b><p>Quarantine is the segregation of a part whose documentation is suspect, pending investigation. The triggers named in the trade press are mismatched part or serial numbers, unusual formatting on a release tag, and an issuing organisation the buyer does not recognise. <span class="cite">[AJW Group via AviTrader, 2025; D3 §2.1]</span></p></div>

### 31.4 Life-limited-part records in practice

Everything in §31.3 converges on the life-limited part, because it is the part whose used value is highest and whose paperwork rule is strictest. A used disk with life is the direct substitute for a new one at list price, and the next shop visit must replace any disk without enough life for the following run (Part I §4.2). So the trade advertises used life-limited parts on their documented remaining cycles, as Part I §4 showed with listings such as a core package at "6235 CR" (cycles remaining). The records summary that supports such a listing is the mini-pack Part I §4.1 boxed: each life-limited part with its certified limit and its cycles consumed. <span class="cite">[Part I §4; D3 §2.2]</span>

Two consequences for a teardown follow. First, the documented fraction of an engine's life-limited parts is a hard cap on what the teardown can yield; §32 returns to this. Second, when a module changes hands as a unit, its documentation must show the history of every life-limited part inside it, which is why §36 can treat the module as an engine in miniature. <span class="cite">[D3 §2.7, §5]</span>

## 32. Teardown and part-out economics

Part-out is a decision; teardown is the work that follows. This section explains how a retired aircraft or engine is bought, where its value sits, how long it takes to turn into cash, and what the one set of published engine figures says. It ends with the full worked example the outline asks for: a 737-800 with two CFM56-7B engines, with every input labelled sourced or unknown.

### 32.1 How a retired asset is bought: purchase and consignment

Part I §3 boxed feedstock as the trade's word for the aircraft and engines bought, or taken on consignment, to be torn down for parts. Those are the two commercial routes, and they allocate capital and risk differently. <span class="cite">[D3 §2.2; Part I §3]</span>

Under purchase the teardown company buys the asset outright and owns the parts. It bears the capital cost and the inventory risk and keeps the margin. Independent traders such as AerSale and GA Telesis (GA), and the trading arm of AAR (AAR Corp., a parts distributor and maintenance company), do this. Lessors such as Willis Lease and AerCap feed their own retired lease assets into their own material subsidiaries, which is purchase by another name. <span class="cite">[D3 §2.2]</span>

<div class="defn"><b>Consignment</b><p>Consignment is teardown and sale of parts on the owner's behalf for a fee or a commission, with title to the parts retained by the owner, who may be an airline, a lessor, a bank or an insurer. It is the alternative to buying feedstock outright and bearing the inventory risk. Willis Aero describes its business as "the acquisition or consignment of aircraft and engines"; the APOC Aviation (APOC) teardown of a 737-800 with Willis Lease offered "packages of parts available for sale and consignment". <span class="cite">[Willis Lease Finance, 10-K FY2025; Asian Aviation, 2025; D3 §2.2]</span></p></div>

### 32.2 Where the value sits

The engines dominate the part-out value of a mature narrowbody. The engine figures in §32.4 put two run-out engines' used-material yield at $3.2–4.4M; the single largest airframe item, the landing gear, fetches "over $1 million" as a fully overhauled set. <span class="cite">[Safe Fly Aviation, consultancy blog, 2026; ISTAT Jetrader, Autumn 2020]</span> A sourced percentage split between engines and airframe was not captured by the research and is recorded as unknown U-D3-01. <span class="cite">[D3 U1]</span>

Within the airframe the value sits in the rotables.

<div class="defn"><b>Rotable</b><p>A rotable is a part that is repaired and reused rather than consumed: landing gear, the auxiliary power unit, thrust reversers, avionics boxes, flight-control actuators, wheels and brakes. Rotables are the airframe items a teardown sells; consumables and expendables are not worth the paperwork. <span class="cite">[D3 §2.2]</span></p></div>

<div class="defn"><b>Auxiliary power unit (APU)</b><p>The auxiliary power unit is the small gas turbine in the aircraft's tail that supplies electrical power and compressed air on the ground and in some flight phases. It is one of the highest-value airframe rotables in a part-out and one of the components for which lessors collect maintenance reserves (Part II §13). Its value in a 737-800 part-out was not captured by the research (U-D3-10). <span class="cite">[D3 §2.2, U10; Part II §13]</span></p></div>

Within an engine the value sits in five places. First, life-limited parts with remaining cycles: fan, booster and compressor disks, shafts and spools, because the next shop visit must replace any that lack life for the following interval and a used disk with life substitutes directly for a new one at list price. Second, the high-pressure turbine (HPT) blades and nozzles and the high-pressure compressor (HPC) airfoils, the most expensive repairable parts; Part I §3 and §7 set out why. Third, fan blades. Fourth, cases and structural parts. Fifth, accessories, of which the fuel-metering unit is the example the trade names.

<div class="defn"><b>Hydro-mechanical unit (HMU)</b><p>The hydro-mechanical unit is the engine's fuel-metering unit, the accessory that sets fuel flow under the engine control's command. It is a high-value engine accessory and, with CFM56 fan disks and structural engine parts, one of the most searched-for teardown items in one 2026 consultancy report. <span class="cite">[Safe Fly Aviation, teardown report, 2026 (consultancy blog); D3 §2.2]</span></p></div>

### 32.3 Time to monetise, and teardown as the end of a lease

A 737-800 teardown takes about three months. During that time every part is assessed by technical teams before being sent to specialist repair shops for overhaul, testing and re-certification. Landing gear are among the first serviceable parts to become available. The auxiliary power unit is sold, and parts are offered as packages for sale and consignment. <span class="cite">[Asian Aviation and AirlinerGS on the APOC–Willis 737-800 teardown, 2025]</span>

Sale proceeds therefore arrive in layers, and the order matters for a working-capital business. Whole modules and life-limited parts with life can be sold to an engine shop or an exchange pool almost at once, because those buyers are ready-made (§36). Repaired rotables arrive after repair-shop turnaround. The long tail of slow-moving airframe parts sells over years. A sourced figure for the total sell-through period, the months to recover most of the proceeds, was not captured and is unknown U-D3-02; AerSale's inventory-turnover disclosure or a trade-press case study would answer it. <span class="cite">[D3 §2.2, U2]</span>

The sequence by which an engine reaches a teardown is visible in one lessor's transactions. APOC leased a CFM56-5A to Condor for twelve months on a green-time lease, with the engine "expected to return to APOC's portfolio for teardown and part-out next year after the lease concludes". Green time, boxed in Part I §8 and owned by Part II §16, is the usable life left before the next mandatory shop visit; a green-time lease rents that life to an operator who will not restore the engine. <span class="cite">[AviTrader, 28 Jul 2025; Aviation Business News, 2025; Part II §16]</span> The same lessor acquired its first CFM56-5B for "up-cycling", its own term for buying an engine to tear down for material, in October 2021; has disassembled two CFM56-7Bs; bought four "young" 737 airframes from a large US legacy carrier for teardown; and in May 2026 bought a 15-year-old A320-200 from FTAI for disassembly at Tarmac Aerosave's Toulouse-Francazal facility. <span class="cite">[AviTrader, 7 Oct 2021; Aviation Business News, undated; ePlaneAI, 2026]</span> That last item is a direct data point on FTAI as a seller of feedstock as well as a buyer (§35).

### 32.4 The engine arithmetic the trade press publishes

One published source gives figures for a CFM56 teardown, and it is a consultancy blog, so it is labelled as such here and wherever it appears in this primer. <span class="cite">[reconciliation E.1 rule 3]</span> Safe Fly Aviation's 2026 report says an exhausted engine bought for $0.8–1.2M can yield $1.6–2.2M of used material, a net $0.4–0.8M per engine. <span class="cite">[Safe Fly Aviation, "CFM56 Engine Market Report 2026" (consultancy blog)]</span>

<div class="defn"><b>Run-out (exhausted) engine</b><p>A run-out or exhausted engine is one with no usable time left before a shop visit: a life-limited part is at its limit, or the temperature margin is gone, or a hard inspection is due. It is the only kind of engine that becomes teardown feedstock, because an engine with life is worth several times its teardown yield as a whole (§32.5). Part II §16 carries what happens when green time runs out. <span class="cite">[D3 §2.2–2.3; Part II §16]</span></p></div>

### 32.5 Why only run-out engines become feedstock, and the owner's choice

The teardown yield has to be read against what the same engine is worth whole, and here the research holds a conflict it does not resolve. The appraiser IBA gives half-life market values for the second half of 2025 of $5.7M for a CFM56-7B24 and $6.4M for a -7B27, describes the -7B market as having "a distinct lack of availability" with values and lease rates above long-term trend, and in its first-half 2026 update says -5B and -7B values "have levelled off after approximately 18 months of increases". <span class="cite">[IBA, Engine Values 2025B, Sept 2025; IBA, H1 2026 update]</span> The consultancy blog gives a -7B "green-time value (new condition)" of $2.8–3.4M, a label the research notes is internally inconsistent since a green-time engine is by definition not in new condition, and a teardown value of $0.8–1.2M; a trade-press item has CFM56 values up "as much as 50% over the past two years". <span class="cite">[Safe Fly Aviation, 2026 (consultancy blog); ePlaneAI, date not captured; reconciliation R-029]</span> The figures sit on different bases, half-life against green-time against run-out, and they disagree on direction. Part I Exhibit 1.11 carries them side by side and this Part does the same.

<div class="box"><div class="h">Why only run-out engines become feedstock</div><p>An engine near half-life, with a fresh restoration and life-limited parts around half their limits, is worth $5.7–6.4M on the appraiser's figures. Torn down, the same engine would yield $1.6–2.2M on the consultancy figures. No rational owner tears down a $6M engine for $2M of parts, so engines with life are sold or leased whole, and only engines at or near run-out reach the teardown market. The corollary runs the other way too: when half-life engines are scarce and expensive, as the appraiser describes for 2024–2026, owners of part-worn engines lease them for their remaining months rather than tear them down, and the feedstock pool shrinks further (§33). <span class="cite">[IBA, Sept 2025 and H1 2026; Safe Fly Aviation, 2026 (consultancy blog); D3 §2.8 step 2, §5]</span></p></div>

That choice, between renting out a part-worn engine for the months it has left and tearing it down now, has no name in the sources. This primer gives it one, because §36 needs it.

<div class="defn"><b>Half-life trade</b><p>The half-life trade is this primer's term, not the sources', for the trade in engines, modules and life-limited parts that are part-way through their lives and priced on the life they have left: a part-worn engine leased for its remaining months or torn down now; a module valued on the remaining cycles of the life-limited parts inside it; a used disk priced at its life fraction times list price less a discount (§34). The term collects what the dossier describes in three places without naming it. <span class="cite">[D3 §2.3, §2.4–2.5, §2.7; registry section 1c]</span></p></div>

## 33. Supply: retirements, feedstock and the 2023–2026 shortage

Used material comes from aircraft and engines that leave service. Its supply is therefore a function of the retirement rate, lagged by the three months of teardown and the repair and certification period that follow. Part III §19 owns the retirement decision and the realised counts; this section carries what they mean for feedstock, and it presents every contested figure with both sources, as the reconciliation requires. <span class="cite">[D3 §2.6; reconciliation E.1 rules 13 and 14]</span>

### 33.1 The retirement decision, as it bears on feedstock

The decision to retire a CFM56-powered aircraft is an economic one, and the trade press states a rule of thumb for it. A CFM56 needs a shop visit roughly every eight years, so the 24-year mark becomes a de facto retirement date because a third full overhaul is "deemed too expensive". Deliveries of the 737NG began in 1997 and ran at about 230 a year in 1998–2002, with A320ceo deliveries at a similar rate, so from 2022 the aircraft and engines of that period began entering their prime retirement window. <span class="cite">[Aviation Week, "Retirement Uptick Will Restock Used Engine Parts", date not captured]</span> That rule sits beside a different reading of the interval in the research: a mature CFM56 has a performance restoration at 10,000–15,000 cycles, which at 1,000–1,500 cycles a year is roughly seven to fifteen years, and a full overhaul with life-limited-part replacement at 20,000–25,000 cycles. The two are carried together and Part I §5 and §6 set out why they differ. <span class="cite">[Leeham News, 8 Mar 2024; Aircraft Value News, undated; reconciliation R-008]</span>

The rule of thumb has a feedstock consequence. If the third overhaul is the retirement trigger, then the engines reaching teardown are the ones whose life-limited parts are at or near their limits for the second time, and whose yield is therefore mostly airfoils, cases and accessories rather than life. Engines retired early, with life-limited parts part-way through a run, are the ones whose teardown carries life worth selling (§32.6 step 3). Which kind retires depends on the owner's alternatives, which is where the 2023–2026 shortage begins.

Part III §19 lists five inputs to the owner's decision: age and the maintenance cliff, lease economics, freighter conversion, part-out value, and the availability of a replacement. The fourth is this Part's subject. When an aircraft's value as a sum of parts exceeds its value flying, the owner parts it out; rising used-material prices raise part-out value and so, at the margin, encourage retirement. That is a stabilising feedback. The research records that it was not strong enough to lift retirements in 2024–2026, because the fifth input overrode it: none of the others triggers a retirement if the airline cannot get a replacement aircraft. <span class="cite">[D9 §2.3, the demand-context dossier (D9); Part III §19]</span>

### 33.4 The 2023–2026 shortage: what the sources describe

The mechanism, as the sources describe it, has six steps. Airlines kept CFM56-powered aircraft flying longer because new narrowbody deliveries slipped and new-generation engines spent time off wing. The expected post-2022 retirement wave therefore did not arrive. Retirements stayed well below the 2010–2019 norm. The engines that would have been feedstock were instead leased as green-time engines or overhauled. Teardown companies competed for a smaller pool. And the price of everything in the pool rose. <span class="cite">[D3 §2.6; D9 §2.5–2.8]</span>

Part III owns the first step and this Part takes its headline figures from it. Cirium data cited in trade press put 835 aircraft powered by Pratt & Whitney's geared turbofan (GTF) grounded or stored in late 2025. CFM's LEAP (the CFM56's successor) was removed early at 2,000–6,000 cycles against a mature CFM56's 10,000–15,000-cycle interval, and its 737 durability kit was certified only in July 2026. Boeing's 737 MAX (the 737NG's successor) was capped by the FAA at 38 a month after January 2024, raised to 42 in October 2025 and reviewed for 47 in May 2026. Airbus delivered 793 aircraft in 2025 against an 820 target. And 75–90% of scheduled lease expiries were being extended, with only about a dozen mid-life 737NGs and fewer than 25 A320ceos available to rent worldwide at the time of that survey. <span class="cite">[The Flying Engineer citing Cirium, 2026; Leeham News, 8 Mar 2024; CFM release, 18 Jul 2026; TravelPulse, Oct 2025; CAPA, May 2026; Air Data News, 2026; Bloomberg via AJOT (American Journal of Transportation), date not visible; Part III §20–22]</span>

The consequences in the figures the research captured are these. CFM56 values rose "as much as 50%" in two years on one trade-press account. <span class="cite">[ePlaneAI, date not captured]</span> CFM56-7B green-time rents in 2026 stood about 34% above 2019 on the consultancy blog, and the appraiser's spare-engine rent rose from about $75,000 to about $100,000 a month from 2019 to 2024, also about a third; the levels differ and the percentage change agrees, which the reconciliation records as one of the few independent corroborations in this area. <span class="cite">[Safe Fly Aviation, 2026 (consultancy blog); IBA, Apr 2024; reconciliation B-5, R-028]</span> IBA called it "a lessors' market" in April 2024 as engine lease rates and market values escalated. <span class="cite">[IBA via AviTrader, 25 Apr 2024]</span> Aviation Week headlined "a seller's market for used parts". <span class="cite">[Aviation Week, "MRO Memo: A Seller's Market For Used Parts", date not captured]</span> AerSale's management described "a hypercompetitive feedstock environment, marked by increased scrutiny and pressure on pricing as more companies enter the teardown sector", and AerSale's whole-asset sales fell from 20 engines and one aircraft in 2024 to 13 engines in 2025. <span class="cite">[AerSale, as relayed in search results; AerSale release, 5 Mar 2026]</span> The three descriptions agree that asset owners had the upper hand and teardown buyers did not; they differ only in vantage point, and the dossier recorded that as its sixth tension rather than a contradiction. <span class="cite">[D3 tension 6]</span>

Whether the parts constraint was easing by 2026 is contested, and both sides are carried. GE's FY2025 annual report says material input from priority suppliers rose more than 40% and turnaround improved more than 10% in the fourth quarter of 2025. Oliver Wyman's April 2026 survey has two-thirds of maintenance operators unable to find enough technicians and labour-rate inflation at 5.5–6.0%, and a 2025 trade-press report says used-material supply "remains thin". <span class="cite">[GE Aerospace 10-K FY2025; Oliver Wyman, Apr 2026; AVM (Aviation Maintenance magazine), 2025; reconciliation R-020]</span>

<div class="box"><div class="h">The same engine on both sides of the aftermarket</div><p>Every CFM56 that is not retired keeps flying, keeps accumulating cycles, and will need a shop visit (Part IV). Every CFM56 that is not retired is also one that is not torn down, so its life-limited parts, airfoils and modules do not reach the used-material market. The driver that raises demand for used parts therefore lowers their supply at the same moment, with the same engine. Part I §2.3 made the point from the fleet side and Part III §24 turns it into translation-table rows; this Part is where the supply side of it is counted. <span class="cite">[D9 §5; Part I §2.3; D3 §2.6]</span></p></div>

### 33.5 The forecast that pointed the other way

In July 2020 Oliver Wyman published a piece titled "A tsunami of used aircraft parts", forecasting a glut of used material from pandemic retirements. The research captured only the title, so none of its figures is reported, and whether Oliver Wyman has since updated the forecast is unknown U-D3-19. <span class="cite">[Oliver Wyman, Jul 2020, title only; D3 U19]</span> The outturn pointed the other way on every count the research reached: Cirium's counts fell every year from 502 in 2019 to 325 in 2022; McKinsey reports 2024–2026 retirements about 24% below the 2010–2019 norm with a return to about 2.7% of the fleet a year only from 2028; GE's realised CFM56 rate was about 1.5% in 2025 against a plan that had started at 3–4%; and AerSale, IBA and Aviation Week all describe scarcity. <span class="cite">[IATA Maintenance Cost Conference, 2023; McKinsey, date not captured; Leeham News, 22 Jan 2026; D3 tension 2; reconciliation B-6]</span> The forecast and the outturn are both carried; the forecast's own numbers are not, because they were not read.

Two Aviation Week headlines in the research point in different directions within the shortage period: "Used Parts Market Shows Signs Of Stabilizing" and "North America Sees Upturn In USM Market". Their dates and bodies were not captured, so the sequence cannot be fixed; IBA's "levelled off" of the first half of 2026 and the consultancy blog's rents "up 15–20% in recent months" sit in the same unresolved tension. <span class="cite">[Aviation Week, two titles, dates not captured; IBA, H1 2026; Safe Fly Aviation, 2026; D3 tension 7]</span>

### 33.6 What GE's used-material programmes mean for independents' feedstock

The manufacturer is also a used-material trader, and by its own statement the largest. GE Aerospace said in 2026 that "GE and CFM are the largest used serviceable material provider for their products". <span class="cite">[GE Aerospace, 2026, via Aviation Week and GE investor materials; D2 §2.2, the shop-visit market dossier (D2)]</span> Part IV §28 owns the programmes; what belongs here is where they touch feedstock.

Two of GE's four branded TrueChoice offerings carry used material. TrueChoice Material sells "high-quality new and used OEM parts, advanced repairs and technology upgrades" to airlines and to third-party shops; Ryanair's long-term materials agreement covering "their entire fleet of ~2,000 CFM56 and LEAP engines" is a contract of that kind. TrueChoice Transitions offers "green time leases, exchanges and material buy-back, plus custom workscopes with shorter builds and maximum used material" to operators changing their ownership horizon. <span class="cite">[GE Aerospace press releases, 2019–2025; GE Aerospace 10-K FY2025; D2 §2.3]</span>

For an independent teardown company the programmes mean three things, each a description rather than a judgment. First, the manufacturer competes for retired engines: material buy-back under Transitions takes engines at the end of an operator's horizon into the OEM's own pool before a trader can bid. Second, the manufacturer sells the material those engines yield through Material, into the same shops the independents supply, and it can bundle it with new parts and repairs, which an independent cannot. Third, a used part sold by the manufacturer carries the manufacturer's release, which matters to a buyer who vets suppliers as §31.2 described. A GA Telesis purchase of $25M of "OEM-aligned" used engine inventory in October 2025 shows OEM-sanctioned used-material channels running beside independent ones. <span class="cite">[D2 §2.3; GA Telesis release, Oct 2025; D3 §3]</span> The name and scale of GE's used-material arm, and its CFM56 role, were not captured; the only hit was a TrueEngine presentation whose content was not read, and the gap is clustered with Part VI's open question on GE's positioning against alternative parts (K-16). <span class="cite">[D3 U15; reconciliation K-16]</span>

## 34. Pricing versus new OEM parts

A used part has no price list of its own. It is priced as a percentage of the manufacturer's catalogue list price for the same part number, and that one fact explains most of what follows: the used price inherits the manufacturer's annual escalation, the discount narrows when the manufacturer cannot deliver, and it widens when retirements flood the market. <span class="cite">[D3 §2.5]</span>

<div class="defn"><b>USM pricing versus new (the USM discount to CLP)</b><p>Used serviceable material is priced as a percentage of the OEM's catalogue list price (CLP) for the same part: on average 60–80% of a new part's price; 70–75% of list when a specialist engine trader sells repaired material to an airline; up to 85% of list for high-demand parts early in a platform's life; and above list when the manufacturer cannot deliver. Because the base is the catalogue, the used price rises with every catalogue revision even if the discount does not move. <span class="cite">[Aviation Week, "MRO Memo: A Seller's Market For Used Parts", date not captured; Aircraft Commerce Issue 120, date not captured; D3 §2.5]</span></p></div>

### 34.2 Life-limited parts: life fraction first, then discount

For most used parts the percentage is applied to the list price directly. For a life-limited part there is an extra step, because what the buyer is purchasing is the remaining cycles. The life-proportional value is the fraction of life remaining times the list price of the new part; the used price is that figure less the trader's discount. Part I §4.4 stated the rule from the engine side; here it is from the trader's. <span class="cite">[D3 §2.4–2.5; Part I §4.4]</span>

The one current list price the research reached for a CFM56-7B life-limited part is a consultancy figure: a high-pressure turbine disk at $420,000 new, with its 20,000-cycle limit. The current list price of representative -7B life-limited parts and of a full set was otherwise not captured and is unknown U-D3-06. <span class="cite">[Safe Fly Aviation, 2026 (consultancy blog), via D2 §4.4; D3 U6]</span>

<div class="eqblock">disk with 70% of life left:  life-proportional value = 0.70 × 420,000 = $294,000
at the trader's 70–75% of list:  294,000 × 0.70 to 0.75 = $206,000–221,000, or 49–53% of the new price
buyer facing a one-year OEM lead time: may pay 0.70 × 420,000 = $294,000 or more, the "above list" case in life-adjusted terms</div>

That last line is how the trade-press statement that used parts can sell "for more than the catalog list price" should be read for a life-limited part. The buyer is not paying more than list for a new disk. It is paying the full life-proportional value, or above it, for a used disk it can have now, because the alternative is a grounded engine for the manufacturer's lead time. The mechanism matters more than the specific disk: the discount to list narrows when the manufacturer's own supply is constrained, and widens when retirements flood the market with used parts. <span class="cite">[Aviation Week, date not captured; D3 §2.5]</span>

### 34.3 How a used part inherits the manufacturer's escalation

Part I §4.3 boxed escalation and Part IV §28 owns it. Two readings sit in the research and both are carried. The first covers all spare parts: new spare-parts prices rising "around 10% per year", a double-digit increase on 1 November 2022, a high-single-digit increase in August 2023, and Safran's chief executive describing a rule of pricing "above inflation by 3 to 4 points ... at least for some years". <span class="cite">[Aviation Week, "Parts Price Hikes Help Boost Safran, GE Aftermarket Sales", late 2023]</span> The second is specific to life-limited parts: a full -7B set compounded at about 7% a year from 2008 to 2025 on the published set prices, a single step of about 12% reported by Ishka without a year, and -7B parts generally up 20–30% in 2023 after CFM's August 2023 catalogue revision. <span class="cite">[Part I worked example 1.3; Ishka, undated; Magnetic Group via Aviation Week, 2024; reconciliation R-018]</span> The years and amounts of each catalogue step between 2019 and 2026 were not retrieved (cluster K-03).

Because the used price is a percentage of the catalogue, every one of those steps moves it. The worked example shows the arithmetic over three years on both escalation readings and on both discount readings.

<p>Three things follow. The used price in dollars rises every year even though the discount is unchanged, by exactly the manufacturer's escalation rate: a trader holding a disk in stock sees its selling price rise with the catalogue. The dollar saving from buying used also grows with escalation, because it is a fixed share of a rising list. And a 20–30% single-year step of the kind reported for -7B parts in 2023 lifts the used price by the same 20–30% in the year it lands. The one thing escalation does not do is close the gap between used and new; only the manufacturer's delivery constraint does that, by narrowing the discount toward 85% of list and above. Limits of the example: the disk price is a consultancy figure; the 7% rate is derived from set prices whose scope differs; the catalogue steps for 2024–2026 are unknown; and the used readings describe different transaction types (R-022). <span class="cite">[Safe Fly Aviation, 2026 (consultancy blog); Aviation Week, late 2023; Aircraft Commerce Issue 120; Part I worked example 1.3; reconciliation R-018, R-022; K-03]</span></p></div>

<div class="box"><div class="h">How the catalogue sets the used price</div><p>The manufacturer never prices a used part, and it sets the price of every used part. It does so because the trade quotes used material as a share of list, and the manufacturer alone sets list and revises it upward on a schedule. A used-material trader therefore carries two exposures it does not control: the manufacturer's escalation, which lifts the value of its stock, and the manufacturer's delivery performance, which sets how close to list it can sell. A third party to the same line in the parts bill is the alternative-manufacturer part of Part VI, which is priced against the same catalogue; the two compete for the same slot, and an engine with alternative parts carries less of the list-price-based maintenance adjustment that appraisers apply, which Aircraft Value News records as undermining full-life adjustments (Part I §8). <span class="cite">[D3 §2.5, §5; D4 §2.1; Aircraft Value News, undated]</span></p></div>
