## 45. What the segment sells, in FTAI's words

FTAI Aviation reports two segments. One owns and leases aircraft and engines (Part VIII §58). The other, Aerospace Products, repairs and sells engines, modules and parts. Part I §11 gave the segment a one-line definition for orientation. This section gives the company's own description, says what the company means by the words in it, and lists the products.

<div class="defn"><b>Form 10-K, Form 10-Q and Form 8-K</b><p>The 10-K is the annual report a US-listed company files with the Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC); the 10-Q is the quarterly report; the 8-K is the current report used for press releases and material events. FTAI's earnings releases are filed as exhibits to 8-Ks and also published on GlobeNewswire. Part X §79 carries the full treatment. <span class="cite">[D5 §8; D10 §3]</span></p></div>

### 45.1 The filing language

The annual report for 2025 describes the segment in one sentence. Aerospace Products "develops and manufactures, repairs/refurbishes, and sells aircraft engines and aftermarket components primarily for the CFM56-7B, CFM56-5B, and V2500 commercial aircraft engines. Engine, module, and parts sales are facilitated through a dedicated commercial maintenance program designed to focus on modular and parts repair and refurbishment of CFM56-7B and CFM56-5B engines". <span class="cite">[FTAI 10-K FY2025]</span> The programme named in that sentence is the Module Factory, which the same filing calls "a dedicated commercial maintenance program designed to focus on modular and parts repair and refurbishment" and says the company "targets assets which require maintenance repairs that can be performed through [its] proprietary Module Factory process". <span class="cite">[FTAI 10-K FY2025]</span>

Three words in the first sentence carry more weight than they appear to. The first is "manufactures". FTAI's filings describe the business as one that "focuses on the manufacturing and sale of new assets through the use of inventory purchased from third parties and salvaged modules and parts from generally unserviceable engines". <span class="cite">[FTAI 10-K FY2025, via D3 §2.7]</span> A module that FTAI has rebuilt from salvaged and purchased parts is, in the filing's framing, a manufactured product, and its sale is a product sale. The second word is "sells". The segment does not describe itself as performing maintenance for a fee on a customer's engine. It describes itself as selling engines, modules and parts. The third is "modular". The unit of the business is the module of Part I §3, not the engine and not the piece part.

The same filing explains where the material comes from. Engines in FTAI's own leasing fleet are "transferred to inventory when determined to be unserviceable for leasing purposes with the intent to tear down the asset into salvageable components that would be manufactured into new modules and parts and sold". <span class="cite">[FTAI 10-K FY2025, via D3 §2.7]</span> Material that FTAI does not build into modules has its own outlet: "serviceable used modules and parts are sold through an exclusive partnership responsible for the teardown, repair, marketing and sales of parts from the CFM56 engine pool". <span class="cite">[FTAI 10-K FY2025, via D3 §2.7]</span> The filing excerpt the research reached does not name the partner. A PR Newswire release located by title, and not read, says "AAR and FTAI Aviation extend their exclusive Serviceable Engine Products agreement, providing CFM56 engine material to the global aviation aftermarket through 2030". <span class="cite">[PR Newswire, 2025, title only; reconciliation B-9]</span>

The 10-K adds that the company "develops and manufactures PMA parts through a joint venture", PMA being the FAA's Parts Manufacturer Approval for a replacement part made by someone other than the engine's manufacturer (Part VI §37). <span class="cite">[FTAI 10-K FY2025; D4 §4.3]</span> Section 52 takes that up.

### 45.2 What MRE means, and how it differs from MRO

The industry's generic term for maintenance work is MRO, Maintenance, Repair and Overhaul (Part I §6; Part IV §25). FTAI's name for its own offer replaces the last word.

<div class="defn"><b>Maintenance, Repair and Exchange (MRE)</b><p>MRE is FTAI's name for its offer to engine owners, chosen as a contrast with the industry's MRO. Instead of restoring a customer's engine part by part while the customer waits, FTAI removes the module or engine that triggered the removal, installs a serviceable one from its inventory, tests and returns the engine, and takes the unserviceable unit as feedstock for its own production. The offer is sold as engine, module and parts sales to airlines, lessors and other shops, and to the Strategic Capital vehicles under MRE Contracts that carry stand-ready obligations (§48). The FY2025 10-K uses the phrase when it describes the Rome joint venture as created "to meet increasing demand for MRE services". <span class="cite">[FTAI 10-K FY2025; D5 §2.2; D7 §2.4; D10 §2.4; registry C-15]</span></p></div>

The distinction is operational, not regulatory. Both an MRO shop and FTAI hold repair-station approvals and work to the manufacturer's manual (Part I §9). The difference is in who waits and for what. In a conventional shop visit the engine's owner waits for its own engine while the shop disassembles, inspects, repairs and rebuilds it; the owner pays for parts at list and labour by the hour, or under a flight-hour agreement (Part IV §28). In an exchange the owner receives a different, already serviceable module or engine, and the wait is the time to swap and test. The owner gives up the unserviceable unit it handed over. Section 46 sets out the sequence step by step.

Two cautions about the vocabulary travel through the rest of this Part. First, the press summaries of the January 2025 short report use "MRO revenue" to describe what FTAI books, while FTAI's own filings say "engine, module, and parts sales" and "MRE Contract revenue"; the two vocabularies name the same transactions and the dispute in §54 is about how they should be classified, not about what they are called. Second, the word "core" has two meanings in this Part: the hot middle module of the engine (Part I §1) and the unserviceable unit a customer hands back in an exchange. Section 47 states the double meaning where it first bears on a price.

## 46. The Module Factory: how an exchange works for a customer

Part I §3.4 boxed the module exchange and the Module Factory in short form. This section is the full treatment: the sequence an exchange follows, what happens to the unit the customer hands back, what makes the flow a factory rather than a shop, and how it differs from the conventional visit of Part IV and from the engine manufacturer's own exchange offer.

### 46.1 The filing's own words, then the function

The Module Factory is a place, a programme and a process, and FTAI's filings use the name in all three senses. <span class="cite">[registry C-16]</span> The language first used in 2022, when the concept was launched with a third-party operator, and still returned by search against the FY2025 10-K, is: "The Module Factory is a dedicated commercial engine maintenance center focused on modular repair and refurbishment of CFM56-7B and CFM56-5B engines, based at Lockheed Martin Commercial Engine Solutions' 500,000 square foot facility in Montreal, Canada with a capacity for up to 300 shop visits per year". <span class="cite">[FTAI 10-K FY2025; FTAI 10-K FY2022]</span> That is the place. The programme is the "dedicated commercial maintenance program" of §45.1. The process is what the rest of this section describes: a production line that builds and holds serviceable modules so that an engine's maintenance becomes a swap from inventory rather than a repair to order. FTAI bought the Montréal facility outright in September 2024 (§50.1).

A conventional performance restoration, as Part I §6 and Part IV describe it, follows one sequence. The engine comes off the aircraft when its temperature margin or a life limit runs out. It waits for an induction slot. The shop disassembles it to the module or piece-part level, inspects every part, repairs or replaces what is out of limits, replaces the life-limited parts that would fall due in the next run, rebuilds the engine and runs it in a test cell. The owner waits for its own engine through all of this. Turnaround for a full CFM56 overhaul was 90 to 120 days in 2025 against about 60 before the pandemic, GE's shops report "sustaining ~90 days", a consultancy blog gives 75 to 90 days for a restoration and 120 to 150 for a heavy visit with life-limited parts, and the wait for a slot before any of that begins had grown by two to three months and in some cases six. <span class="cite">[Aviation Business News, 2025; GE Aerospace, 27 May 2026; Safe Fly Aviation, consultancy blog, 2026; Bain, 2024; reconciliation R-019]</span>

The Module Factory inverts the order of the work. FTAI does the disassembly, inspection, repair and rebuilding before any customer engine arrives, on its own schedule, and holds the finished modules in inventory. When a customer engine arrives, the module that triggered the removal is taken off and a serviceable module from inventory is bolted on. The engine is tested and returned. The removed module then joins FTAI's production flow and becomes, in time, a serviceable module for a later customer. <span class="cite">[D5 §2.2]</span> The company's own description of the consequence is that "modules produced" is the quantity it reports each quarter, the way a factory reports units (§49). <span class="cite">[FTAI Q2 2025 release, 29 Jul 2025; FTAI Q4 2025 call via Motley Fool, 26 Feb 2026]</span>

Step 4 is where Part I's conditions for a module to move bear directly on the business. A module from inventory can go onto a customer's engine only if the two are at compatible build standards, if the module's records are complete for every life-limited part inside it, and if the part numbers match the receiving engine (Part I §3.3). The -5B and -7B share enough core hardware that used-parts dealers advertise one "CFM56-5B & -7B Core LLP Package", which is what lets a pool built for one variant draw on the other. <span class="cite">[Aviation Fleet Support, undated listing, via Part I §3.3; D1 U2]</span> How FTAI manages configuration across a pool of hundreds of modules, and how it matches the remaining life in an inventory module to the run a customer plans, were not retrieved. The industry's pricing convention, in which the fee is adjusted for the difference in life-limited-part life between the module handed over and the module received, implies that a mismatch is settled in price rather than avoided (§47). <span class="cite">[D2 §2.5; D3 §2.7]</span>

Step 5 produces one of the few numerical cross-checks the research allows. That is an observation about the company's own figures, not a disclosed rule; whether every exchange is tested was not verified. <span class="cite">[D1 U14]</span>

### 46.3 What happens to the unit the customer hands back

The second meaning of "core" enters at step 7. In the trade an exchange is priced as a fee plus the core, the unserviceable module the customer hands over, which has value to the exchanger as feedstock. <span class="cite">[D5 §2.3; registry C-6]</span> Part I §3.3 glossed feedstock in its narrow sense, the aircraft and engines bought to be torn down, and said later Parts would extend it. This Part extends it to the returned core and to engine inventory held for module production, which is how FTAI's filings use the material. <span class="cite">[registry C-14]</span>

A returned module is an engine section in miniature, and FTAI treats it the way Part V §32 describes a teardown. Its repairable parts are repaired, in FTAI's own shops, at specialist repair vendors (Part IV §29), from 2026 at the Bristol accessories joint venture (§50.6), and under the component-repair element of the January 2026 agreement with CFM (§52). Its expired life-limited parts are replaced, either with new parts from CFM at catalogue price or with used packages carrying the cycles a buyer needs (Part I §4; Part V §34). Its parts with remaining life are retained as used serviceable material for FTAI's own builds or sold through the exclusive serviceable-parts partnership of §45.1. The rebuilt module returns to inventory. <span class="cite">[FTAI 10-K FY2025, via D3 §2.7; D5 §2.2]</span> Material comes into this flow from four directions at once: customer cores from exchanges, engines retired from FTAI's own leasing fleet, engines on the aircraft the Strategic Capital vehicles own, and engines or aircraft bought for the purpose, such as the ten off-lease 737-700s acquired from WestJet in September 2026 "to expand the Company's supply of CFM56-7B engines and modules available to its Maintenance, Repair and Exchange customers". <span class="cite">[FTAI release, 28 Sep 2026, via D6 §4.7; D5 §5]</span>

<div class="box"><div class="h">Why the exchange turns maintenance into production</div><p>Three things change when the module, not the engine, is the unit of work, and FTAI does the work before the customer arrives rather than after. First, the customer's downtime becomes the swap-and-test interval, not the repair interval, because the repair was done earlier on another unit. Second, FTAI's own work becomes repetitive and schedulable: the same two engine variants, split at the same flanges, rebuilt to a stock of standard configurations, so the shop can be loaded like a production line and its output counted in units. Third, every exchange feeds the next one, because the unserviceable module that comes back is the raw material for a future serviceable module. The cost of this arrangement is the inventory: FTAI must own a stock of serviceable modules large enough to meet exchanges as they arise, and a stock of unserviceable ones in work. Section 53 gives the inventory figure. None of this is unique to FTAI in principle; StandardAero lists "engine module changes" among its quick-turn services and GE sells exchanges under TrueChoice Transitions (§46.5). What FTAI's filings describe as distinctive is the scale and the organisation of the work as a factory. <span class="cite">[D5 §2.2; StandardAero brochure, Oct 2022; D2 §2.3]</span></p></div>

### 46.4 Downtime, and what is known about it

The whole commercial case for an exchange rests on the time saved, and the research could not source FTAI's own turnaround. <span class="cite">[D5 U3]</span> What it could source is the market's figures for the two alternatives. A hospital or quick-turn visit, which Part I §6.1 defined as a visit that fixes one finding and returns the engine, runs about 45 days in 2025 trade reporting; a module exchange is one of the things such a visit does. <span class="cite">[Aviation Business News, 2025; StandardAero brochure, Oct 2022]</span> A performance restoration runs 75 to 90 days on a consultancy blog's figures and a full overhaul 90 to 120 days in trade reporting, with GE at about 90; the slot wait of two to six months comes before either. <span class="cite">[Safe Fly Aviation, 2026; Aviation Business News, 2025; GE Aerospace, 27 May 2026; Bain, 2024]</span> FTAI's Miami business is branded "QuickTurn" and the Rome joint venture "QuickTurn Europe", which names the intent without quantifying it; the lower-case workscope and the capitalised brand are kept distinct in this primer. <span class="cite">[FTAI release, 5 Jun 2025; registry C-20]</span> Section 55 works the arithmetic of the downtime saving with every input labelled.

## 47. Exchanges, module sales and engine sales: pricing and revenue recognition

The segment books three kinds of transaction, and the boundary between them is where the dispute in §54 sits. This section defines the three, sets out how the industry prices an exchange and how US accounting rules book one, records what FTAI has and has not disclosed about its own fees, and states the definitional question the short report raised without resolving it.

### 47.1 The three transactions

<div class="defn"><b>Engine sale, outright module sale and exchange</b><p>The segment's three product transactions. An engine sale books the whole-engine price as revenue. An outright module sale books a module's cash price as revenue with no unit coming back. An exchange books an exchange fee plus the fair value of the unserviceable unit received. In all three, revenue is recognised at the point in time when control of the delivered engine or module passes to the customer, and cost of sales is the carrying value of what was delivered. The January 2025 short report's central allegation concerns the boundary between the first and the other two (§54). <span class="cite">[D5 §2.3]</span></p></div>

<div class="defn"><b>Exchange fee and the returned core</b><p>The exchange fee is the cash price a customer pays to receive a serviceable module in return for an unserviceable one. The returned core is the unserviceable module the customer hands over, which has value to the exchanger as feedstock. The industry prices an exchange as the fee plus the value of life-limited-part life transferred, adjusted for the condition of the other hardware. This is the second meaning of "core" in the primer; the first is the engine's hot middle module (Part I §1), and this Part writes "returned core" or "the unit handed back" whenever the exchange sense is meant. FTAI publishes no exchange price list, so the revenue-per-module figures of §56 are upper bounds on the fee, not prices. <span class="cite">[D5 §2.3, §2.10; D2 §2.5, U7; D3 §2.7, U20; registry C-6]</span></p></div>

The price structure of an exchange follows from Part I §4 and §8. A module's value is the sum of its serviceable parts at used-material discounts to list plus the remaining life of the life-limited parts inside it, less the repair needed to make it serviceable (Part V §36). <span class="cite">[D3 §2.7]</span> When a customer hands over a module whose turbine disk has 1,200 cycles left and receives one whose disk has 10,000, the customer has bought 8,800 cycles of disk life and the price reflects it. The fee above that life transfer, which is what FTAI, GE or MTU charge for the module itself, the avoided downtime and the margin, was not found in any source the research reached. <span class="cite">[D2 U7; D3 U20; D5 U3; reconciliation cluster K-01]</span> One consultancy blog quotes "removal costs of $150,000–$400,000 per module" for life-limited modules; the research recorded the phrase as ambiguous and did not use it. <span class="cite">[Safe Fly Aviation, 2026, via D2 §2.5]</span> Part IV §30 brackets the life-transfer element at about $250,000 for a high-pressure-turbine set carrying 10,000 cycles, or about $2.0M if a whole stack's life is priced at $200 a cycle, both derived from contested list prices and with the fee itself unknown. <span class="cite">[D2 §2.5; reconciliation R-016, R-017]</span>

### 47.2 How an exchange is booked

<div class="defn"><b>ASC 606 and noncash consideration</b><p>ASC 606 is the US revenue-recognition standard for contracts with customers. Under it, noncash consideration received from a customer is measured at fair value and included in the transaction price. For an exchange, that means revenue equals the cash fee plus the fair value of the returned core, and cost of sales equals the carrying value of the module delivered. FTAI's filings state that MRE Contract sales to the Strategic Capital vehicles are booked under ASC 606 and that the profit on them is eliminated through equity-method earnings (§48). The exact wording of FTAI's revenue-recognition policy for exchanges, including how it values a returned core and whether it quantifies fees separately, was not retrieved. <span class="cite">[D5 §2.3, U1; D7 §2.5]</span></p></div>

The consequence for reading FTAI's revenue is that an exchange generates more revenue than cash. If a customer pays a fee of F and hands over a core worth C, FTAI books F plus C as revenue and receives F in cash and a module in kind. The module in kind then becomes inventory at C and, after repair, cost of sales on a later exchange. <span class="cite">[D5 §2.3]</span> This is standard accounting, not a peculiarity of FTAI's. It matters here because the segment's revenue per module (§56) includes the fair values of cores received, and because an inventory build (§53) is partly the accumulation of cores.

<div class="worked"><div class="h">Worked example 7.1 — How one exchange books, with every number assumed</div>
<p>Inputs, all assumed for illustration because FTAI publishes none of them (D5 U1, U3; cluster K-01): a customer pays an exchange fee F of $1.0M for a serviceable core module; the unserviceable core it hands over is valued by FTAI at a fair value C of $0.6M; the module FTAI delivers is carried in inventory at $1.1M, being its acquisition cost plus the parts, labour and overhead spent making it serviceable.</p>
<div class="eqblock">revenue = F + C = 1,000,000 + 600,000 = $1,600,000
cost of sales = carrying value delivered = $1,100,000
gross profit = 1,600,000 − 1,100,000 = $500,000, or 31% of revenue
cash received = F = $1,000,000
inventory added = C = $600,000 (the returned core, before repair)</div>
<p>So the transaction shows $1.6M of revenue on $1.0M of cash, and the $0.6M difference is a module FTAI now owns and must spend money repairing before it can be sold again. If the same module were sold outright for $1.6M with nothing coming back, revenue and gross profit would be identical and cash would be $0.6M higher. Limits of the example: every input is assumed; FTAI's valuation method for returned cores is not disclosed; the carrying value of a delivered module depends on how much of its content was used material, PMA or new OEM parts (§52); and the sourced revenue per module of §56 is a blend of exchanges, outright sales, whole engines and parts. <span class="cite">[D5 §2.3, §2.10]</span></p></div>

### 47.3 What is known and unknown about FTAI's fees

Three facts are known. First, the filings describe the outputs as "engine, module, and parts sales" and present them, for all customers other than the Strategic Capital vehicles, on a single income-statement line. <span class="cite">[FTAI 10-K FY2025]</span>

<div class="defn"><b>Aerospace products revenue (income-statement line)</b><p>The consolidated income-statement line for the segment's sales of modules, engines and parts to customers other than the Strategic Capital vehicles: $454,970 thousand in 2023, $1,079,821 thousand in 2024 and $1,600,456 thousand in 2025. It is distinct from MRE Contract revenue (§48), which the filings show on a separate line from 2025, and from segment revenue, which is the sum of the two. No retrieved filing disaggregates it into engines, modules, parts and repairs. <span class="cite">[FTAI 10-K FY2025, segment table via orchestrator notes; D5 §2.3, U2; D10 §2.4]</span></p></div>

Second, the only observable bound on the average fee is the ratio of that line to modules produced: $1,600.5M divided by 757 modules is $2.11M for 2025, and $692.2M divided by 296 is $2.34M for the second quarter of 2026. Those are upper bounds on the average cash fee for a module, because the numerator contains whole-engine sales, parts sales and the fair values of cores received while the denominator counts only CFM56 modules; §56 works the figures and their limits. <span class="cite">[D5 §2.10–2.11; reconciliation R-046]</span> Third, the one cost lever FTAI has quantified is on the production side rather than the price side: the Bristol accessories venture is "expected to deliver up to $75,000 in average savings per shop visit". <span class="cite">[FTAI Q3 2025 call, via Insider Monkey]</span>

What is not known is longer. The exchange fee itself, the method for valuing a returned core, whether fees and core values are quantified separately, the split of the revenue line among engines, modules, parts and repairs, the margin by line, and whether disclosure changed after the February 2025 review are all recorded as open. <span class="cite">[D5 U1, U2, U3; D2 U7; D3 U20]</span> Trade interviews that may contain estimates were located and not read. <span class="cite">[In Practise, "FTAI Aviation: CFM56 repair and module swap", located by D2 and D5, not read]</span>

### 47.4 The definitional question the short report raised

A whole CFM56 is, in FTAI's counting convention, three modules (Part I §3.2). A serviceable engine built from three modules and sold outright is an engine sale. The same three modules installed one at a time into three different customers' engines are three module exchanges. The revenue booked may be similar; the character of the transaction, and what a reader infers about how repeatable it is, differ. The January 2025 report by Muddy Waters Research alleged, in the words of the later securities complaint, that FTAI had "presented whole engine sales as module sales" and had reported "one-time engine sales as MRO revenue"; FTAI "strongly disagrees", and its Audit Committee found the allegations "without merit". <span class="cite">[law-firm case pages via orchestrator notes; Seeking Alpha, Jan 2025; FTAI 8-K exhibit, Feb 2025]</span> This section states only the definitional point: because the filings do not disaggregate the revenue line, a reader cannot tell from the filings alone how much of it is whole engines and how much is modules and parts. <span class="cite">[D5 U2]</span> Section 54 gives each party's position with its source.

## 48. MRE Contract revenue: the Strategic Capital vehicles as customer

From 2025 the segment has a second revenue line whose customer is a partnership that FTAI manages and part-owns. This section defines the line by quoting both filings that describe it, gives every figure the research retrieved, explains how the profit on it is treated, and derives the share of segment revenue it represented by period.

### 48.1 The customer

Part VIII §60 to §62 describe the Strategic Capital Initiative in full. For this section the reader needs four facts. FTAI launched the initiative on 30 December 2024. Its first vehicle, the 2025 Partnership, raises third-party capital into a closed-end partnership whose special-purpose vehicles buy on-lease mid-life 737NG and A320ceo aircraft; it reached a $2.0B equity hard cap in October 2025 and about $6.0B of total capital across more than 300 aircraft by August 2026. FTAI is the Servicer, the party contracted to manage the assets for a fee (Part VIII §61), and holds a limited-partner stake described as 19% in the FY2025 10-K and the 2026 10-Qs and as 20% in the Q3 2025 10-Q. <span class="cite">[FTAI releases, 30 Dec 2024, 27 Oct 2025 and 17 Aug 2026; FTAI 10-K FY2025; FTAI 10-Qs; reconciliation R-048]</span> And each release on the vehicle repeats one sentence about its engines: they "will be powered exclusively via engine and module exchanges with FTAI's Maintenance, Repair and Exchange (MRE) business". <span class="cite">[FTAI releases, 26 Feb 2025, 25 Mar 2025 and 17 Aug 2026, via D7 §2.4]</span>

### 48.2 The line, defined by two filings

<div class="defn"><b>MRE Contract revenue</b><p>The income-statement line, new in FY2025, for FTAI's sales of CFM56-5B, CFM56-7B and V2500 engines and related modules to the special-purpose vehicles of the 2025 Partnership under the MRE Contract: $335.8M for 2025, $221.2M in the first quarter of 2026, $182.8M in the second (against $69.6M a year earlier) and $404.0M for the first half of 2026 (against $170.2M). The Q2 2026 10-Q defines it as "the transaction price for sales of CFM56-5B, CFM56-7B and V2500 engines and related modules to the SPVs of the first SCI partnership" and describes it as covering "the sale and purchase of engines to and from the 2025 Partnership". The Q3 2025 10-Q describes "stand-ready obligations to provide replacement CFM56-5B and CFM56-7B engines to customers as they become unserviceable". Profit on these related-party sales is eliminated through equity-method earnings. <span class="cite">[FTAI 10-Q Q2 2026, via orchestrator notes; FTAI 10-Q Q3 2025, via D10 §2.4; FTAI 10-K FY2025; D5 §2.3; registry C-15]</span></p></div>

<div class="defn"><b>Stand-ready obligation</b><p>A contractual duty to supply on demand. Under the MRE Contracts FTAI must provide replacement CFM56-5B and -7B engines to the customer as the customer's engines become unserviceable, so it must hold a pool of serviceable engines against the obligation whether or not any particular engine fails in a given period. The FY2025 10-K lists these obligations among its risk factors. <span class="cite">[FTAI 10-Q Q3 2025, via D10 §2.4 and §5; D10 U-15]</span></p></div>

The two filings describe the same line in different registers, and the research recorded the difference as a tension rather than resolving it. <span class="cite">[reconciliation R-044]</span> The Q3 2025 10-Q's language is that of an obligation: FTAI stands ready to supply engines as they become unserviceable, which is the language of a service a provider commits to perform over time. The Q2 2026 10-Q's language is that of sales: a transaction price for engines and modules sold to specified buyers, which is the language of a product delivered at a point in time. One dossier reads the line as "a customer pays FTAI to keep its CFM56 fleet powered"; another reads it as "a sale by FTAI of the replacement module or engine ... usually with the run-out unit taken back as feedstock". <span class="cite">[D10 §2.4; D7 §2.4]</span> Both quotations are from FTAI's own filings of different dates, both are carried here, and the phrase "sale and purchase of engines to and from" in the later filing indicates that units also flow back from the Partnership to FTAI, which is the returned-core mechanism of §46.3 applied at engine level. Whether the line is recognised at points in time, over time, or in some combination was not retrieved. <span class="cite">[D5 U1]</span>

### 48.4 The profit-elimination mechanics, both readings

<div class="defn"><b>Profit elimination (intra-entity profit elimination)</b><p>When a company sells to an entity it accounts for under the equity method, the share of profit on the sale that corresponds to its own ownership is removed from current earnings and recognised later, as the investee realises the asset's value. FTAI's Q2 2026 10-Q: the elimination is "of profit on sales of engines and modules to the 2025 Partnership under ASC 606. The profit from the MRE Contract revenue is eliminated through equity method earnings and will be recognized over time as the 2025 Partnership generates income from leasing and sales activities". Eliminations were $10.0M in Q1 2026, $6.6M in Q2 2026 and $16.6M for the half. Whether the elimination is proportional to FTAI's stake or covers 100% of the profit is not stated. Part VIII §63 carries the full treatment. <span class="cite">[FTAI 10-Q Q2 2026, via D7 §2.5; reconciliation R-047]</span></p></div>

The disclosed sentence supports two readings, and the research presents both without choosing. <span class="cite">[reconciliation R-047; E.1 rule 12]</span> Under the first, the usual treatment of profit on sales to an equity-method investee, FTAI eliminates only the share of profit that corresponds to its own stake, because the other 81% (or 80%) of the Partnership's owners are third parties who have paid an arm's-length price. Under the second, FTAI defers the whole profit until the Partnership realises it. The arithmetic differs by a factor of about five.

<div class="worked"><div class="h">Worked example 7.3 — What $16.6M of eliminations implies about the gross profit on MRE sales to the Partnership, under each reading</div>
<p>Inputs: eliminations of $16.6M for 1H 2026 (sourced: Q2 2026 10-Q, $10.0M plus $6.597M); FTAI's stake of 19% (sourced: FY2025 10-K and 2026 10-Qs) or 20% (sourced: Q3 2025 10-Q); gross MRE Contract revenue to the Partnership of $404.0M for 1H 2026 (sourced: Q2 2026 10-Q).</p>
<div class="eqblock">Reading A, proportional to the stake:
  at 19%: implied gross profit = 16.6 ÷ 0.19 = $87.4M for the half (≈$175M annualised)
  at 20%: implied gross profit = 16.6 ÷ 0.20 = $83.0M for the half (≈$166M annualised)

  implied gross profit = $16.6M for the half (≈$33M annualised)

Under reading A about $87M of half-year gross profit on related-party sales would have been recognised in the segment's revenue and cost of sales and about $17M of it removed one line lower; under reading B the whole profit would have been removed. A reader comparing the segment's reported margin on Partnership volume with its margin on third-party volume cannot do so from the filings. Limits of the example: the elimination method is undisclosed; the 19% and 20% figures come from filings of different dates and may reflect dilution at the final close; the half-year may include catch-up exchanges on newly acquired aircraft; and "gross profit" here is the profit the elimination acts on, whose definition the filing does not give. <span class="cite">[FTAI 10-Q Q2 2026, via D7 §2.5 and orchestrator notes; reconciliation R-047, R-048; D7 T7]</span></p></div>

First, the Partnership also pays FTAI servicing fees, $10.2M in 2025 and $12.85M in the first half of 2026, and FTAI records equity-method income on its stake; neither sits in Aerospace Products, and Part VIII §63 sets out all the streams. <span class="cite">[FTAI 10-K FY2025; FTAI 10-Q Q2 2026, via D7 §2.5]</span> Second, the 10-K accounts for FTAI's sales of aircraft into the Partnership as "non-recurring in nature and not considered part of the Company's ordinary activities", while the engine and module sales under the MRE Contract are ordinary revenue under ASC 606; the two kinds of sale to the same buyer are treated differently, as Part VIII §64 explains. <span class="cite">[FTAI 10-K FY2025, via D7 §2.5; reconciliation R-057]</span>

### 49.2 Capacity: what each statement counts

<p>Inputs: 2,300–2,400 CFM56 shop visits a year in 2026–2028 (sourced: GE via Aviation Week, 2026; the earlier Safran peak of about 2,500 is the alternative, sourced c. 2023/24); three modules per engine (sourced: FTAI release, 26 Feb 2025); four major modules including the gearbox (sourced: Part I §3.2); 1.5 modules exchanged per shop-visit event (one dossier's labelled assumption, D7 §5.2; FTAI discloses no modules-per-event ratio); capacity of 3,000 modules (call coverage) and the 2026 targets of 1,050 and 1,200.</p>
<div class="eqblock">25% of visits = 575–600 visits a year (on GE's range); 625 on Safran's 2,500
if every one of those visits consumed a full engine's modules:
  at 3 per engine: 1,725–1,800 modules a year
  at 4 per engine: 2,300–2,400 modules a year
if each visit consumed 1.5 modules (assumed): 863–900 modules a year
against capacity of 3,000 (call coverage): 58–60% utilised at 3 per engine; 77–80% at 4; 29–30% at 1.5

### 50.1 Montréal: Lockheed Martin Commercial Engine Solutions

Montréal is the place the Module Factory language names. From 2022 FTAI ran the programme at Lockheed Martin Commercial Engine Solutions' facility under a third-party arrangement; it then bought the business. Miami is the brand FTAI uses for quick-turn work. ### 51.2 What FTAI does with V2500 engines and modules

Functionally the V2500 programme is the reverse of the Module Factory. On the CFM56, FTAI is the shop: it holds the inventory, does the work and sells the module. On the V2500, FTAI is the customer: a large engine owner buying manufacturer-network restorations at a negotiated price for a committed volume, then leasing or selling the restored engines and selling V2500 material. <span class="cite">[D5 §2.5]</span> Two things connect the programme to the rest of the segment. First, the MRE Contract definition names "CFM56-5B, CFM56-7B and V2500 engines and related modules" as what FTAI sells to the 2025 Partnership's vehicles, so V2500 engines restored under the IAE agreement are among the units the Partnership's A320ceo aircraft can draw on. <span class="cite">[FTAI 10-Q Q2 2026, via orchestrator notes]</span> Second, the Module Factory language names only the two CFM56 variants; whether FTAI performs any V2500 module work in-house, how many of the 100-plus visits have been completed, and the current V2500 fleet against the 200 target were not retrieved. <span class="cite">[D5 U6]</span> Chromalloy's FAA-approved V2500 high-pressure-turbine blade is a parts input that could bear on the cost of a V2500 restoration; FTAI's statements on it were not retrieved. <span class="cite">[Aviation Week, MRO Europe; D5 §2.5]</span>

### 51.3 The market context

The V2500 pool is about a quarter the size of the CFM56 pool: 5,286 engines in service in 2023 against roughly 23,000 to 24,000 CFM56s (Part I §10.1; Part III §18). <span class="cite">[Aviation Week 2023 Fleet & MRO Forecast; reconciliation R-010]</span> Its shop-visit count carries a direct contradiction that the primer carries in both Parts IV and VII: Aviation Week's data tool projected a spike to about 1,400 visits in 2025, and RTX's chief financial officer, reported by the same publication in 2026, put 2025 at "slightly more than 800" with 2026 expected within about 20 of that. <span class="cite">[Aviation Week data tool, 2023; Aviation Week, 2026; reconciliation R-009; E.1 rule 16]</span> Against either figure, FTAI's roughly 20 committed visits a year is a small share: about 2.5% of 800 or 1.4% of 1,400, derived. The largest independent on the engine, MTU Maintenance, performed 38% of all V2500 visits in 2024. <span class="cite">[MTU Aero Engines, 2025]</span> Part III §24's translation table assigns the V2500 its demand drivers: the grounding of geared-turbofan-powered A320neos pushed A320ceo utilisation up and brought V2500 and -5B visits forward, and the easing of those groundings from 2026 is expected to soften that lift later in the decade. <span class="cite">[D9 §5]</span> The current cost of a V2500 visit was not retrieved; the only figures are fifteen years old and are not set beside the 2026 CFM56 figures of Part I §7. <span class="cite">[Aircraft Commerce Issue 70, 2010; D1 U13]</span>

### 52.2 The Chromalloy approvals as they bear on FTAI

The high-pressure turbine is the module that most often drives a shop visit and the most expensive to restore; its blades and vanes are 15 to 25% of a visit's parts cost on a consultancy blog's figures, and most of an engine's lost temperature margin is recovered there (Part I §3.1 and §5). <span class="cite">[Safe Fly Aviation, 2026; D5 §2.7]</span> An approved non-OEM blade therefore changes the parts bill of exactly the module FTAI's exchanges most often replace. Chromalloy's FAA approval of a CFM56-5B/7B high-pressure-turbine blade on 30 October 2025 is described by Chromalloy as "the only aftermarket alternative to the OEM manufactured new part"; Chromalloy said its order backlog would consume all production for the rest of 2025 with customers "seeking to reserve production slots into 2026". <span class="cite">[Chromalloy release, 30 Oct 2025, via Newswire; AviTrader, 31 Oct 2025]</span>

How many CFM56 parts Chromalloy has approved depends on which reading of its releases is taken. One dossier counts three announced approvals (the HPT blade, the HPT stage-1 vane and an LPT vane up-change); another reads the 30 October 2025 release as saying Chromalloy "now offers four PMA parts on the CFM56-5B/7B: the HPT blade, the HPT nozzle guide vane, the LPT stage-1 nozzle guide vane and the HPT shroud". <span class="cite">[D4 §4.2; D5 §2.7; reconciliation R-025; E.1 rule 33]</span> Both readings are carried. Part VI §41 and §43 give the roster, the dates and the investor-blog claims that the venture was formed in 2018 as an "exclusive perpetual partnership" covering five hot-section parts supplied to FTAI "at cost". <span class="cite">[Komodo Capital; Kairos Research; In Practise, via D4 §4.3; reconciliation R-026]</span>

What FTAI's filings say about the venture is one clause: FTAI "develops and manufactures PMA parts through a joint venture". <span class="cite">[FTAI 10-K FY2025]</span> The filings' equity-method note lists an "Advanced Engine Repair JV", 25% owned, with $15.0M invested in December 2016 and $13.5M more in August 2019 and a carrying value of $22,429 thousand at 31 December 2025. Whether that venture is the Chromalloy PMA joint venture is unknown; the dates (2016 against the blogs' 2018) do not match, and this primer reports the figures as an equity-method investment without equating the two. <span class="cite">[FTAI 10-K FY2025, via orchestrator notes; reconciliation R-026; registry 1c]</span> No PMA revenue, no equity-method earnings line for the venture and no FTAI-stated saving per module were retrieved. <span class="cite">[D5 U11; D4 U3; cluster K-04]</span>

### 52.3 The CFM materials agreement

<div class="defn"><b>CFM materials agreement</b><p>A multi-year agreement between FTAI and CFM International announced on 22 January 2026 under which, in the release's words, FTAI "secures OEM replacement part supply, thrust performance upgrades and component repair" for CFM56 engines. The chief executive described it on the 26 February 2026 call as "a materials agreement with CFM that includes parts and repairs" and named it, with the PMA blade and more used material, as a lever toward segment margins approaching 40%. Its duration, volumes, pricing and any restriction on FTAI's use of PMA parts were not disclosed. Part VI §42 places it among the OEM's responses to alternative parts. <span class="cite">[FTAI / GlobeNewswire, 22 Jan 2026; MarketBeat, 26 Feb 2026; D4 §4.3, U14; D5 §2.7, U18; cluster K-19]</span></p></div>

The agreement sits oddly beside the PMA lever only at first sight. The two address different parts of the bill. No alternative manufacturer makes a life-limited part for the CFM56, so every new disk, spool and shaft is a single-source purchase from CFM at catalogue price (Part I §4.4; Part VI §38). <span class="cite">[D4 U7]</span> Thrust-rating upgrades, which turn a low-rated engine into a higher-rated one worth more, are sold only by the manufacturer (Part I §1). <span class="cite">[D3 §2.4]</span> And the manufacturer's own repairs, of which CFM says it has "industrialized about 400 CFM56 repairs since early 2023", are an input any shop buys. <span class="cite">[CFM International, via D4 §2.7]</span> GE's stated posture is that it will "continue to strengthen MRO access to OEM materials to support further CFM56 longevity" within "an open network ... with multiple providers who compete and invest to win shop visits". <span class="cite">[GE Aerospace, 2026, via D2 §2.3]</span> Ryanair's "long-term materials agreement" covering about 2,000 CFM56 and LEAP engines is the other named example of such an agreement, under GE's TrueChoice Material brand (Part IV §28). <span class="cite">[GE 10-K FY2025, via D2 §2.3]</span> The terms of FTAI's agreement were located as a release title and not read. <span class="cite">[D4 [23]; D8 §2.7]</span>

### 53.5 Inventory, cash flow and capital expenditure

<div class="defn"><b>Inventory (the Module Factory's working capital)</b><p>Serviceable modules and parts awaiting exchange or sale, plus unserviceable engines, modules and parts in work: $1,364.3M net at 31 March 2026. Under US accounting, cash spent buying assets for sale is an operating outflow and cash spent buying assets for lease is an investing outflow, so the inventory build is why FTAI's operating cash flow was negative in 2024 and 2025 while the company reported net income. The balances at 31 December 2025 and 30 June 2026, the segment's capital expenditure and the cost of building a module were not retrieved. Part X §81 carries the consolidated cash-flow treatment. <span class="cite">[FTAI 10-Q Q1 2026, via D5 §2.8; D10 §2.6; D5 U12; cluster K-18]</span></p></div>

### 53.7 Two comparison points, each on its own basis

The peer research chose StandardAero and GE Aerospace as the listed companies whose filings isolate the activities a module business performs. <span class="cite">[D11 §2.1]</span> Neither sells what FTAI sells, and Part XI §87 to §89 carry the full comparison with every caveat; two facts from each belong here.

StandardAero, an independent engine shop, reported Engine Services revenue of $5,354.0M for 2025 with Segment Adjusted EBITDA of $706.9M, a 13.2% margin on its own definition, and described a CFM56-dedicated "Center of Excellence" in Dallas, opened in the second half of 2024, on which it had spent more than $100M since 2022 to "more than double our shop visit capacity on the CFM56-7B". <span class="cite">[StandardAero 10-K FY2025, via D11 §3.6]</span> Its unit is inductions; FTAI's is modules; and StandardAero sells labour and material on a customer's engine while FTAI sells a module it owns, so the 13.2% and FTAI's 34.7% are on the same EBITDA basis but not for the same activity. <span class="cite">[D11 §2.3, §3.6; E.1 rule 35]</span>

GE Aerospace's Commercial Engines & Services segment reported 2025 revenue of $33,314M, of which services were $25,010M, and segment profit of $8,861M, a 26.6% margin on GE's own measure; it attributed growth to "increased spare parts volume, internal shop visit volume and workscopes, increased engine deliveries and pricing". <span class="cite">[GE 10-K FY2025, via D11 §3.8]</span> GE is at once the supplier of the new parts FTAI's modules consume, the seller of the TrueChoice Transitions exchanges of §46.5 and the largest provider of used material for its own products; its services revenue is the size of the pool from which the segment's $1.9B is drawn. <span class="cite">[D11 §3.8; D2 §2.3]</span>
