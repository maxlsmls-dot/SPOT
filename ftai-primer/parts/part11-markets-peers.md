<div class="part-divider"><div class="pn">Part XI</div><h1>Markets and money: margin pools and peers</h1><p>This Part sets FTAI Aviation beside the other companies that make money from the same engines, link by link along the chain: the manufacturer, the independent shops, the makers of approved alternative parts, the traders in used material, and the lessors. Each company's figures come from its own filing and carry their own basis. A reader who finishes it will be able to read any one of those filings against FTAI's and know, before comparing a single number, which numbers can be compared and which cannot.</p></div>

## 86. The margin pool across the chain

Part I §11 gave a first map of where money is made in keeping a CFM56 flying. This section builds the map link by link from the filings, with every margin on its stated basis, and then explains mechanically why each link's margin sits where it does. It ranks nothing. A high percentage margin and a low one can describe businesses of equal or unequal quality; the percentage is a function of what sits in the revenue line and what sits above and below the profit line, and that is what this section takes apart.

<div class="defn"><b>Margin pool</b><p>The margin pool is this primer's term for how the profit earned in keeping an engine type flying is distributed across the links of the chain: the manufacturer's new parts and services, the shops' labour and test, the component repairers, the makers of approved alternative parts, the traders in used material and teardown, the lessors of engines and aircraft, and the servicers of third-party capital. No company reports a "margin pool"; the term names a way of reading the filings together. Its inputs are the slice-by-slice capture of a shop visit (Part IV §29) and each peer's reported margin on its own basis (this section). Part I §11 carried the preview and Part XII §92 carries the summary table. <span class="cite">[registry 1c; D2 §2.2; D11 §4.2]</span></p></div>

<div class="defn"><b>Comparable metrics</b><p>Comparable metrics are the figures from a peer's filing that can be set beside a figure in FTAI's filings because both measure the same physical or financial quantity: owned engines, utilisation on a stated definition, maintenance-reserve revenue, gain on sale of leased equipment, a segment margin on a stated basis, approvals of alternative parts per year, gross margin on used material, and the services share of a manufacturer's revenue. The phrase is the primer's; the filings supply the quantities without naming the concept. A metric is comparable only when its definition, its period and its unit match on both sides, which §88 tests one by one. <span class="cite">[registry 1c; D11 §2.1]</span></p></div>

<div class="defn"><b>Segment margin bases (five)</b><p>The peers in this Part report profit on five different bases. Segment Adjusted EBITDA (StandardAero and FTAI) is segment profit before depreciation, amortisation and items each company defines as adjustments. Segment operating income (HEICO) is after depreciation and amortisation. Adjusted EBIT (MTU and Lufthansa Technik) is operating profit after depreciation and amortisation but before purchase-price-allocation effects and other defined adjustments. GE segment profit is GE's own measure, excluding certain corporate and non-operating items. Gross margin (AerSale) is sales less cost of sales, before all operating expenses. An EBITDA margin exceeds an EBIT margin for the same business by the depreciation share of revenue, so none of the five can be set beside another without an adjustment, and the adjusting figure is often not disclosed. Three fiscal calendars (December, October, May) add a second layer. <span class="cite">[D11 §2.3, §2.5; reconciliation E.1 rule 35]</span></p></div>

### 86.1 The bill before the margin: a shop visit, slice by slice

Every margin in this section is earned on some slice of the money an engine owner spends. The largest single spending event is the shop visit (Part I §6 and §7), so the pool is easiest to see by starting with one visit and asking who collects each part of the bill. Air Cargo Week, a trade publication, puts material at 60–70% of a visit, labour at 20–30% and repairs and other shop work at 10–20%. <span class="cite">[Air Cargo Week, 2025/26]</span> Safe Fly Aviation, a consultancy blog, gives a finer cut for the CFM56: life-limited parts are 40–60% of parts cost when replaced, high-pressure turbine blades and vanes 15–25%, labour 15–20%. <span class="cite">[Safe Fly Aviation, 2026, consultancy blog]</span> The two breakdowns come from different secondary sources and neither names a shop-level data set; Part I §7.2 carries the tension between them. <span class="cite">[reconciliation R-014]</span>

Two mechanical consequences follow, and both shape every margin below. Because 60–70% of the visit is material and most material is priced off the manufacturer's catalogue, the catalogue's annual repricing flows into the cost of every visit regardless of who performs it. <span class="cite">[D2 §2.2]</span> And because the shop that is not the manufacturer buys those parts at list, its only levers on the bill sit on the material line: used parts instead of new, approved alternative parts instead of the manufacturer's, repair instead of replacement, and exchange instead of overhaul. Parts V, VI and VII treat those four levers; this section shows what each one's practitioner reports earning.

### 86.2 Link one: the manufacturer's new parts and services

GE Aerospace reports the CFM56's manufacturer side in one segment, Commercial Engines & Services (CES). For 2025 CES had equipment revenue of $8,304M and services revenue of $25,010M, a total of $33,314M, so services were 75.1% of the segment (derived in the research). Segment profit was $8,861M, a margin of 26.6%. Revenue rose $6.4B (24%) and profit $1.8B (26%). <span class="cite">[GE 10-K FY2025]</span> GE names the drivers in two lists. Revenue grew on "increased spare parts volume, internal shop visit volume and workscopes, increased engine deliveries and pricing". Profit grew on "increased spare parts volume, internal shop visit volume and workscopes and improved pricing", partly offset by "the impact of higher install engine deliveries, inflation, higher growth investment and an unfavorable change in estimated profitability of long-term service agreements, primarily from the estimated impact from tariffs". <span class="cite">[GE 10-K FY2025]</span> Internal shop-visit revenue grew 24% in 2025, after 19% in 2024 and 27% in 2023. <span class="cite">[GE 10-K FY2025, via D2 §4.3]</span>

<div class="defn"><b>GE segment profit</b><p>GE segment profit is GE Aerospace's own segment-level profit measure, which excludes certain corporate and non-operating items. For Commercial Engines & Services it was $8,861M on $33,314M in 2025, a 26.6% margin. It is a different level from GE Aerospace's total operating profit of $9.1B, which comes after the Defense & Propulsion Technologies segment and corporate items. Whether depreciation sits above or below GE's segment profit line was not retrieved in the research, so the measure is carried as GE's own and not re-labelled as EBIT or EBITDA. <span class="cite">[GE 10-K FY2025; D11 §2.3, §6 T4]</span></p></div>

The two profit figures in GE's filing need to be held apart. The 10-K text returned gives CES segment profit of $8,861M and GE Aerospace operating profit of $9.1B, up 25%; free cash flow was $7.7B, up 24%. <span class="cite">[GE 10-K FY2025]</span> The two sit at different levels, segment versus total company, and the items that reconcile them (the Defense & Propulsion Technologies segment's profit and the corporate lines) were not returned. Read one way, the CES figure is the manufacturer's commercial aftermarket profit and the $9.1B is the whole company; read the other way, $9.1B is the figure to set beside other companies' operating profit and $8,861M is a sub-total that excludes items the others include. Both are recorded; the research did not retrieve the bridge, and this Part does not construct one. <span class="cite">[D11 §6 T4]</span> GE's total company revenue for 2025 was also not returned, so no total-company margin is computed here. <span class="cite">[D11 U15]</span>

Three further statements in GE's filing bear on the pool's size and life. On the fleet: "The LEAP engine, which entered into service in 2016, is expected in the coming years to overtake the mature CFM56 as the industry's largest fleet. This is expected to drive a significant increase in shop visits and need for MRO capacity as LEAP engines come due for services." On operations: about 1,450 LEAP-1A durability kits (the upgraded-parts package treated in Part III §20) had been deployed across new production and overhaul shops since certification, and turnaround times for LEAP, CFM56 and GE90 improved "by over 10% year-over-year in the fourth quarter of 2025". On deliveries: 2,386 commercial engines in 2025, 1,802 of them LEAP, up 25%. <span class="cite">[GE 10-K FY2025]</span> A CFM56 shop-visit count, a spare-parts price-increase percentage and any wording on a used-material programme were searched for in the filing and not returned; what the research holds on those three points comes from trade press and is carried in Part IV §26 and §28. <span class="cite">[D11 U16; reconciliation C.11]</span>

Safran, the other half of CFM, is not an SEC filer and is carried as context. Its Propulsion services revenue for 2025 was €10,122M, 64.6% of Propulsion revenue, against €8,430M and 61.7% in 2024, which implies Propulsion revenue of about €15.7B (derived in the research as €10,122M ÷ 0.646). Its civil installed base at end-2025 was about 29,900 engines "including more than 22,800 CFM56s", from which Safran "can expect sustained aftermarket revenue from this fleet until 2030". <span class="cite">[Safran FY2025 results presentation, 13 Feb 2026]</span> Civil engine spare-parts revenue in dollars rose 17.6% in 2025, "driven primarily by the CFM56". <span class="cite">[Safran, 13 Feb 2026, via D2 §4.3]</span> Safran's CFM56 count is one of three OEM-level fleet figures the primer carries together with the other two, about 23,000 (CFM website, undated) and about 24,000 (Aviation Business News, September 2025); Part III §18 treats the difference. <span class="cite">[reconciliation R-001; E.1 rule 14]</span>

The manufacturer's pricing is the one input every other link's margin is measured against, so its escalation belongs here even though Part IV §28 owns it. ### 86.3 Link two: the independent shop

The independent shop earns the labour-and-test slice, the shop margin, and whatever it saves on material by sourcing used, alternative or repaired parts; it passes the rest of the material through. Three shops report margins the research retrieved, on two bases.

<div class="defn"><b>Adjusted EBIT</b><p>Adjusted EBIT is operating profit after depreciation and amortisation, before purchase-price-allocation effects and other adjustments each company defines. MTU Aero Engines reports its commercial maintenance business on this basis (€478M on €6.0B in 2025, 8.0%), and Lufthansa Technik reports its whole company on it (€603M on €8.049B, 7.5%). Because depreciation has already been deducted, an adjusted EBIT margin is lower than an EBITDA margin for the same business by the depreciation share of revenue, and the two cannot be compared without that share, which neither company's retrieved figures give. <span class="cite">[MTU, 24 Feb 2026; Lufthansa Technik, FY2025 results; D11 §2.3, §8]</span></p></div>

StandardAero, listed on the NYSE since October 2024, reports two segments. Engine Services, the shop-visit business, had revenue of $5,354.0M in 2025, up $709.3M (15.3%) from $4,644.7M, and Segment Adjusted EBITDA of $706.9M, up $96.0M (15.7%) from $610.9M; the margin was 13.2% in both years (derived in the research for 2024). Component Repair Services had revenue of $708.6M, up 19.6%, and Segment Adjusted EBITDA of $202.7M, a 28.6% margin. <span class="cite">[StandardAero 10-K FY2025]</span> The filing attributes Engine Services growth to "ramping volumes from LEAP, CFM56 DFW Center of Excellence, and CF34 expansion investments", plus business-aviation and military programmes. <span class="cite">[StandardAero 10-K FY2025]</span> The two segments' margins are fifteen points apart within one company, and the difference is itself a lesson in the pool: the component-repair segment sells repairs on parts, where the shop's own work is most of the price, while the engine-services segment sells whole shop visits, where new parts pass through the revenue line at list. <span class="cite">[D11 §2.4; D2 §2.2]</span>

MTU Aero Engines, listed in Frankfurt, reported commercial maintenance revenue of €6.0B in 2025, up 18% from €5.1B, and adjusted EBIT of €478M, up 9% from €438M, so the margin fell from 8.7% to 8.0%. MTU attributes the decline to "the higher proportion of Geared Turbofan MRO as well as the costs associated with the ramp-up of our Fort Worth site"; Geared Turbofan work was "around 40% of commercial maintenance in 2025". At group level MTU reported adjusted revenue of €8.7B and adjusted EBIT of €1.35B, a 15.5% margin (14.0% in 2024). <span class="cite">[MTU, 24 Feb 2026]</span> Lufthansa Technik, a segment of Deutsche Lufthansa and not separately listed, reported revenue of €8.049B, up 12% and above €8B for the first time, and adjusted EBIT of €603M, down 1%, so the margin fell from 8.5% to 7.5%; it names US tariffs, "ongoing cost increases for materials and a dollar devaluation that was unfavorable". Engine Services "generates almost half" of its revenue. <span class="cite">[Lufthansa Technik, FY2025 results; Deutsche Lufthansa Annual Report 2025]</span>

The three shops' margins moved differently in the same year, and the research flags the pattern as a tension rather than a trend. On one side, StandardAero's Engine Services margin held at 13.2% in both 2024 and 2025 while MTU's fell from 8.7% to 8.0% and Lufthansa Technik's from 8.5% to 7.5%, which could be read as the independent shops diverging. On the other side, the bases differ (Segment Adjusted EBITDA for StandardAero against adjusted EBIT for the two Europeans), the platform mixes differ (MTU names the Geared Turbofan at about 40% of its work; Lufthansa Technik names tariffs, materials and the dollar; StandardAero names LEAP, CFM56 and CF34 volume), and the currencies differ. A business whose depreciation share rose in the year, as a shop ramping new sites would expect, can show a flat EBITDA margin and a falling EBIT margin at once. <span class="cite">[StandardAero 10-K FY2025; MTU, 24 Feb 2026; Lufthansa Technik, FY2025 results; D11 §6 T3]</span> The figures cannot be read as one series, and this Part does not read them as one.

### 86.4 Link three: the maker of approved alternative parts

A PMA part (Parts Manufacturer Approval, the FAA approval that lets a company other than the manufacturer design and make a replacement part; Part VI §37) is sold under the manufacturer's list price and competes for the same slot in the shop visit as the new OEM part and the used one. HEICO's Flight Support Group (FSG) is the listed reference business. For the fiscal year ended 31 October 2025 FSG had net sales of $3,117.3M, up 18% from $2,639.4M, and operating income of $750.4M, up 27% from $593.1M. The operating margin was 24.1% (derived in the research), against 22.5% in fiscal 2024. <span class="cite">[HEICO 10-K FY2025; HEICO release, 18 Dec 2025]</span> HEICO describes FSG as a business that "designs, manufactures, repairs, overhauls and distributes jet engine and aircraft component replacement parts, which are approved by the FAA", using "proprietary technology to design and manufacture jet engine and aircraft component replacement parts for sale at lower prices than those manufactured by OEMs". <span class="cite">[HEICO 10-K FY2025]</span> Two readings of the same filing give the rate at which HEICO adds approvals: "approximately 300 to 500" a year in one dossier's search and "approximately 400 to 550 Parts Manufacturer Approvals per year" in another's. Both come from the FY2025 10-K and both are carried. <span class="cite">[HEICO 10-K FY2025; reconciliation R-024]</span>

Three cautions attach to the 24.1%. FSG is not a pure PMA business; it includes repair and distribution, and its "aftermarket replacement parts" line grew 15% organically in fiscal 2025. <span class="cite">[HEICO release, 18 Dec 2025, via D4 §4.1]</span> The operating margin is after depreciation and amortisation, so it sits on a lower base than StandardAero's or FTAI's EBITDA figures and cannot be set beside them without the depreciation share, which was not retrieved. And HEICO's stated discount to OEM price, its cumulative part count and its consolidated totals were searched for and not returned. <span class="cite">[D11 U5; reconciliation K-05]</span> FTAI's own PMA activity runs through a joint venture whose revenue is not separately disclosed; Part VI §43 and Part VII §52 carry what is filed and what is reported. <span class="cite">[FTAI 10-K FY2025; reconciliation R-026]</span>

The size of the PMA market the research reached comes from market-research vendors and is labelled so: $11.8B for commercial-aircraft PMA in 2025 (IMARC); $14B by 2026 (Global Industry Analysts, 2022); $2,952.2M for engine PMA plus DER repair in 2022 rising to $6,174.7M by 2032 (a vendor report listed on GII); and an undated FlightGlobal article describing PMA's share of engine parts as "capped at around 21%". The scope definitions differ and were not retrieved. <span class="cite">[IMARC, 2025; Global Industry Analysts via PR Newswire, 2022; GII listing; FlightGlobal, undated; reconciliation R-023]</span>

### 86.5 Link four: used material, teardown and distribution

Used serviceable material (USM, parts recovered from dismantled engines, inspected and re-released; Part V §31) is the other substitute for the new part. Its economics are a gross-margin story: buy feedstock (whole aircraft or engines bought for teardown; Part V §33), dismantle, certify, hold inventory, sell parts at a percentage of list. AerSale's Asset Management Solutions (AMS) segment is the cleanest listed example because the filing isolates it.

<div class="defn"><b>Gross margin (AerSale Asset Management Solutions)</b><p>AerSale's AMS gross margin is the segment's gross profit divided by its sales: $74.1M on $211.6M in 2025, 35.0% (derived in the research), against $82.5M on $215.5M in 2024, 38.3%. The filing attributes the fall to "lower margin on USM sales resulting from changes in the product mix" and reports the aircraft line within AMS at 33.2% against 34.8%. It is the only listed margin for a business whose activity is buying whole assets and either leasing or selling them whole or dismantling them for used material. Being a gross margin, it is before every operating expense, so it is the highest of the five bases in this Part and the furthest from a profit figure. <span class="cite">[AerSale 10-K FY2025; AerSale release, 5 Mar 2026; D11 §2.4, §3.5]</span></p></div>

AerSale's total revenue for 2025 was $335.3M, down 2.8% from $345.1M; AMS sales were $211.6M, down $3.9M; AMS gross profit was $74.1M, down $8.4M (10.2%). <span class="cite">[AerSale 10-K FY2025; AerSale release, 5 Mar 2026]</span> Engine revenue rose on higher USM sales (+$34.0M) and higher leasing revenue (+$7.5M), "primarily attributable to greater activity in the PW4000 and CF6-80 product lines as the company continues to monetize its feedstock"; those are widebody engines (the PW4000 is a Pratt & Whitney engine and the CF6-80 a GE engine, both for widebody aircraft), and the CFM56 is not named in the returned text. <span class="cite">[AerSale 10-K FY2025]</span> The company's used-material revenue across both segments was $137.6M in 2025, and its whole-asset sales fell to $56.4M from 13 engines, against $110.1M from 20 engines and one aircraft in 2024; its Adjusted EBITDA (AerSale's adjusted earnings measure, defined by the company) was $46.1M, 13.8% of revenue, against $33.4M and 9.7%. <span class="cite">[AerSale release, 5 Mar 2026, via D3 §4.6]</span> Management described "a hypercompetitive feedstock environment, marked by increased scrutiny and pressure on pricing as more companies enter the teardown sector", a statement the research holds only as relayed in search results and not from the filing text. <span class="cite">[AerSale, as relayed; D3 §2.6; D11 U12]</span>

The teardown margin in dollars per engine comes from a consultancy blog and is labelled so. Safe Fly Aviation gives a run-out CFM56 bought for $0.8–1.2M yielding $1.6–2.2M of used material, a net margin of $0.4–0.8M per engine; the difference between yield-minus-purchase and the stated net margin implies $0.2–0.4M of dismantling, repair, certification and selling cost, which is arithmetic on the source's endpoints rather than a figure it gives. <span class="cite">[Safe Fly Aviation, 2026, consultancy blog; D3 §2.2]</span> Part V §32 works the full part-out. Used-material prices sit at 60–80% of a new part's price on average, up to 85% for high-demand parts, and above list when the OEM cannot deliver, in one trade source, and at 70–75% of catalogue list for a specialist trader selling to airlines in another. <span class="cite">[Aviation Week, date not captured; Aircraft Commerce Issue 120; reconciliation R-022]</span> Because used material is priced off list, it inherits the manufacturer's escalation.

The market's size is contested among vendors and the figures are carried with that label: more than $4B a year with the CFM56 the largest segment (Safe Fly, consultancy blog); $5.87B (The Business Research Company), $7.7B (Market.us) and $8.55B (Fortune Business Insights, URL not isolated), all for 2025 and all from market-research vendors whose scope definitions were not retrieved. <span class="cite">[D2 §4.6; D3 T1; reconciliation R-021]</span>

Because the manufacturer sets the cost of goods, a distribution margin is structurally thinner than a PMA or used-material margin. ### 86.6 Link five: the lessors

Lessors own the engines and aircraft and collect rent, maintenance reserves and gains on sale. Their percentage margins look nothing like a shop's, for a mechanical reason set out in §86.9: a lessor's largest costs are depreciation and interest, and both sit below EBITDA.

<div class="defn"><b>Net book value (NBV)</b><p>Net book value is an asset's cost less accumulated depreciation and impairments. It is the denominator for a lessor's implied yield and the figure against which a gain on sale is measured. Air Lease's flight equipment subject to operating lease had a net book value of $29.1B at 31 Dec 2025; FTAI's leasing equipment, net, was $1,545.8M at the same date, down from $2,373.7M a year earlier after the Seed Asset sales. Part VIII §58 carries FTAI's line. <span class="cite">[Air Lease 10-K FY2025; FTAI 10-K FY2025; D11 §2.2; D6 §2.6]</span></p></div>

Willis Lease Finance, the only US-listed company whose primary business is engine leasing, had total revenue of $730.2M in 2025, up 28.3%, and Adjusted EBITDA of $459.1M, up 16.6%, which is 62.9% of revenue (derived in the research). <span class="cite">[WLFC 10-K FY2025]</span> Its lease rent revenue was $291.6M, up 22.4% from $238.2M. Its "core lease rent and maintenance reserve revenues" were $523.6M, up 15.8% from $452.1M, so maintenance reserve revenue was $232.0M (derived in the research by subtraction), 44% of the combined line. Gain on sale of leased equipment was $54.0M, up 19.9%. <span class="cite">[WLFC 10-K FY2025; D11 §2.2]</span> Maintenance reserves (the per-hour or per-cycle payments a lessee makes so that the next shop visit is pre-funded; Part II §13) become revenue when the lessor determines they will not be paid back, typically at lease end or under a non-reimbursable lease. For a lessor of mid-life engines the reserve line is large relative to rent because the assets are close to shop visits; at Willis it was about 80% of lease rent in 2025 (232.0 ÷ 291.6, arithmetic in this Part on the filed and derived figures). <span class="cite">[WLFC 10-K FY2025; D11 §2.2]</span>

### 86.9 Why each link's margin sits where it does

The percentages in Exhibit 11.7 run from 7.5% to 62.9%, and the spread is almost entirely explained by three mechanical facts about each link: how much of the revenue line is somebody else's product passing through, how much capital the link ties up and where that capital's cost is booked, and whether the link sets a price or takes one. None of the three says anything about which link is the better business. A thin percentage on a large pass-through can be a large dollar profit; a wide percentage on a small revenue line can be a small one.

<div class="box"><div class="h">Why a parts pass-through compresses a percentage margin</div><p>Take an illustrative shop (all figures assumed) that sells $1.0M of labour and test on a visit at a 30% margin, earning $300,000, and buys $2.0M of new parts at list for the same visit, passing them through at a 5% handling margin, earning $100,000. Its revenue is $3.0M and its profit $400,000, a 13.3% margin. If the airline instead buys the parts directly from the manufacturer under a materials agreement, the shop's revenue is $1.0M and its profit $300,000, a 30% margin, on $100,000 less profit. The shop did the same work in both cases. The percentage moved because the denominator changed, not the business. This is the arithmetic behind the fifteen-point gap between StandardAero's two segments and behind the gap between any shop's margin and a parts maker's. <span class="cite">[mechanism from D2 §2.2; figures assumed, illustrative]</span></p></div>

The manufacturer sets the list price and every other link prices off it. Its services revenue is three-quarters of its commercial segment, and the CFM56 part of that revenue is 85% spare parts and time-and-materials work rather than long-term contracts, so on this engine the manufacturer's capture is mostly the parts line, sold at a price it revises upward each year. <span class="cite">[GE 10-K FY2025; Safran via Aviation Week, c. 2023/24]</span> Its percentage margin is reduced by the new engines it sells into the fleet, which GE lists among the offsets to profit ("the impact of higher install engine deliveries"), and by changes in the estimated profitability of its flight-hour contracts, on which it bears the cost risk. <span class="cite">[GE 10-K FY2025]</span> The PMA maker prices under the manufacturer's list; it carries the design and approval cost up front, at 300–550 approvals a year each requiring a separate FAA action, and then manufactures parts whose price is anchored to a list it did not set. <span class="cite">[HEICO 10-K FY2025; D4 §2.2]</span> The used-material trader also prices off list, at 60–80% of it, and its gross margin is what is left after the feedstock purchase; when retirements run low, feedstock gets dearer and the margin narrows, which is what AerSale's filing describes in attributing its three-point fall to mix and its management describes as a hypercompetitive feedstock market. <span class="cite">[AerSale 10-K FY2025; Aviation Week; D3 §2.6]</span> The distributor earns the thinnest percentage because the manufacturer sets both its cost of goods and, through list, the ceiling on its price. <span class="cite">[D11 §2.4]</span>

The independent shop is a price taker on material and a price setter only on its own labour and test. Its revenue line therefore carries the manufacturer's parts at list, which compresses the percentage as the callout shows, and its own levers are to substitute used, alternative or repaired parts for new ones. <span class="cite">[D2 §2.2]</span> Its capital is mostly people, tooling and working capital; its depreciation share of revenue is modest in a mature shop and rises when it builds new sites, which is why MTU names the Fort Worth ramp-up as a drag on an EBIT margin and why StandardAero's EBITDA margin need not move for the same reason. <span class="cite">[MTU, 24 Feb 2026; StandardAero 10-K FY2025]</span> The component repairer sits between the shop and the parts maker: its revenue is mostly its own work on a part it did not sell, so its percentage is wider than the shop's and narrower than a parts maker's gross margin. <span class="cite">[StandardAero 10-K FY2025]</span>

The lessor's capital is its product. Its revenue is rent on assets it owns, and its two largest costs, depreciation of those assets and interest on the debt that bought them, sit below EBITDA; so its EBITDA margin is high by construction and says little until depreciation and interest are deducted. Willis's 62.9% is on that basis. FTAI's consolidated statement shows the scale of the lines that sit below its own Adjusted EBITDA: depreciation and amortisation of $225.8M and interest expense of $247.8M in FY2025, against total Adjusted EBITDA of $1,190.9M. <span class="cite">[WLFC 10-K FY2025; FTAI 10-K FY2025, via orchestrator notes]</span> A lessor's yield, not its margin, is the comparable figure, and worked example 11.2 derives it. The mid-life lessor also collects a maintenance-reserve line that a new-aircraft lessor barely has, about 80% of rent at Willis against about 10% at AerCap, because its engines are near shop visits; that line is cash the lessor will mostly spend on the next visit or pass to the next lessee, so its size is a fact about the fleet's age rather than about profit. <span class="cite">[WLFC 10-K FY2025; AerCap Q4 2025 release; D11 §2.2]</span>

FTAI's Aerospace Products segment sells a refurbished module or engine rather than a labour-and-material visit priced to a customer, so its revenue line is the product's price and its cost line is the feedstock, the parts it bought (new, PMA or used) and the shop work, whether its own or a partner's. Its 34.7% and 28.5% are therefore closer in construction to a parts business's margin than to a shop's, and the research warns against reading them beside StandardAero's 13.2% as the same activity measured twice. <span class="cite">[D11 §2.3; D5 §2.2]</span> Management named the levers it expects to move the margin, the PMA high-pressure turbine blade, more used material and the CFM materials agreement, without a dollar figure, and the 2026 aspiration to "approach 40%" sits beside the first-half 2026 figures of 29.9% and 28.5%; Part VII §53 and §57 carry that bridge. <span class="cite">[MarketBeat, 26 Feb 2026, call coverage; FTAI Q1 and Q2 2026 releases; reconciliation R-027]</span>

<div class="worked"><div class="h">Worked example 11.1 — Putting an EBITDA margin and an EBIT margin on one footing with depreciation and amortisation</div>
<p>The question. StandardAero's Engine Services segment reports 13.2% Segment Adjusted EBITDA (sourced: StandardAero 10-K FY2025). MTU's commercial maintenance reports 8.0% adjusted EBIT (sourced: MTU, 24 Feb 2026). Can the 5.2-point gap be read as a difference in profitability?</p>
<p>The identity. For any business, <span class="eq">EBIT margin = EBITDA margin − (D&A ÷ revenue)</span>, where D&A is depreciation and amortisation. So the two can be compared only if the D&A share of revenue is known for at least one of them, and neither company's retrieved figures give it (assumed below, in three cases).</p>
<div class="eqblock">StandardAero ES, moved to an EBIT basis:
  D&A at 3% of revenue: 13.2 − 3.0 = 10.2% EBIT
  D&A at 5% of revenue: 13.2 − 5.0 =  8.2% EBIT
  D&A at 7% of revenue: 13.2 − 7.0 =  6.2% EBIT
MTU commercial maintenance, moved to an EBITDA basis:
  D&A at 3% of revenue:  8.0 + 3.0 = 11.0% EBITDA
  D&A at 5% of revenue:  8.0 + 5.0 = 13.0% EBITDA
  D&A at 7% of revenue:  8.0 + 7.0 = 15.0% EBITDA</div>
<p>Reading. At a 5% D&A share on both sides the two businesses are within 0.2 points of each other on either basis (8.2% against 8.0% EBIT; 13.2% against 13.0% EBITDA). At 3% StandardAero sits above MTU on both bases; at 7% it sits below. The headline gap of 5.2 points is therefore inside the range that plausible D&A shares alone would produce, and the two figures cannot be ordered without the D&A line. A real data point for scale: FTAI's consolidated D&A was $225,797K on revenue of $2,507,409K in FY2025, 9.0% (sourced: FTAI 10-K FY2025), but most of that is depreciation of leasing equipment, so it is not a proxy for a shop's share and is shown only to illustrate the size the term can take.</p>
<p>Second-order limits. Even with D&A known, the two measures would still differ: StandardAero's "adjusted" removes items it defines as non-recurring, MTU's removes purchase-price-allocation effects, and the currencies and platform mixes differ (D11 T3). HEICO's 24.1% is a GAAP segment operating income, after D&A, so it sits on the EBIT side of this identity; FTAI's 34.7% and 28.5% sit on the EBITDA side. Any exhibit that lists them in one column, including Exhibit 11.7, lists five bases and not one. <span class="cite">[StandardAero 10-K FY2025; MTU, 24 Feb 2026; HEICO 10-K FY2025; FTAI 10-K FY2025; D11 §2.3, §6 T3]</span></p></div>

### 87.1 Willis Lease Finance Corporation (NASDAQ: WLFC)

Willis Lease owns commercial jet engines and a small number of aircraft and leases them to airlines and shops; it collects maintenance reserves, sells engines and parts out of the portfolio through Willis Aero, and manages engines for third parties. It is the only US-listed company whose primary business is engine leasing, which makes it the closest filing analogue to FTAI's Aviation Leasing segment. <span class="cite">[WLFC 10-K FY2025; D11 §3.1]</span>

For the year ended 31 December 2025, Willis reported total revenue of $730.2M, up 28.3%; net income attributable to common shareholders of $108.1M, up 3.5%; and Adjusted EBITDA of $459.1M, up 16.6%, which is 62.9% of revenue (derived in the research). Lease rent revenue was $291.6M, up 22.4%; core lease rent and maintenance reserve revenues together were $523.6M, up 15.8%; gain on sale of leased equipment was $54.0M, up 19.9%. <span class="cite">[WLFC 10-K FY2025]</span> At year-end the operating lease portfolio comprised $2,801.7M of equipment held for lease, $139.9M of notes receivable, $30.6M of maintenance rights and $16.6M of investments in sales-type leases, representing 363 engines, 20 aircraft, one marine vessel and other leased parts and equipment, with 69 lessees in 37 countries; 116 further engines and related equipment were managed for third parties. Average portfolio utilisation was 84.9%, against 82.9% in 2024. <span class="cite">[WLFC 10-K FY2025]</span>

<div class="defn"><b>Maintenance rights</b><p>Maintenance rights is a line in Willis Lease's lease portfolio ($30.6M at 31 Dec 2025; $27.0M at 30 Sep 2025) that the research reports without a filing definition. From first principles, a lessor that buys an engine subject to an existing lease acquires, alongside the metal, the right to the maintenance reserves or end-of-lease compensation that the lessee has accrued but not yet paid over; that right is an asset separate from the engine and is carried at the value attributed to it. The reader should treat this as a working gloss and consult the 10-K's note for Willis's own definition. <span class="cite">[WLFC 10-K FY2025; WLFC Q3 2025 release; registry section 2]</span></p></div>

Two sets of Willis figures circulate in this primer and they sit at different dates. The Q3 2025 release gives utilisation of 86.0% at 30 September 2025, a lease portfolio of $2,888.5M including $2,700.4M of equipment, and a third quarter with $76.6M of lease rent and $76.1M of maintenance reserve revenue. The FY2025 10-K gives average utilisation of 84.9% for the year, equipment of $2,801.7M at 31 December 2025 and full-year lease rent of $291.6M. A point-in-time figure and a period average, and a quarter and a year, are different quantities; both are quoted with their dates and neither is wrong. <span class="cite">[WLFC Q3 2025 release and 10-Q; WLFC 10-K FY2025; reconciliation R-032]</span>

On demand, Willis's filing states: "Both Airbus and Boeing have delivery delays which have been compounded by engine manufacturer delays, shorter on-wing engine time of most new technology engines, and ongoing supply chain challenges." <span class="cite">[WLFC 10-K FY2025, via D9 §2.8]</span> That is a lessor's description of the drivers Part III §20 and §21 treat. The filing's CFM56 and LEAP portfolio split, its stated lease rate factor and its separately stated maintenance-services and spare-parts revenue lines for 2025 were searched for and not returned. <span class="cite">[D11 U1, U3; reconciliation K-14, K-15]</span> The Q2 2026 10-Q was not located; the Q1 and Q2 2026 earnings-call exhibits were located by URL only, and a 10-K/A for FY2025 was filed whose subject was not returned. <span class="cite">[D11 U2; WLFC 10-K/A FY2025]</span> Willis funds itself through the WEST series of engine securitisations (Willis Engine Structured Trust), nine to WEST VIII and ten to WEST IX; Part II §17 carries the structure and Part VIII §65 the servicing fees. <span class="cite">[KBRA via D6 §2.5]</span>

<div class="worked"><div class="h">Worked example 11.2 — Deriving a lessor's gross lease yield, and its lease rate factor, from filed figures: Willis, Air Lease and FTAI</div>
<p>Definitions. A lease rate factor (LRF; Part II §14) is monthly rent divided by the asset's value. Twelve times the LRF is the gross annual yield before depreciation, interest and maintenance cost. No filing in the research states an LRF, so each is derived from a rent line and a book-value line, and each derivation carries the mismatch between a flow for the year and a stock at one date (reconciliation R-030).</p>
<p>Willis Lease. Inputs: 2025 lease rent revenue $291.6M (sourced: WLFC 10-K FY2025); equipment held in operating lease portfolio $2,801.7M at 31 Dec 2025 (sourced); average utilisation 84.9% (sourced).</p>
<div class="eqblock">gross yield on year-end book  = 291.6 ÷ 2,801.7 = 10.4%
yield on the on-lease portion = 10.4% ÷ 0.849  = 12.3%
implied LRF                   = 12.3% ÷ 12     ≈ 1.0% per month</div>
<p>Limits: the denominator is the year-end book, not the average over the year; it includes 20 aircraft and a vessel as well as 363 engines; and the numerator excludes the $232.0M maintenance reserve line, which is cash the lessor collects but mostly spends on shop visits. The result approximates the filing's own LRF, which was not returned (D11 U1).</p>
<p>Air Lease. Inputs: 2025 lease rental revenue $2,615.4M (sourced: Air Lease 10-K FY2025); net book value of flight equipment subject to operating lease $29.1B at 31 Dec 2025 (sourced).</p>
<div class="eqblock">gross yield on year-end NBV = 2,615.4 ÷ 29,100 = 9.0%;  implied LRF = 9.0% ÷ 12 = 0.75% per month</div>
<p>New-technology aircraft on long leases carry lower rate factors than mid-life engines, which is the mechanism behind the gap between 9.0% and the Willis figure; Air Lease's own stated yield was not returned (D11 U10).</p>
<p>FTAI Aviation Leasing. The research derived two bounded figures before the FY2025 lease income was known: 10.4% on average cost and 13.4% on average net book value using first-half 2025 lease income annualised, or 6.6% and 8.8% using first-half 2026 income against the 31 Dec 2025 base (derived in the research, D6 §2.7). The FY2025 10-K's lease income of $235,210K (sourced, via the orchestrator notes) can now be put over D6's average 2025 bases of $2,510.5M at cost and $1,959.8M net (sourced: FTAI 10-K FY2025 balance sheets, averaged in D6).</p>
<div class="eqblock">on average cost: 235.2 ÷ 2,510.5 = 9.4%;   on average NBV: 235.2 ÷ 1,959.8 = 12.0%
on the on-lease portion at 77% utilisation (10-K definition): 9.4% ÷ 0.77 = 12.2% on cost
implied LRF on cost: 9.4% ÷ 12 = 0.78% per month;  on NBV: 12.0% ÷ 12 = 1.0% per month</div>
<p>Limits specific to FTAI: the 77% utilisation is by equity value and days on lease, excluding airframes, not by book value as at Willis (registry C-3; worked example 11.4); the cost base may still carry the 17 engines and 8 aircraft in Russia at zero net value (D6 U4); and the base fell through 2025 as Seed Assets were sold, so an average of two year-end balances is a rough centre. Reading across: Willis about 1.0% a month on the on-lease portion; Air Lease 0.75%; FTAI 0.78% on cost or 1.0% on net, and 0.87–1.1% on the research's earlier bases. None is a stated figure; all are shown with their bases and none is chosen (E.1 rule 6). <span class="cite">[WLFC 10-K FY2025; Air Lease 10-K FY2025; FTAI 10-K FY2025; D11 §2.2; D6 §2.7; reconciliation R-030]</span></p></div>

### 87.2 AerCap Holdings N.V. (NYSE: AER)

AerCap is the largest aircraft lessor by fleet and also a large engine lessor, directly and through Shannon Engine Support, a joint venture with Safran Aircraft Engines that leases CFM engines. It owns helicopters and runs AerCap Materials, its part-out and used-material arm. It is a foreign private issuer and so files an annual report on Form 20-F and interim results on Form 6-K rather than a 10-K and 10-Qs. <span class="cite">[AerCap 20-F FY2025; AerCap 6-K Q3 2025; D11 §3.2]</span>

<div class="defn"><b>Form 20-F / Form 6-K</b><p>Form 20-F is the annual report a foreign private issuer files with the SEC in place of a Form 10-K, and Form 6-K is the form on which it furnishes interim results and other reports in place of 10-Qs and 8-Ks. AerCap, incorporated in the Netherlands and listed on the NYSE, files on these forms. The content differs in layout and in some disclosure requirements from a domestic filer's, which is one reason AerCap's segment-level margins were not returned in the same shape as the other peers'. <span class="cite">[D11 §3.2, §8]</span></p></div>

<div class="defn"><b>Shannon Engine Support (SES)</b><p>Shannon Engine Support is AerCap's 50% engine-leasing joint venture with Safran Aircraft Engines, the Safran half of CFM, that leases CFM engines. It matters to the engine count: AerCap's "over 1,200 engines" at 30 Sep 2025 includes engines owned and managed by SES, while its "470 owned engines" at 31 Dec 2025 does not. AerCap recognised $155M of lease rental income from SES in the first nine months of 2025. It is also a model of OEM-partnered engine leasing, with the manufacturer as co-owner of the lessor. <span class="cite">[AerCap 6-K Q3 2025; AerCap 20-F FY2025; D11 §3.2, §6 T1]</span></p></div>

For 2025 AerCap reported total revenues and other income of $8,517M (2024: $7,997M), up 7%, and net income attributable to AerCap of $3.8B (2024: $2.1B); the drivers of the increase were not returned. Total lease revenue was $7,369M, of which basic lease rents were $6,679M and maintenance rents and other receipts $690M. <span class="cite">[AerCap 20-F FY2025; AerCap Q4 2025 release]</span> At 31 December 2025 the portfolio was 3,500 aircraft, engines and helicopters owned, managed or on order, and AerCap owned 470 engines, "9% of fleet". The owned passenger fleet averaged 7.3 years, split 5.4 years for new-technology and 15.2 years for current-technology aircraft, with 7.1 years of lease term remaining. By net book value the passenger and freighter fleet was 37% A320neo family, 20% 787, 8% A350, 8% 737NG and 7% A320ceo family; the five lines sum to 80% and the 737 MAX share was not among the lines returned. <span class="cite">[AerCap 20-F FY2025; D11 U8]</span> The filing also notes that when a lease is extended, the remaining rentals are recorded straight-line over the remaining original term plus the extension, which is how the extension behaviour Part III §22 describes reaches a lessor's revenue line. <span class="cite">[AerCap 20-F FY2025]</span>

The engine count carries a tension that this Part presents both ways. The Q3 2025 6-K states "over 1,200 engines (including engines owned and managed by its Shannon Engine Support JV)" at 30 September 2025. The FY2025 20-F states that AerCap "owned 470 engines (9% of fleet)" at 31 December 2025. On one reading the 1,200 figure is the scale of AerCap's engine platform, since SES engines are leased under AerCap's management; on the other the 470 figure is the only count on the same basis as Willis's 363 and FTAI's 243, which are owned engines. The bases differ (owned against owned plus managed plus joint venture) and the dates differ by a quarter. Both are recorded, neither is chosen, and any comparison with FTAI's owned engines in §89 uses 470 and says so. <span class="cite">[AerCap 6-K Q3 2025; AerCap 20-F FY2025; D11 §6 T1; reconciliation R-033; E.1 rule 35]</span> A second, smaller arithmetic point: 470 engines is 13.4% of the 3,500-unit portfolio, not 9%, so the "9% of fleet" is on a basis (perhaps value, perhaps a different unit count) that the retrieved text does not state; this is arithmetic in this Part on two retrieved figures and not a finding of the research. <span class="cite">[AerCap 20-F FY2025]</span>

The 20-F was searched for AerCap Materials revenue, an engine-leasing revenue split, a stated lease yield or net spread, and statements on mid-life aircraft values or extension rates; none was returned. <span class="cite">[D11 U6, U7]</span> The one hard statement on current-technology aircraft is the age split: AerCap's current-technology owned fleet averaged 15.2 years, the age band FTAI's Strategic Capital vehicles buy (Part VIII §60). <span class="cite">[AerCap 20-F FY2025]</span> The Q1 2026 interim report was located by URL with no figures returned. What the research holds on AerCap Materials comes from its factsheets rather than the filing: more than 300 aircraft dismantled and more than 15,000 unique parts in 2023; more than 250,000 items stocked and more than 850 customers at the first quarter of 2026. <span class="cite">[AerCap Materials factsheets, 2023 and 1Q 2026; D3 §3]</span>
