## 1. Summary

1. The CFM56 is the engine family on the Boeing 737NG (all of them) and on the majority of Airbus A320ceo-family aircraft (the rest carry the IAE V2500). Roughly 24,000 CFM56 engines were in service as of September 2025 (trade press); production of engines for new airframes ended in 2019 (CFM56-7B) and 2022 (CFM56-5B), so the installed base can only shrink. The pace of shrinkage is the central number.
2. Retirements have run far below the pattern the OEMs planned on: GE Aerospace's expectation for CFM56 retirements moved from 3-4% of the installed base to 2-3% and then to 1.5-2%; realised 2025 retirements were ~1.5%, broadly the same as 2024, and 2026 is expected at ~2% (GE Q4 2025 results, 22 Jan 2026).
3. CFM56 shop visits: GE and Safran entered 2026 projecting ~2,300 per year in 2026 and 2027 (GE's own range: 2,300-2,400). Earlier GE/Safran framing put the peak at ~2,500 in 2025-26. One independent research house dates the peak in *first* shop visits to 2027.
4. V2500 (the competing A320ceo engine): slightly more than 800 shop visits in 2025; 2026 expected within ±20 of that (RTX CFO, 2026).
5. New-generation engines are not yet relieving the old fleet. Cirium data cited in trade press put 835 PW1100G (Pratt & Whitney GTF)-powered aircraft grounded or stored in late 2025; RTX says grounded-aircraft counts are down more than 20% from the 2025 peak. CFM's LEAP-1B high-pressure-turbine durability kit was certified in July 2026 with full production cutover in early 2027; about one-third of the LEAP-1A fleet had received the equivalent upgrades.
6. Airframer output: 737 MAX deliveries were 387 (2023), 260 (2024), 440 (2025); the FAA capped production at 38/month after January 2024, lifted the cap to 42 in October 2025, and Boeing completed the review to go to 47 in May 2026. Airbus delivered 793 aircraft in 2025 against an original 820 target, targets ~870 in 2026, and now expects A320neo-family rate 75/month only by end-2027.
7. Lessors: 75-90% of scheduled lease expiries were being extended (Cirium Ascend); only ~12 mid-life 737NGs and fewer than 25 A320ceos were available to rent worldwide at the time of that survey; lease rates on 10-year-old 737-800s were 44% above January 2022 levels; extension terms have stretched to 4-6 years.
8. Engine MRO market size: Oliver Wyman $70B (2026) rising to $102.6B (2036). Aviation Week counts ~8,800 engine shop visits across all types in 2024 and ~9,000 in 2025, of which ~2,300 CFM56. Bain's model has shop-visit demand exceeding capacity by more than 17% by the end of the decade if capacity keeps its current trajectory.
9. Supply side (D2 owns this): CFM56 full-overhaul turnaround 90-120 days, light visit ~45 days, slot waits of 2-6 months, legacy-engine turnaround times up 35% versus five years earlier.
10. Unresolved: exact 2026 in-service counts and average ages for the A320ceo family and 737NG, the CFM56-5B/-7B engine split, a reliable V2500 engine count, and a year-by-year GTF grounded-aircraft series from RTX's own disclosures.

## 2. Mechanics

### 2.1 What "demand" means in this market, and the fleet states that generate it

**Aftermarket demand** for an engine type is the sum of the work and assets needed to keep that type flying: **shop visits** (an engine removed from the wing and sent to a repair shop to be opened, inspected and restored), **modules** (the four to five major sub-assemblies into which a CFM56 divides — fan, booster/low-pressure compressor, core (high-pressure compressor, combustor and high-pressure turbine) and low-pressure turbine — each of which can be swapped as a unit; D1 covers this), **spare engines** (engines held or leased to cover the gap while an installed engine is in the shop), **used serviceable material (USM)** (parts recovered from torn-down engines, inspected and recertified for reuse), and **leases** of whole engines or aircraft.

Demand is generated by the **installed base** (engines fitted to aircraft that are flying) multiplied by how hard they fly, net of engines that leave. The fleet states used by the data providers (Cirium, IBA) are:

- **In service**: assigned to an operator and flying, or in short-term maintenance.
- **Parked / stored**: not flying, but not formally withdrawn; an aircraft parked for more than about 90 days is usually counted as stored. Stored aircraft can return to service (they did, in volume, after 2021) or go on to retirement.
- **Retired / withdrawn**: formally taken out of the operating fleet. A retired aircraft is either scrapped whole, **parted out** (disassembled so its engines, landing gear, avionics and other components can be sold individually), or converted to another use, most commonly a **passenger-to-freighter (P2F) conversion**.
- **Teardown**: the disassembly of a retired engine (or airframe) into parts. Teardown is the source of USM. A retired aircraft therefore does two opposite things to the aftermarket at once: it removes a future customer for shop visits and it adds supply of used parts and green-time engines.

The **average age** of a fleet matters because maintenance need rises with age in steps (the first shop visit, the first life-limited-part replacement, the first heavy airframe check), and because the retirement decision is largely a function of age-related cost against residual value (2.3 below).

### 2.2 The installed base and why it only shrinks

CFM International (the 50/50 joint venture of GE Aerospace and Safran) describes the CFM56-5B (A320ceo family) as having powered more than 4,100 aircraft delivered over a nearly 30-year run, with production of installed engines ending in 2022, and the CFM56-7B (737NG) as having powered more than 7,000 aircraft delivered over nearly 25 years, with production of installed engines ending in 2019; CFM's product page also states that "in excess of 8,000 CFM56-7Bs are currently installed in 737NGs" (date of that statement not visible) [S76]. Trade press puts the total CFM56 fleet at approximately 24,000 engines in service as of September 2025, out of more than 35,000 delivered across the 737, A320 and military platforms [S78].

Because no new -5B or -7B engines are being built for airframes, the installed base falls by exactly the number of engines retired (or converted to non-aviation use — FTAI Power's Mod-1 generator sets, D8, are a new sink). Each engine that stays in service continues to accrue **flight cycles** (one take-off and landing) and **flight hours**, which are what trigger shop visits. So the retirement rate drives both sides of the market at once:

- Lower retirements → more engines accruing cycles → more shop visits and module demand → fewer teardown engines → less USM and fewer green-time engines → used-material prices and spare-engine lease rates rise.
- Higher retirements → the reverse.

**Worked example (illustrative arithmetic on sourced figures).** Take the ~24,000-engine installed base [S78] and the three retirement assumptions GE has used [S1, S3]:

| Assumption | Engines retired per year | Over three years (2024-26) |
|---|---|---|
| 3.5% (midpoint of the original 3-4%) | 24,000 × 0.035 = 840 | ~2,520 |
| 2.5% (midpoint of 2-3%) | 24,000 × 0.025 = 600 | ~1,800 |
| 1.75% (midpoint of 1.5-2%) | 24,000 × 0.0175 = 420 | ~1,260 |

The difference between the original planning assumption and the realised one is roughly 420 engines a year, or about 1,260 engines over three years, that (a) are still flying and will need maintenance and (b) are not available as teardown feedstock. The base shrinks slightly each year, so these are upper-bound figures, and the 24,000 figure may include stored and spare engines (Unknown U3).

A second piece of arithmetic: 2,300 CFM56 shop visits per year [S3, S7] on ~24,000 engines is about 9.6% of the installed base visiting a shop each year, which, if every engine were on a regular cycle, would imply an average interval of about ten years between shop visits. CFM56 shop-visit intervals are usually shorter than that (D1), which suggests either that a substantial share of the 24,000 are spares, stored, or on **green time** (an engine flown until its remaining life runs out, with no further shop visit planned — 2.7 below), or that the 24,000 figure is defined more broadly than "flying". This dossier records the implication and does not resolve it (Tension T1).

### 2.3 How a retirement decision is made

The owner of an aircraft (an airline or a lessor) retires it when the cost of keeping it in service exceeds what it is worth kept, and when the alternatives — selling it to another operator, converting it to a freighter, or parting it out — are better. The main inputs:

1. **Age and the maintenance cliff.** Costs come in large lumps: an engine **performance-restoration shop visit** (opening the core to restore exhaust-gas-temperature margin; D1), a **life-limited part (LLP)** replacement (rotating parts with a hard cycle limit beyond which they must be replaced regardless of condition, forcing the engine open), and a heavy airframe check. Acumen Aviation's December 2025 note on the 737-700 states that "as the 737-700 fleet ages, heavy checks and major engine shop visits increasingly exceed the residual value of the aircraft, driving earlier retirements or conversions" [S16]. A 2023 analysis found that once aircraft reach 16-18+ years they become vulnerable to retirement, particularly if the owner does not want to perform the next heavy check or shop visit, and that ~570 A320ceo-family aircraft stored for 90+ days at that time had an average age of 17.1 years [S14].
2. **Lease economics.** At the end of an operating lease the lessor chooses between extending with the current airline, transitioning the aircraft to a new lessee (which costs reconfiguration, maintenance and downtime), or selling/parting it out. The airline, for its part, chooses between extending and returning. Both choices depend on whether a replacement aircraft is available: in 2023-26 it usually was not (2.8).
3. **Freighter conversion.** A P2F conversion gives a 20-plus-year-old airframe a second life. Cargo Facts reports narrowbody freighters (737, 757) being converted at ages of 21-22 years on average [S18], with more than 260 737-800 freighters in service as of mid-2025 and a pipeline exceeding 300, against fewer than 50 737-700 conversions [S16, S17]. A conversion candidate still needs engines with life on them, so conversion is a source of demand for green-time engines or shop visits rather than a source of teardown supply.
4. **Part-out value.** When an aircraft's value "as a sum of parts" exceeds its value flying, the owner parts it out. For a mid-life CFM56-powered narrowbody the engines are the dominant share of that sum (D3 works the numbers). Rising USM prices raise part-out value and so, at the margin, encourage retirement — a stabilising feedback, but one that has not been strong enough to lift retirements in 2024-26.
5. **Replacement availability.** None of the above triggers a retirement if the airline cannot get a replacement aircraft. This is the mechanism through which the new-generation engine and airframer problems (2.5, 2.6) act on the old fleet.

### 2.4 Realised retirements, 2019-2025

- IBA (September 2024): aircraft retirements remained very low and were set to remain at fewer than 200 aircraft in 2024, similar to 2023, and 70% less than the 2020 peak; operators were holding onto aircraft by extending leases, and delivery delays led operators to maximise daily utilisation across all age groups, with mature passenger-narrowbody utilisation rising significantly [S60, S61].
- A separate figure, attributed in the search result to Cirium Fleets Analyzer data: 587 aircraft retirements in 2024, and a "Global Replacement Ratio" (deliveries needed to replace retirements, as a share of deliveries) of 46.1% for 2025 based on 2015-2024 data [S62]. The 587 and the "fewer than 200" figures are not reconcilable from the snippets (different scope — passenger jets only, year-to-date at the time of writing, or jets above a size threshold); Tension T2.
- Share of passenger aircraft retired relative to the active-plus-stored fleet: 2.2% in 2019, 0.4% in 2024 [S59/S49 — attribution in the result is to the Aviation Week / Bain material; exact document not visible].
- GE Aerospace on the CFM56 specifically: ~1.5% of the installed base retired in 2025, broadly consistent with 2024; ~2% expected in 2026, below the 2-3% previously anticipated [S1]. GE CFO Rahul Ghai, as quoted by Aviation Week: the 2026 planning assumption started at 3-4% retirements, was cut to 2-3% at the start of the year, and GE was "currently seeing something between 1.5% to 2%" [S3].
- Named retirement programmes under way: United, WestJet and Copa have started retiring 737NGs in favour of the MAX 8 [S16]; Wizz Air began a gradual phase-out of its A321ceo fleet (press release, 24 March 2026) [S15]. Counter-examples: Lauda Europe (Ryanair group) is extending A320 leases through the early 2030s because the transition to new aircraft proved too costly [S66]; Lufthansa and Ryanair were reported holding onto older aircraft to fill the gap [S63].

### 2.5 New-generation engines: durability and production, and what each does to the old fleet

The A320neo and 737 MAX were designed to replace the A320ceo and 737NG. Two things have slowed that replacement: the new engines spend less **time on wing** (the interval between installation and the first removal for shop maintenance) than planned, and the airframers have delivered fewer aircraft than planned. Both keep older aircraft flying longer and harder.

**Terms.** **LEAP** is CFM's new-generation engine: LEAP-1A on the A320neo family, LEAP-1B (sole engine) on the 737 MAX. **GTF** (geared turbofan) is Pratt & Whitney's PW1100G on the A320neo family (one of two engine options) and the PW1500G/PW1900G on the A220 and Embraer E2. **HPT** is the high-pressure turbine, the hottest rotating part of the core and the usual limiter of time on wing. **AOG** (aircraft on ground) is an aircraft unable to fly for lack of a serviceable part or engine. **Powder metal** refers to the manufacturing route for certain GTF turbine discs; a contamination defect in powder produced over a period of years required those discs to be inspected or replaced, which can only be done with the engine off wing and opened.

**Pratt & Whitney GTF powder-metal issue.**
- July-September 2023: RTX disclosed the defect, estimated that 600-700 engines would be pulled from aircraft for inspections, and took a ~$3bn charge (11 September 2023) [S26]; a later estimate put the total cost at about $7bn [S27]. RTX's planning figure at the time was that around 350 aircraft on average would be grounded across 2024-2026 [S25].
- Realised groundings exceeded that. Cirium data cited in trade press: 835 PW1100G-powered aircraft grounded or in storage globally as of late 2025 [S25]. The same source gives "720 out of 1,912 A320neo family aircraft — nearly four in ten — currently out of service due to engine issues"; the denominator is not defined in the snippet and is most plausibly the PW-powered A320neo sub-fleet rather than all A320neos (Unknown U5).
- RTX's own framing at FY2025 results (27 January 2026): 2026 is "the third year of elevated AOG levels"; AOG levels down more than 20% from their 2025 peak; PW1100 AOG down 25% year-to-date, enabled by a 40% increase in MRO output and a 23% reduction in turnaround time; Q4 2025 GTF MRO output up 39% year on year; customer-compensation cash outflows ~$1bn in 2025 and ~$700m expected in 2026, cumulative ~$2.8bn by end-2026 [S31]. Trade commentary expects the issue to be fully worked through only in 2027-28 [S25].
- Named effects: Turkish Airlines grounding more than 40 aircraft [S28]; Wizz Air signing a compensation deal [S29]. The fix for the fleet going forward is the **GTF Advantage**, an upgraded PW1100G certified by EASA in 2026 for entry into service later in 2026 [S30].
- Mechanism for the CFM56 aftermarket: every PW-powered A320neo on the ground needs its flying replaced. The practical replacement is an A320ceo (same pilot type rating, same cabin crew qualification, same maintenance organisation approvals), which is why the lease-extension and used-lease-rate figures in 2.8 are what they are. The A320ceo engines affected are the V2500 and the CFM56-5B, not the -7B.

**CFM LEAP durability.**
- The LEAP-1A and -1B have had shorter time on wing than the CFM56 they replaced, particularly in hot and harsh environments (the Middle East, India), driven by HPT blade and fuel-nozzle issues [S23, S24].
- The fix is a **Durability Improvement Package (DIP)** whose core is a new HPT stage-1 blade, a new HPT stage-1 nozzle and a forward inner nozzle support. On 18 July 2026 CFM announced FAA and EASA certification of the LEAP-1B HPT durability kit, stating it "will double time on wing in hot and harsh environments," that industrialisation was beginning, and that full production cutover is expected in early 2027 [S22]. The same release notes initial engine-level certification of the LEAP-1B **reverse bleed system (RBS)**, a cooling arrangement that reduces the need for on-wing fuel-nozzle replacements, "bringing on-wing maintenance needs in line with CFM56 standards" [S22].
- LEAP-1A: the new HPT blades and the RBS fix were already being deployed on A320neo-family aircraft, with approximately one-third of the LEAP-1A fleet updated at the time of the report [S24].
- Scale: about 5,500 LEAPs were in service at the time of the Aviation Week piece (date not visible), with ~50% covered by long-term service agreements versus ~15% of the CFM56 fleet [S7]. CFM has outlined investments to support growth in LEAP shop visits [S11], and Safran opened a new shop in Querétaro, Mexico, on 30 June 2026 [S12].
- Mechanism: a LEAP that comes off wing early needs a shop visit in a LEAP-capable shop (mostly OEM-network under long-term agreements) and a spare LEAP while it is away. It does not generate CFM56 shop visits. Its effect on the CFM56 aftermarket is indirect: airlines whose neo/MAX fleets are less available than planned slow their ceo/NG retirements (Willis Lease's FY2025 10-K lists "shorter on-wing engine time of most new technology engines" alongside airframer and engine-maker delays as compounding factors in the tight market [S69]).

### 2.6 Airframer output: Boeing and Airbus

**Boeing 737 MAX.**
- Production cap: after the Alaska Airlines door-plug event of January 2024 the FAA capped 737 production at 38 aircraft/month [S33]. The FAA raised the cap to 42/month in October 2025 [S33]. In May 2026 Boeing CEO Kelly Ortberg said the company had completed the "capstone" review with the FAA to move from 42 to 47/month, and that the 38-to-42 stabilisation had not taken long [S34]. Boeing's stated plan was a further step in September 2026 and another in late 2026, potentially reaching 52-53/month by year-end [S35, S36]; Ortberg said he remained cautious about the step to 52 because of inventory concerns [S34]. The 2018 peak was 52/month; the MAX backlog exceeds 4,800 aircraft [S34, S36].
- Deliveries: 737 MAX deliveries were 387 in 2023, 260 in 2024 and 440 in 2025 [S38]. Boeing's total deliveries (all types) were 348 in 2024; by August 2025 Boeing had delivered 385 aircraft, already more than all of 2024 [S39]. Cumulative MAX deliveries reached 2,399 by the date of the Wikipedia table (2026) [S38].
- Worked example: a full year at 38/month is 456 aircraft; at 42, 504; at 47, 564; at 52, 624. Against those, 260 deliveries in 2024 is about 196 short of a full year at the capped rate, and 440 in 2025 is roughly in line with the cap plus some deliveries from stored inventory (the source notes Boeing exceeded 38 in some months by delivering from storage [S38]). The difference between the 2018 rate and the capped rate — (52 − 38) × 12 = 168 aircraft a year — is a rough measure of 737NG replacement that did not happen in 2024-25 for production-cap reasons alone, before counting the 2019-20 grounding and the 2021-23 ramp.

**Airbus A320neo family.**
- Deliveries: Airbus originally targeted 820 deliveries in 2025 [S42], delivered 793 [S40], and targets around 870 in 2026 [S40, S45]. In August 2026 it delivered 57 aircraft, described as leaving "a steep climb" to the 2026 target [S44].
- **Gliders** (completed airframes parked awaiting engines): about 60 at the end of 1H 2025, 32 at the end of Q3 2025, with CEO Guillaume Faury saying the number would reach zero by year-end [S41, S43]. The engine shortfall came mainly from Pratt & Whitney (powder-metal production and inspection load) and, earlier in 2026, from CFM, which fell behind before catching up [S40]. Airbus was still negotiating P&W engine volumes for 2026 production in late 2025 [S84].
- Rate: Airbus now expects A320neo-family output to reach 70-75/month by the end of 2027 and stabilise at 75 thereafter, instead of earlier ambitions for that rate sooner [S40, S46].
- Backlog: a record 8,754 aircraft at end-December 2025, more than ten years of production at 2026 delivery rates [S45].
- Fleet context: the A320 family (ceo and neo combined) had 20,378 orders, 12,798 deliveries and 11,308 aircraft in service as of July 2026 [S13]. The ceo/neo split of the in-service figure is not given (Unknown U1).

### 2.7 Airline maintenance decision-making at the fleet level

An airline's engineering and fleet-planning functions run a rolling forecast of **engine removals** — each engine's projected date of removal for a shop visit, driven by whichever of its limits is reached first: remaining **EGT margin** (the gap between the engine's exhaust-gas temperature at take-off and its certified limit; it erodes with wear and when it reaches zero the engine must come off), remaining LLP cycles, or a hard inspection. From this forecast the airline **inducts** engines into shops, which in 2025-26 means booking slots 12-18 months ahead because slot waits run 2-6 months [S48, S57].

For each removal the airline chooses a **workscope** — how much of the engine to open and restore. The choice depends on how long the airline expects to keep the aircraft:

- If the aircraft will be retired or returned to its lessor within a few years, the airline tries to avoid a full performance restoration (which costs several million dollars; D1) and instead buys **green time**: it installs an engine or module with enough remaining EGT margin and LLP life to reach the retirement date, then returns or sells the engine at or near the end of its life. The green-time engine comes from the USM/teardown market or from an engine lessor. Trade press describes airlines "replacing LLP-expired modules with green-time units to maintain engines on wing while awaiting new aircraft or engine deliveries," through **quick-turn** or **hospital** shop visits that do only the essential work [S48, S56].
- If the aircraft will stay for a long time, the airline does the full restoration and replaces LLPs so the engine runs another full interval.

Bain's 2024 brief on the engine-maintenance capacity crunch notes the limit of the green-time strategy: at 80% fleet utilisation, green-time capacity becomes tight and "there is no huge buffer if airlines rely on green time and are getting to 80%" [S50]. In other words, green time is a finite pool fed by retirements; when retirements are low the pool shrinks exactly when demand for it is highest.

**Spare engines.** Airlines carry a ratio of spare engines to installed engines to cover removals; when shop turnaround lengthens, the same removal rate ties up more spares, and airlines either buy or lease more. Examples from FY2025 10-K filings: Spirit (Spirit Aviation Holdings) had 16 spare engines financed under operating leases and owned 18 spare engines at 31 December 2025 [S70]; American Airlines had agreements for 47 spare engines to be delivered in 2026 and beyond [S71].

**The extend-or-return decision.** When a lease expires the airline weighs (a) the extension rent the lessor asks, (b) the cost of meeting **return conditions** (contractual minimum remaining life on engines, LLPs and airframe checks at redelivery, which can force a shop visit just before return; D6), and (c) the availability and price of a replacement. In 2023-26, (c) has usually been decisive: with new aircraft late and used aircraft scarce, extending has been the only way to keep capacity, so airlines extend and perform the maintenance the extended term needs, rather than returning aircraft at end-of-life and leaving the shop visit to the next owner.

### 2.8 How lessor behaviour changed, 2023-2026

- **Extension share.** Rob Morris, global head of consultancy at Cirium Ascend, said 75-90% of scheduled lease expirations were being extended; Cirium Ascend estimated only about a dozen mid-life 737NGs and fewer than 25 A320ceos were available to rent anywhere in the world [S63] (Bloomberg reporting republished by AJOT/Gulf News; date not visible in the snippet, context is 2023-24).
- **Terms and rates.** IBA's research found lease lengths on legacy single-aisle aircraft being extended, with terms growing to 4-6 years and extension deals running out to 2026 and beyond; lease rates in some cases rose more than 40% above the age-specific rates negotiated just before the spike; in extreme cases aircraft due back at year 12 were not expected to return until 2032, at 18 years of age [S64, S65]. Lease rates on 10-year-old 737-800s were 44% above January 2022 levels [S63].
- **Lessor filings.** Air Lease Corp's FY2025 10-K: "As global air traffic expands and aircraft production volumes remain constrained, lessors are experiencing strong demand for aircraft through new lease requests and lease extension requests. The strong demand for aircraft, combined with elevated interest rates and inflation, helped increase lease rates on new lease agreements and lease extensions during 2025. Lease rates are expected to remain strong as the supply and demand environment for commercial aircraft remains tight" [S68]. Willis Lease Finance's FY2025 10-K: "Both Airbus and Boeing have delivery delays which have been compounded by engine manufacturer delays, shorter on-wing engine time of most new technology engines, and ongoing supply chain challenges" [S69].
- **FTAI's position in this chain.** FTAI's FY2025 10-K describes the 2025 Partnership (the Strategic Capital Initiative vehicle, D7) as focused on acquiring 737NG and A320ceo aircraft and as the primary buyer of all future on-lease narrowbody aircraft [S72]. The lessor behaviour above cuts two ways for a buyer of mid-life narrowbodies: high extension rates and rents support the value of aircraft already owned, and the same scarcity makes new acquisitions harder to source.
- **Worked example.** If 100 A320ceo leases expire in a year and 75-90 are extended, only 10-25 aircraft come back to lessors for transition, sale or part-out. Those 10-25 are the entire flow into the used market from that cohort. With fewer than 25 A320ceos available to rent worldwide at the time of Cirium's count, the stock of unplaced aircraft is of the same order as one year's returns from a 100-aircraft cohort — which is why the used lease rate moved 40%+.

### 2.9 The forecast curves

Definitions: a **shop-visit forecast** counts expected engine inductions per year for a type; a **first shop visit** is the first removal of a new engine; the **MRO market** is the dollar value of maintenance, repair and overhaul spending, of which **engine MRO** is the engine share.

- **GE/Safran (CFM56).** GE and Safran entered 2026 projecting about 2,300 CFM56 shop visits in each of 2026 and 2027 [S3, S51]; GE's stated range was approximately 2,300-2,400 [S3]. In earlier framing (Aviation Week; dates not visible, context 2024-25) CFM56-variant shop visits were expected to peak in 2025-26 at about 2,500 per year, and by 2030 "the CFM stable could be generating more than 4,000 shop visits annually, roughly split between LEAPs and previous-generation models," implying ~2,000 CFM56 visits in 2030 [S7, S8]. Safran's 2024 Capital Markets Day: LEAP shop visits to more than double by 2030 and peak above 5,000 a year by 2040; LEAP aftermarket revenue to reach parity with CFM56 by 2028 [S9]. Visual Approach Analytics (independent research) titles a note "CFM56 and V2500 engines to reach peak in first shop visits in 2027" [S10].
- **RTX (V2500).** Slightly more than 800 V2500 shop visits in 2025; 2026 expected approximately the same, "probably within 20" [S19]. Aviation Week's V2500 data tool projected more than 7,800 overhaul shop visits and 3,142 LLP-related shop visits over the following ten years (date of the tool not visible) [S20].
- **Aviation Week 2026 Fleet & MRO Forecast.** Approximately 8,800 engine shop visits (all types) in 2024, rising to ~9,000 in 2025, with the CFM56 accounting for ~2,300; unconditional MRO demand of $853bn over ten years; the overall aftermarket surpassing $1.6 trillion (cumulative over the forecast period, as read) at a 3.2% CAGR [S51].
- **Oliver Wyman.** Engine MRO market $70bn in 2026, growing to $102.6bn by 2036 [S47, S48]; drivers include retirements of skilled workers raising maintenance costs and a growing share of new-generation engines based on advanced materials from 2031 [S47].
- **Bain.** If engine MRO capacity keeps growing on its current trajectory, demand for shop visits at the end of the decade could exceed available supply by more than 17% [S49, S50].
- **IATA study (via AFM).** Engine shop visits "set to rise sevenfold by 2040" — the base is not stated in the snippet; given the Safran LEAP curve this most plausibly refers to new-generation engines, and the figure should be treated with caution until the study is read [S53].
- **Trade press on the CFM56 cycle.** Engine MRO demand is "likely to experience a near-term peak in 2026 and remain constrained through the end of the decade" [S52]; Acumen expects passenger MRO demand on the 737-700 "to decline sharply after 2027" [S16].

## 3. Market structure and players (the demand side)

Who generates and shapes demand for CFM56 and V2500 aftermarket work, and what each does:

**Engine OEMs**
- GE Aerospace (NYSE: GE) and Safran (EPA: SAF), 50/50 partners in CFM International. They set the retirement and shop-visit assumptions the rest of the market plans against, sell new parts, run OEM shop networks, and build LEAP, whose durability fixes determine when the CFM56 fleet can be let go [S1-S9, S22].
- RTX (NYSE: RTX), parent of Pratt & Whitney, which with MTU Aero Engines (ETR: MTX) and Japanese Aero Engines Corporation owns IAE, the V2500 consortium. RTX's GTF inspection programme is the single largest cause of A320neo groundings [S25-S31].

**Airframers**
- Boeing (NYSE: BA): 737 MAX production under FAA cap [S33-S39].
- Airbus (EPA: AIR): A320neo output engine-limited [S40-S46].

## 6. Constraints and bottlenecks (brief; D2 owns the supply side)

- **MRO slots and turnaround.** CFM56 full-overhaul turnaround of 90-120 days and ~45 days for light work; slot waits of two to six months; legacy-engine turnaround times up 35% and new-generation up more than 150% versus five years earlier [S48]. Engine stand utilisation for legacy engines rose by a third in 2025 over 2024, with CFM56-5A/B stand usage up 60% [S52]. Trade press expects engine MRO demand to peak in the near term in 2026 and remain constrained through the end of the decade [S52].
- **Parts and repairs.** Bottlenecks in test capacity and material supply; lengthy on-wing testing or repairs at third-party facilities with four-to-six-month waits; buyers advised to plan procurement early for LLPs and critical rotables and to use exchange pools, USM and diversified suppliers [S57, S58].
- **Labour.** Oliver Wyman cites retirements of skilled workers as a driver of rising maintenance cost [S47]. No headcount figures surfaced (Unknown U8).
- **Capacity response.** Narrowbody engine demand is driving MRO capacity additions (Aviation Week) [S54]; CFM's own investments target LEAP shop-visit growth [S11]; Safran's Querétaro shop opened June 2026 [S12]. Bain's >17% shortfall is conditional on capacity growing only at its current trajectory [S49].
- **Green-time supply.** Finite, fed by retirements, and tight at 80% fleet utilisation (Bain) [S50].
- **Capital.** Spare-engine and aircraft acquisition is capital-intensive; airline filings show spares financed through operating leases (Spirit: 16 of 34) [S70]. D6/D7 cover the capital layer.

## 7. Tensions

**T1 — Shop-visit count vs installed base.** ~2,300 CFM56 shop visits a year [S3] on ~24,000 engines in service [S78] is ~9.6% of the base a year, implying a ~10-year average interval, longer than typical CFM56 intervals (D1). Either the 24,000 includes spares and stored engines, or a large share of engines are on green time with no further visit planned, or both. Sources: GE/Safran via Aviation Week; Aviation Business News. Not resolved here.

**T2 — Total aircraft retirements in 2024.** IBA: "fewer than 200," similar to 2023, 70% below the 2020 peak [S60]. Cirium Fleets Analyzer, as cited in a forecast page: 587 [S62]. The scope (passenger jets only vs all commercial, year-to-date vs full year, size threshold) is not visible in either snippet.

**T3 — GE's retirement assumption, by year.** Leeham's reading of the Q4 2025 call: 2025 actual ~1.5%, 2026 expected ~2%, "below the 2-3% range previously anticipated" [S1]. Aviation Week's quotation of CFO Ghai: the 2026 guidance started at 3-4% "last year," was cut to 2-3% at the beginning of the year, and is now 1.5-2% [S3]. The direction is the same; the figures differ by half a point on the 2026 expectation (2% vs 1.5-2%) and on whether 3-4% was ever the 2026 number. Record both.

**T4 — CFM56 shop-visit peak level and timing.** Earlier GE/Safran framing: peak ~2,500 per year in 2025-26 [S7, S8]. 2026 framing: ~2,300-2,400 in 2026 and ~2,300 in 2027, i.e. a plateau rather than a peak [S3, S51]. Visual Approach: peak in *first* shop visits in 2027 [S10] (a different measure — first visits only — so not strictly contradictory, but a different shape). Acumen: 737-700 passenger MRO demand declines sharply after 2027 [S16].

**T5 — CFM56-7B installed count.** CFM's page: "in excess of 8,000 CFM56-7Bs currently installed in 737NGs" and ">7,000 aircraft delivered" [S76]. Two engines per aircraft on >7,000 delivered implies >14,000 engines built; 8,000 installed would mean ~43% already off-wing or retired, which does not fit a 1.5% retirement rate on a 24,000-engine total base [S78]. ## 9. Terms introduced

- **Aftermarket demand** — the shop visits, modules, spare engines, used material and leases needed to keep an engine type flying.
- **Shop visit** — removal of an engine from the wing and induction into a repair shop for inspection and restoration.
- **Module** — one of the major sub-assemblies (fan, booster, core, low-pressure turbine) of a CFM56 that can be removed and exchanged as a unit.
- **Spare engine** — an engine held or leased to replace an installed engine while it is in the shop.
- **Used serviceable material (USM)** — parts recovered from torn-down engines, inspected and recertified for reuse.
- **Installed base** — the population of engines fitted to aircraft in the operating fleet.
- **In service / parked (stored) / retired** — the three fleet states: flying; not flying but not withdrawn (stored usually means >90 days); formally withdrawn.
- **Part-out** — disassembly of a retired aircraft so its engines and components can be sold separately.
- **Teardown** — the disassembly of a retired engine into parts; the source of USM.
- **Passenger-to-freighter (P2F) conversion** — structural modification of a used passenger aircraft into a freighter.
- **Flight cycle / flight hour** — one take-off and landing / one hour of operation; the units in which engine life is consumed.
- **Average age** — mean years since delivery of the aircraft in a fleet.
- **Maintenance cliff** — the point at which the next heavy maintenance event costs more than the aircraft is worth.
- **Performance-restoration shop visit** — a shop visit that opens the core to restore EGT margin.
- **Life-limited part (LLP)** — a rotating part with a hard cycle limit beyond which it must be replaced regardless of condition; replacement forces the engine open.
- **Operating lease** — a lease under which the lessor retains ownership and the airline pays rent; at expiry the airline extends, or returns the aircraft to the lessor.
- **Return conditions** — the contractual minimum maintenance status (engine life, LLP cycles, airframe checks) an aircraft must meet at redelivery.
- **Lease transition** — moving an aircraft from one lessee to the next, with reconfiguration and maintenance in between.
- **Time on wing** — the interval between an engine's installation and its first removal for shop maintenance.
- **LEAP / GTF** — CFM's and Pratt & Whitney's new-generation engines for the 737 MAX and A320neo family.
- **High-pressure turbine (HPT)** — the hottest rotating section of the core; its blades and nozzles usually set time on wing.
- **Aircraft on ground (AOG)** — an aircraft unable to fly for lack of a serviceable part or engine.
- **Powder metal** — the manufacturing route for certain GTF turbine discs; a contamination defect required disc inspection or replacement off-wing.
- **Durability Improvement Package (DIP)** — CFM's set of LEAP hardware changes (HPT stage-1 blade, nozzle, nozzle support) to lengthen time on wing.
- **Reverse bleed system (RBS)** — a LEAP cooling arrangement that reduces on-wing fuel-nozzle replacements.
- **GTF Advantage** — Pratt & Whitney's upgraded PW1100G, certified 2026.
- **Production rate cap** — the FAA-imposed monthly ceiling on 737 output (38 → 42 → 47).
- **Glider** — a completed airframe parked awaiting engines.
- **Backlog** — firm orders not yet delivered.
- **Engine removal forecast / induction** — the airline's rolling schedule of expected engine removals and the act of sending an engine into a booked shop slot.
- **EGT margin** — the gap between an engine's take-off exhaust-gas temperature and its certified limit; it erodes with wear and sets the removal date.
- **Workscope** — the defined scope of work for a shop visit, from a light repair to a full overhaul.
- **Green time** — the remaining usable life on an engine or module that is flown out with no further shop visit planned.
- **Quick-turn / hospital shop visit** — a minimal shop visit doing only the essential work to return the engine to wing quickly.
- **Spare-engine ratio** — spares held per installed engine.
- **Long-term service agreement (LTA)** — an OEM contract (TrueChoice, EngineWise) covering an engine's maintenance for a fixed rate per flight hour.
- **Turnaround time (TAT)** — days from an engine's induction to its return.
- **Engine stand** — the transport and storage fixture an engine sits on when off wing; a capacity indicator.
- **Shop-visit forecast / first shop visit** — expected inductions per year for a type / the first removal of a new engine.
- **MRO market / engine MRO** — the dollar value of maintenance, repair and overhaul spending / its engine share.
- **Global Replacement Ratio** — retirements as a share of deliveries over a period.
